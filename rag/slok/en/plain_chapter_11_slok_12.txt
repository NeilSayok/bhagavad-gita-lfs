Life Application:
Imagine the brilliance of a thousand suns rising simultaneously in the sky, matching the radiance of the Supreme.

The light produced by the simultaneous rising of a thousand suns in the sky might resemble the splendour of that Supreme Soul (in His Cosmic Form).

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
After briefly describing the outline of the Cosmic Form to the blind King Dhritarashtra in haste, Sanjaya now describes the glory of that Cosmic Person. In His Cosmic Form, the Lord was shining with His own divine light, and His dazzling splendour was blinding the eyes. Perhaps, for this reason too, Sanjaya could not give more detailed information in the previous two verses. To describe this light of the Lord, Sanjaya gives a unique but powerful comparison here. The splendour of that glorious Cosmic Person was as if a thousand suns were shining simultaneously in the sky. In the Upanishads as well, the Self is described in a similar manner; however, it must be admitted that coming from Sanjaya's mouth, and especially when he is describing the divine form of Lord Krishna, this comparison acquires a special charm and dignity. Making the description even more beautiful by adding more details, Sanjaya says:

Commentary by Swami Adidevananda:
If a thousand suns were to rise at once in the sky, the resulting splendour may be like the splendour of that mighty One.

Commentary by Swami Gambirananda:
Should the effulgence of a thousand suns blaze forth simultaneously in the sky, that might be similar to the radiance of that exalted One.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
The word 'sahasra' (thousand) denotes 'infinite'. Even that is only for the purpose of making it understood (illustrated), like the expression 'the valour of Indra' (Pakashasana-vikrama) etc. For, it is said in the Rigveda Khilas: 'He has infinite power, supreme infinite strength, and He is of infinite splendour, even beyond that'. Thus, by virtue of this great purport, it has strength (validity); indeed, there is no purpose served by specifying a finite measure.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Objection: Does the supreme form of the Lord possess splendour or not? If not, it would be like wood etc. (dull). If it does, what is its nature? Raising this doubt, the commentator says: 'that indeed' (ya punar...). The syntax is: 'If that (splendour of a thousand suns) were to exist, it might be similar to His splendour'. The word 'yadi' (if) is used in the sense of assuming an impossible scenario. The word 'syat' (would be) is used to indicate certainty. To explain whether it could somehow be similar, or rather that (the Lord's splendour) indeed excels it, he says 'yadi va' (or if).

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
If thousands of suns were to rise at once in the sky, even all their light combined would hardly be equal to the splendour of that Great Soul (the Cosmic Form of the Lord).

Commentary: 'divi sūryasahasrasya ... tasya mahātmanaḥ' — Just as even if thousands of stars were to rise together in the sky, their combined light cannot be equal to the light of a single moon, and the combined light of thousands of moons cannot be equal to the light of a single sun; similarly, even if thousands of suns were to rise together in the sky, their combined light cannot be equal to the light of the Cosmic Lord. The meaning is that even the light of thousands of suns cannot be compared to the light of the Cosmic Lord. In this way, when the divine-sighted Sanjaya hesitates to compare the light of thousands of suns, how can that light be a proper comparison for the light of the Cosmic Lord! The reason is that the light of the sun is physical, whereas the light of the Cosmic Lord is divine. No matter how great the physical light may be, it is insignificant in front of the divine light. Since the nature of physical light and divine light are different, they cannot be compared with each other. Yes, just like pointing a finger, physical light can be used to indicate divine light. Here too, Sanjaya, by imagining the physical light of thousands of suns, indicates the light (splendour) of the Cosmic Lord.

Connection: After describing the divine form, limbs, and splendour of the Cosmic Lord in the preceding verses, Sanjaya now speaks about Arjuna beholding the Cosmic Form.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
By the phrase 'divi sūryasahasrasya' (a thousand suns in the sky), the Cosmic Form's splendour is described as resembling a thousand suns. To refute the notion that the word 'sahasra' literally means ten hundred (1000), the author says 'sahasra...' (the word sahasra). Even the splendour resembling infinite suns is not literal for the Lord, because He is incomparable (peerless). 'Then what is it?' In response, he says 'tad api' (that too), meaning it is stated only for illustration (to bring it within the grasp of the intellect), just as the valour of Lord Rama (the son of Dasharatha) is compared to that of Indra (Pākashāsana). 'Why discard the well-known literal meaning of the word sahasra?' To this, he says 'tathā hi' (for thus indeed). 'Even beyond that' (tatas tato api) refers to exceeding the suns etc.; 'distinct/transcendent' is to be supplied as understood. If it is asked how the Gitā's statement can be modified by the Shruti, the answer is: since 'hundred' and 'thousand' are names of the infinite, the Gitā statement has room for interpretation (and is thus weaker), whereas the Shruti has no other scope (and is thus stronger/authoritative). For this reason too the Shruti is stronger, as stated by 'mahātātparyāc ca' (and due to the great purport); this means the Shruti conforms to the great purport of all scriptures, which point to the Lord as the supreme goal, whereas a literal reading of the Gitā would run counter to it. If it is asked how the Gitā would run counter, we ask: is the Lord's splendour actually finite, or is He of infinite splendour and here described as resembling a thousand suns? The first option is invalid as it contradicts all Shrutis, Smritis, Itihāsas, and Purāṇas. In the second option, no purpose is served by stating a finite measurement for the infinite, as indicated by 'na ca'.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Thus, Sanjaya, after describing the vision of the Form, speaks of its supernatural character by presenting an unprecedented comparison (abhūtopamā): 'divi...' etc. In this way, by this unprecedented comparison, its transcendence (distinctness from the world) is declared.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
The commentator explains the word 'devam' (divine Form) mentioned earlier: 'divi...' etc. If in the sky (antariksha) the simultaneously arisen splendour of a thousand suns, i.e., an infinite group of suns, were to exist, then would it be equal to the splendour of that exalted One (the Cosmic Form)? Or if it might not be equal, then indeed the splendour of the Cosmic Form surely excels even that—this is my opinion. The meaning is that there is no other comparison possible. Here, by imagining a non-existent scenario, this figure of speech is an 'abhūtopamā' (unprecedented comparison) and 'atishayokti' (hyperbole) which suggests 'utprekshā' (poetic fancy), conveying its absolute incomparability, just like the verse: 'If both [Ganges and Yamuna] had separate flows in the sky...' etc.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
The commentator describes the incomparability of the splendour of the Cosmic Form: 'divi...' etc. If in the divine sky there were to be the simultaneously arisen splendour of a thousand suns rising together, then that splendour might somehow be similar to the splendour of that exalted One, the Cosmic Form. The meaning is that there is no other comparison. The connection is with the previous statement: '[He] showed such a form'.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Desiring an illustration for that splendour, Sanjaya says: 'divi...' etc. If in the sky or in heaven, there were to be the simultaneously arisen splendour of a thousand suns rising together, that might be similar (equal) to the splendour of that exalted One (the Cosmic Form), or it might not be (meaning that the splendour of the Cosmic Form exceeds even that)—this is the intent.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Showing the connection of the verse beginning with 'divi', the commentator says: 'tām eva' (that very splendour...). The meaning is that a specific attribute, namely the unsurpassable nature of the specified brilliance, is being stated. To refute the doubt that the splendour is limited by a specific number (like a thousand), he says 'tejasah...' (of the splendour).

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
The commentator describes the immeasurable nature of the splendour of that Form: 'divi...' etc. If in heaven the simultaneously arisen splendour of a thousand suns rising at the same time were to exist, that might be similar to the splendour of that exalted One, Purushottama, who possesses multiple forms. But it would not be similar at all; this is a rhetorical expression (kākukti). Such is His Form.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
'Divi' means in the sky (antariksha). 'Bhāḥ' means splendour (dipti), and 'bhāsaḥ' means of splendour. This is an unprecedented comparison (abhūtopamā). It shows the absolute incomparability of His splendour, just like the expression: 'If both [Ganges and Yamuna] had separate flows in the sky...' etc.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
If hundreds of thousands of suns were to rise at once into the sky, their radiance might resemble the effulgence of the Supreme Person in that universal form.

 What Arjuna saw was indescribable, yet Sañjaya is trying to give a mental picture of that great revelation to Dhṛtarāṣṭra. Neither Sañjaya nor Dhṛtarāṣṭra was present, but Sañjaya, by the grace of Vyāsa, could see whatever happened. Thus he now compares the situation, as far as it can be understood, to an imaginable phenomenon (i.e., thousands of suns).
