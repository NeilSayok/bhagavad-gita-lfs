Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
In this verse and the next four beautiful verses, Sri Krishna, following Hindu psychology, explains the cause of man's fall from his state of divinity. His sole purpose is to impress upon the mighty-armed Arjuna the importance of keeping every sense-organ under control; according to the scriptures, only one who has mastery over the senses is called a man of steady wisdom.

This passage also sketches the inner life-story of countless spiritual seekers who, after long years of practice, are dashed to pieces on the rocks of failure and despair. A true seeker on the path of Vedanta cannot ultimately fall, yet failures are common, and their sole cause is falling victim to sense-objects. It is also observed that once a person falls in this way, he rarely recovers - for him, the fall means total ruin. This is a very beautiful description of the staircase of self-destruction, given in such detail so that seekers like us may understand how the pure, divine nature within can once again be regained.

Just as a great tree grows from a tiny seed, the seed of our own destruction is unwholesome thought and false imagination. Thought has creative power - it can build us up or destroy us, depending on how that power is used or misused. When a person continually dwells on some sense-object, considering it beautiful and a source of happiness, attachment to that object is born. As this attachment intensifies, it takes the form of an intense desire or longing, without whose fulfilment the person cannot rest in peace. If any obstacle appears in the path of fulfilling that desire, the reaction to that obstacle is called anger.

The result of anger is that a person begins to see in things qualities that are not really there - this is called delusion (moha), meaning loss of discrimination. This delusion is itself the cause of the destruction of memory: in the grip of anger a person, forgetting all relationships, may behave however he pleases. Shri Shankaracharya notes that under the sway of anger, a man even forgets his debt of gratitude to his revered teachers and parents and insults them.

Thus, beginning with unwholesome thought, the fall of man proceeds through attachment, desire, anger, delusion, and destruction of memory - and the next step down is the destruction of the intellect (buddhi). The intellect possesses the discriminating power by which we judge right from wrong, righteousness from unrighteousness; even while performing forbidden acts, the intellect tries to restrain us. If this very intellect is destroyed, man's humanity itself must be considered destroyed. After the destruction of the intellect he behaves worse than an animal, and can never again recognise or attain life's highest and noblest goal. Here, 'destruction of man' means that, having failed to recognise his own pure nature, he becomes unfit to attain liberation, the supreme goal of human life.

Dwelling on sense-objects has thus been shown to be the root of all evil; the means to liberation is described next.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
[Madhavacharya] These two verses explain the cause of the faults beginning with attachment (raga), in order that they may be avoided. 'Sammoha' here means the desire for what is unrighteous (adharma) - as stated elsewhere, this same craving toward wrongdoing directed at sinful acts is termed 'moha,' and 'sammoha' likewise means desiring what is improper. 'Smrti-vibhrama' is the loss of memory regarding scriptural prohibitions and the like. 'Buddhi-nasha' is the complete destruction of the faculty that distinguishes right from wrong. 'Vinashyati' means he falls into hell and other such calamities. As it is said elsewhere: when a person desiring unrighteous acts loses awareness of scriptural teaching, through the resulting distorted vision and the sinful deeds it produces, he attains hell.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
[Anandagiri] This passage confirms through common experience that anger is the cause of delusion, illustrated by the fact that an angry man abuses others and rebukes them as unworthy. It then explains the effect of delusion, namely confusion of memory, and describes the nature of memory by pointing to the cause of its disturbance. An objection is raised: since memory is by nature momentary and perishes of itself, how can its loss be attributed to delusion? This is answered by showing that the destruction of memory here specifically means the failure of memory to arise on the proper occasion. A further question is raised as to how, if memory merely fails to arise, this constitutes an actual destruction of the intellect - this too is explained by showing it is the destruction of that intellect's capacity, as a real effect. Finally, since the Self (purusha) is eternally established, its 'destruction' through the destruction of intellect seems inadmissible; this is resolved by clarifying that 'destruction' here means that, in the absence of the inner faculty (antahkarana) capable of discriminating between what should and should not be done, the person - though the Self itself is never lost - is spoken of as 'destroyed' only in the sense of having become incapable of achieving the true end of human life.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
A person who dwells on sense-objects develops attachment to them; from attachment arises desire; from desire arises anger; when anger arises, delusion (a state of utter confusion) sets in; from delusion, memory is lost; when memory is lost, the intellect is destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, the person's downfall is complete.

When a person is not devoted to God and does not think of Him, he inevitably thinks only of worldly objects, since on one side of the soul stands God and on the other the world - abandoning reliance on God, he takes refuge in the world and dwells on nothing but it. Through such constant dwelling, attachment, fondness and love for those objects arise. This attachment leads him to indulge in those objects, whether mentally or physically, and the pleasure derived from that indulgence makes the objects dearer still, causing him to think of them again and again - so that whether or not he actually indulges further, attachment toward the object inevitably deepens; this is the rule.

From attachment is born desire (kama) - the longing to obtain those very objects for oneself. When desired things keep coming one's way, greed is born; and when something obstructs the fulfilment of that desire, anger arises. Desire is such that any obstruction to it invariably produces anger - even the pride one takes in one's caste, station, qualities or ability carries within it a desire for respect and honour, and if someone obstructs that, anger arises there too. Desire is a quality of rajas, delusion (sammoha) is a quality of tamas, and anger lies between the two.

Wherever anger arises over anything, some attachment lies at its root - anger at one who acts against justice reveals attachment to justice; anger at one who insults reveals attachment to honour; anger at a critic reveals attachment to praise; anger at one who finds fault reveals attachment to one's own pride of blamelessness, and so on.

'From anger arises delusion' - that is, a state of utter confusion overtakes the mind. In truth, delusion can arise from desire, anger, greed, and attachment (possessiveness) alike: delusion born of desire makes a person, his discrimination obscured, perform even improper acts under desire's sway; delusion born of anger makes a person speak wrongly even to friends and revered elders and behave improperly toward them; delusion born of greed destroys a person's sense of true and false, right and wrong, so that he deceives others through fraud; delusion born of attachment/possessiveness destroys equanimity and produces partiality. If delusion can arise from all four - desire, anger, greed and attachment - why does the Lord name only anger here? Because in desire, greed and attachment, the underlying impulse is still one's own pleasure and self-interest, whereas in anger the impulse is to harm another; hence the delusion born of anger is more terrible than that born of the other three, and for this reason the Lord singles out anger alone as the source of delusion here.

'From delusion, confusion of memory' - once overwhelming confusion sets in, memory of the resolve formed through scripture and right thinking (that 'I must do this, practise this, secure my own upliftment') is lost; it is simply forgotten.

'From confusion of memory, destruction of intellect' - once memory is lost, the discrimination that would normally manifest in the intellect disappears; the person loses the capacity to think anew or judge rightly.

'From destruction of intellect, he perishes' - with discrimination gone, the person falls from his (spiritual) state. Hence, to be saved from this downfall, every seeker has the greatest need to remain devoted to God.

Here the sequence described - from mere dwelling on objects comes attachment, from attachment desire, from desire anger, from anger delusion, from delusion loss of memory, from loss of memory destruction of intellect, and from destruction of intellect the fall - though it takes time to explain in words, in actual experience these states arise and destroy a person almost instantaneously, like an electric shock.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
[Jayatirtha] Anticipating the question of why this teaching on the chain beginning with dwelling on objects is introduced here, though seemingly unrelated to the immediate context, it is explained that this passage gives the cause of the faults of attachment and the rest, so that they may be avoided - since it was said that one should remain devoted and disciplined (yukta) with the mind fixed on the Lord as the supreme means of mastering the senses, and since avoiding attachment and aversion will also be stated as another means; this passage, by way of introduction, explains how that avoidance is possible. 'Sammoha' is explained (departing from the ordinary sense of 'fainting,' which does not fit here) as the desire for what is improper - specifically, desire directed toward unseen forms of unrighteousness and toward sinful acts that produce it; this specific craving is called 'moha,' with the prefix 'sam' intensifying it, and textual support for this is cited. The word 'smrti-vibhrama' is explained appropriately, since a general topic is not relevant here - 'vibhrama' means unsteadiness, i.e., destruction, of memory. To the question of how a conscious being (whose intellect might seem indestructible) can be said to suffer 'destruction of buddhi,' it is explained that this destruction is total precisely because it is a case of confusion/loss of memory itself. Since the Self is eternally established, the question arises how it can be said to perish; this is answered by clarifying the sense of 'perishing' here, with scriptural support cited: it is stated that when there is loss of awareness of scriptural teaching due to a mind fixed on wrongdoing, through the resulting distorted vision and the actions it generates, one attains hell and other calamities in succession - via attachment and aversion. The overall point: knowing that dwelling on sense-objects, through this chain, leads step by step to such grave misfortune, and desiring to avoid it, one should give up dwelling on objects, since that is recognised as its cause, and thereby attachment and aversion themselves fail to arise.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
[Vallabhacharya] Having described the fault that arises from failing to control the external senses, the fault arising from failure to control the mind - namely the loss of the intellect fixed in yoga - is now explained in four verses, which also reveal the nature of one of steady wisdom. Of these, the fault of failing to control the mind is described in two verses. 'Dhyayatah vishayan' means one who repeatedly thinks of, dwells upon, sense-objects; from this, attachment arises, and from attachment, longing for those objects. When that longing is obstructed, anger follows; from anger comes loss of discrimination; from that, confusion - a wavering - of the memory instilled by scripture and teacher; from that, destruction of the resolve-forming intellect; and from that he perishes - meaning he falls into utter forgetfulness and becomes a mere creature of his lower nature (prakrita), even though not wholly impure.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
[Madhusudana Sarasvati] An objection is raised: since the mind causes harm only by directing the external senses outward, if the external senses are firmly restrained, then even an uncontrolled mind - like a snake with its fangs pulled - can do no real damage, because without external engagement the goal is already achieved; hence the earlier injunction to 'remain devoted' would be pointless. In answer, these two verses show that even for one whose external senses are restrained, if the mind is left uncontrolled, the gravest ruin still follows. Even in one whose external senses are controlled, if he repeatedly dwells in the mind on sense-objects such as sound, a special fondness arises - the notion that these objects are supreme sources of pleasure for him; this is attachment. From attachment - the notion that these objects cause happiness - arises desire, a particular craving that these objects be his. When that desire is obstructed by something, anger, in the nature of a blazing hostility toward the obstructing object, arises. From anger comes delusion, the absence of discrimination between what should and should not be done. From delusion comes confusion of memory - a wavering, a deviation, of the memory that follows the meaning taught by scripture and teacher. From that failure of memory comes destruction of the intellect - the mental state directed toward the unity of the Self fails to arise, being obstructed by the accumulation of contrary impressions, or, if it does arise, dissolves as unfit to bear fruit. From destruction of intellect he perishes - through the loss of that fruit-bearing intellect, he becomes unfit for any of the true goals of human life; and one who is born yet unfit for the true goals of life is spoken of in the world as though already dead - hence it is said 'he perishes.' Since, therefore, even with the external senses controlled, an uncontrolled mind leads to the greatest calamity, one should restrain the mind with great effort; this is the intent behind the earlier injunction, 'let him remain devoted, having brought all these under control.'

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
[Dhanpati] It was earlier said that mastery of the senses must be achieved through effort, with the practice of not dwelling mentally on objects as the means; here the fault of the opposite is shown. For a person who dwells on sense-objects, fondness for them arises; from attachment comes longing; and when that desire is obstructed by something, anger arises. From anger comes confusion regarding what should and should not be done, since an angry, deluded person even rebukes his own teachers. From that follows deviation of the memory instilled by the teaching of scripture and teacher - when the occasion for memory to arise comes, it fails to do so. From that comes unfitness to discriminate right action from wrong, i.e., destruction of the intellect. From that he perishes - meaning that even while alive he is as good as dead, unfit for the true goals of human life. Thus dwelling on sense-objects is shown to be the seed of all misfortune - this is the point of both verses.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
[Purushottamji] 'And from anger, delusion (sammoha)' - here 'sam' indicates thoroughness, so 'moha' means the complete absence of discrimination. 'From delusion, confusion (vibhrama) of memory' refers specifically to confusion of the remembrance of the Lord (bhagavat-smarana) - a particular disturbance affecting the continuity of remembrance that follows upon remembering the Lord. 'From confusion of memory' comes the aforementioned 'destruction of intellect.' 'From destruction of intellect, he perishes' - meaning the complete absence, once again, of engagement in the practice of devotion; this is the culmination of that ruin. The implication is that one should proceed free from attachment born of dwelling on sense-objects.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
[Neelkantha] From that anger arises delusion, the absence of discrimination between what should and should not be done. From that arises confusion of memory - a wavering, a deviation in the pursuit of the meaning of scripture. From confusion of memory comes destruction of the intellect - even the meaning of scripture that had been firmly ascertained becomes obscured. And when that indirect knowledge born of scripture is also destroyed, the person perishes, becoming unfit for the true goal of human life; indeed, people in the world speak of such a person as though he were already destroyed.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
From anger, complete delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, intelligence is lost, and when intelligence is lost one falls down again into the material pool.

 Śrīla Rūpa Gosvāmī has given us this direction: prāpañcikatayā buddhyā hari-sambandhi-vastunaḥ mumukṣubhiḥ parityāgo vairāgyaṁ phalgu kathyate ( Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu 1.2.258) By development of Kṛṣṇa consciousness one can know that everything has its use in the service of the Lord. Those who are without knowledge of Kṛṣṇa consciousness artificially try to avoid material objects, and as a result, although they desire liberation from material bondage, they do not attain to the perfect stage of renunciation. Their so-called renunciation is called phalgu, or less important. On the other hand, a person in Kṛṣṇa consciousness knows how to use everything in the service of the Lord; therefore he does not become a victim of material consciousness. For example, for an impersonalist, the Lord, or the Absolute, being impersonal, cannot eat. Whereas an impersonalist tries to avoid good eatables, a devotee knows that Kṛṣṇa is the supreme enjoyer and that He eats all that is offered to Him in devotion. So, after offering good eatables to the Lord, the devotee takes the remnants, called prasādam. Thus everything becomes spiritualized, and there is no danger of a downfall. The devotee takes prasādam in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, whereas the nondevotee rejects it as material. The impersonalist, therefore, cannot enjoy life, due to his artificial renunciation; and for this reason, a slight agitation of the mind pulls him down again into the pool of material existence. It is said that such a soul, even though rising up to the point of liberation, falls down again due to his not having support in devotional service.
