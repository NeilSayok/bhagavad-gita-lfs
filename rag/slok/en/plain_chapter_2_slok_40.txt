Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Loss of the sequence: Just as, in farming, to reap a harvest one must follow a sequence -- ploughing the field, irrigating, sowing the seed, weeding, guarding and reaping -- and failing to do so brings loss, so too in the ritualistic sacrifices prescribed in the Vedas, if the sequence of procedure is not followed, the sacrifice does not yield its fruit. Not only that, if the actions enjoined by the Veda are not performed at all, that is called the fault of transgression, the evil result of which the doer (the individual soul) must suffer. The same is seen in the pursuit of worldly results. There are many examples of this kind in the material world too, as when a patient loses his very life through the wrong use of medicines. Because of these two faults present in the field of action, we are unable to obtain the desired result. Lord Sri Krishna here, as if advertising this knowledge, assures Arjuna that Karma Yoga is entirely free from and safe from both the above faults. Now He explains the nature of this knowledge.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Objection: since the performance of action does not invariably yield its fruit and is therefore ineffective, and since it is tainted with many evils and is thus faulty, even the disposition of Yoga (equanimity) is not to be relied upon. To this the Lord replies with 'kiñca' (moreover) -- something further is now said, namely, the reason why performance of action is indeed necessary. Since it is impossible to practise meditation together with action, on account of the many possible obstacles, and since its fruit -- direct realisation, attainable only through long practice -- cannot be accomplished in a single lifetime, one might apprehend that the Yogi would thereby fall away and come to grief. To dispel this the Lord says 'neha' (not here). The word 'na' (not), taken up first for emphasis, is repeated in construction as a restatement -- 'it does not exist.' As for the objection that action is ineffective because it does not invariably yield its fruit, this is refuted by the illustration: unlike agriculture or commerce, whose results are uncertain and depend merely on chance, the fruit of Vedic action, once begun, cannot properly be regarded as uncertain, since that would contradict scripture. As for the further objection that action is faulty because tainted with many evils, the Lord answers with 'kiñca' again -- having thus affirmed once more that action is necessary, He now brings out yet another reason with 'nāpi' (nor is there). In medical treatment, when it is undertaken, either an aggravation of the disease or even death may result as a contrary effect, because the outcome of treatment is uncertain; it is not so with the performance of enjoined action, since there is no fault in it, it being scripturally ordained. Now the fruit of performing action is asked about with 'kiṃtu' (but), and explaining the second half of the verse, the intended fruit is stated with 'svalpamapi' (even a little) -- the protection meant here is through the arising of true knowledge, as declared in the traditional text: 'Even one whose mind is engrossed in every sin becomes an ascetic, a purifier of the very row he sits in, by meditating on the Imperishable One even for the duration of a wink.'

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
In this human world, the beginning of this Dharma of equanimity is never destroyed, its practice never produces a contrary result, and even a little practice of it protects one from the great fear of birth and death.

Explanation -- The glory of this equanimity has been declared by the Lord in the latter half of the previous verse and in this fortieth verse in four ways: (1) through it one becomes free from the bondage of action, (2) its beginning is never destroyed, (3) it never yields a contrary result, and (4) even a little practice of it protects from the great fear.

'Nehābhikramanāśo'sti' -- Even if only the beginning of this equanimity is made, that very beginning is never lost. The longing and eagerness in the mind to attain equanimity is itself the beginning of this equanimity, and this beginning never ceases to exist, because the longing for what is real is itself real.

Here the word 'iha' (in this world) implies that only the human being in this human world is entitled to attain this equanimity; all other forms of life exist only for the enjoyment of pleasures. Hence in those forms there is no opportunity to destroy the unevenness of attachment and aversion, since enjoyment itself proceeds only through attachment and aversion; without them there would be no enjoyment, only endeavour.

'Pratyavāyo na vidyate' -- In actions performed with a desire for results, if there is any deficiency in the chanting of mantras or in the ritual procedure, a contrary result follows. For instance, if a man performs a sacrifice desiring a son and there is a flaw in the procedure, not only does the son fail to be born, but someone in the house may even die; or, short of such a reversal, the son may be born with imperfect limbs. But for one who strives to bring this equanimity into his conduct, his effort and practice never produce a contrary result, because there is no desire for a result in his action. As long as the desire for fruit remains, equanimity does not arise, and once equanimity arises, the desire for fruit no longer remains. Hence a contrary result from his action is simply impossible.

What is a contrary result? The arising of unevenness towards the world is itself the contrary result. Attachment to some undertakings and aversion to others is unevenness, and this very unevenness is the cause of the bondage of birth and death. But when equanimity arises in a person, attachment and aversion cease, and with their cessation unevenness ceases, so there remains no cause at all for a contrary result.

'Svalpamapyasya dharmasya trāyate mahato bhayāt' -- Even a little practice of this Dharma of equanimity, even a small measure of evenness brought into one's life and conduct, protects one from the great fear of birth and death. Just as action performed with desire is destroyed after yielding its fruit, this equanimity is not destroyed by yielding some fruit such as wealth -- that is, its fruit is not the attainment of perishable wealth and the like. Whatever degree of equanimity a seeker attains in his heart towards favourable and unfavourable objects, persons, events and circumstances, becomes permanent to that extent, and this equanimity can never be destroyed at any time. Just as the equanimity and the accumulated means of practice attained by one who has fallen from Yoga are never lost even after he enjoys pleasures for many years in the higher worlds and afterwards in the homes of the prosperous on earth (Gita 6.41-44), so this equanimity, this stock of practice, is never spent in the least but remains ever safe and unchanged, because it is real, ever-abiding.

Dharma here means two things: (1) performing acts of charity, such as giving alms, setting up water-stalls, opening food-houses, and other works of public good, and (2) diligently performing one's own duties as prescribed by scripture according to one's class and stage of life. When these duties are performed without desire for reward, the Dharma of equanimity arises of itself, for this Dharma of equanimity is one's own Dharma, that is, one's own true nature. It is with this in view that equanimity of mind is here called Dharma.

A special point about equanimity: People generally believe that worship and remembrance of God are of value only when the mind is absorbed in them, and that repeating the divine Name without absorption of mind is useless. But in the Gita's view, absorption of mind is not the highest thing; the highest thing in the Gita's view is equanimity. Whether the other marks of perfection appear or not, one in whom equanimity has arisen is declared perfect by the Gita; one in whom all other marks appear but equanimity does not arise is not declared perfect.

Equanimity is of two kinds -- equanimity of the inner faculties and equanimity of one's essential nature. The all-pervading God, equally present everywhere, is one's essential nature; one who is established in that all-equal God has conquered the entire world and become liberated while living. But this is recognised through the equanimity of the inner faculties (Gita 5.19). Equanimity of the inner faculties means remaining even-minded in success and failure (Gita 2.48) -- whether one is praised or blamed, whether an undertaking succeeds or fails, whether millions of rupees come or millions go, there is no disturbance within, no joy or sorrow, no elation or grief (Gita 5.20). This equanimity is never destroyed. Apart from the ultimate good, this equanimity yields no other result whatsoever.

Whatever meritorious act a person performs -- austerity, charity, pilgrimage, vows -- it is destroyed after yielding its fruit; but if even a little equanimity, freedom from agitation, arises in the heart while practising, it is never destroyed, and instead it brings about one's ultimate good. Therefore equanimity is a far higher thing in practice than mere concentration of mind. By concentration one may attain occult powers, but not the ultimate good; by the coming of equanimity, however, a person is happily freed from the bondage of the world (Gita 5.3).

Connection -- In verse thirty-nine the Lord spoke of hearing about this equanimity in Yoga; in the following verse He now describes the means of attaining that same equanimity.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Here, in Karma Yoga, there is no loss of 'Abhikrama' or of effort that has been put in; 'loss' means the loss of efficacy to bring about the fruits. In Karma Yoga if work is begun and left unfinished, and the continuity is broken in the middle, it does not remain fruitless, as in the case of works undertaken for their fruits. No evil result is acired if the continuity of work is broken. Even a little of this Dharma known as Karma Yoga or Niskama Karma (unselfish action without desire for any reward) gives protection from the great fear, i.e., the fear of transmigratory existence.

The same purport is explained later thus:  'Neither in this world nor the next, O Arjuna, there is annihilation for him' (6.40). But in works, Vedic and secular, when there is interruption in the middle, not only do they not yield fruits, but also there is accrual of evil.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
The Lord speaks of the ease attainable through Yoga in every respect with the words 'nehābhikramanāśaḥ.' Here, in this disposition of Yoga, there is no destruction of the abhikrama, the commencement, of Dharma -- as declared in the words of the Bhagavata: 'Even a slight beginning of one's own Dharma once undertaken, even out of a mere limb of it, does not perish, for it has been resolved upon by Me' (Bhagavata 11.29.20). Even a little commencement of this Dharma saves one from great fear. In the Sankhya system, however, renunciation of Dharma and action is prescribed only upon the attainment of perfection; here it is not so, for it has been said: 'The observances such as yama are indeed to be practised, whereas fulfilment of purpose comes only when Yoga is perfected.'

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Objection: Since the scripture 'Brahmanas seek to know That through the study of the Veda, through sacrifice, through charity, through austerity that consists in fasting' enjoins all actions with a view to the desire to know and to knowledge, on the principle that connection implies distinct injunction, and since the purification of the inner faculties is achieved through action as its means, the performance of action is prescribed with reference to Me. Here, since the scripture teaches that 'just as a world won by action is exhausted here, so too a world won by merit is exhausted there,' the loss of fruit is possible; and since sacrifice and the like, performed with a view to the desire to know and to knowledge, are of the nature of desire-prompted acts requiring the complete accomplishment of all their component parts, any deficiency even in a single component entails a defect, and since it is impossible for a single human lifespan to suffice for the performance of all the actions enjoined by texts beginning with 'yajñena,' one might apprehend, 'How then will you be freed from the bondage of action?' -- expecting such a result. To this the Lord replies: that which is undertaken through an action, the fruit which is begun (abhikramyate) is called abhikrama; its destruction, illustrated by 'just as here...,' does not exist in this Yoga of desireless action, because this fruit, being of the nature of purification, that is, of the destruction of sin, and not being an object of enjoyment describable by the word 'world' (loka), admits of no such exhaustion...

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Objection: Since, as in farming and similar undertakings, actions may sometimes fail to yield their fruit owing to an abundance of obstacles, and since a defect in mantra or other components may bring about a contrary result, how then can Karma Yoga remove the bondage of action? To this the Lord replies with 'neha' (not here). In this desireless Karma Yoga, there is no destruction, that is, no fruitlessness, of the commencement, and no contrary result exists, because, the action being undertaken for God's sake alone, no defect of obstacle or deficiency of component can arise. Moreover, even the mere commencement, however small, of this Dharma, when performed, protects, that is, saves one from the great fear of worldly bondage; unlike desire-prompted action, this Dharma never becomes fruitless through any deficiency of its component parts -- such is the meaning.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
With the thought of showing how greatly this differs from desire-prompted actions, the Lord says 'neha' (not here). In this desireless action, that is, in the path of liberation through the Yoga of concentration, there is no destruction of the commencement. Unlike in farming and the like, there is also no contrary result, that is, no arising of sin, as there can be in medical treatment. Even a little practice of this Dharma protects, that is, saves one from the great fear characterised by the worldly bondage of birth, death and so forth.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Objection: After saying 'hear this' (2.39), the topic 'vyavasāyātmikā' (2.41) ought to follow directly; the intervening words 'nehābhikrama' do not fit in between. To this the Lord replies, indicating that these words serve as a preliminary recommendation, arousing greater eagerness to know by declaring the greatness of the subject at its very outset. The word 'iha' (here) indicates the distinctiveness of this Karma Yoga from other forms of action. Dispelling any doubt that abhikrama might mean literally 'stepping forward,' it is explained that abhikrama here means simply the commencement. As to how the commencement, being of the nature of an action, could be imperishable, it is explained that 'nāśa' here means the destruction of its efficacy as a means to a result -- that is, of a commencement once begun. Then, showing the ground for apprehending a contrary result, the second line is explained: the fear that discontinuance of what has begun would entail such a result, is answered. The latter half of the verse, expounding this, is then explained through the word 'asya' (of this). 'Fear of worldly existence' -- 'great' here specifically qualifies the fear as none other than the fear of transmigratory existence itself. That even a fragment of this practice can be a cause of freedom from worldly existence, even though interrupted by deficiency of time, place or other conditions, or through carelessness or omission, is shown by pointing out that reunion with such practice inevitably follows -- thus establishing that this verse is wholly devoted to the meaning already stated, in summary as well as in elaborated form, and to the imperishability yet to be declared...

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Objection: Since actions are numerous and dependent on time and other conditions, their performance can never be complete, and hence deficiency, and even the possibility of a contrary result through defect of some component, may arise -- how then will bondage not occur? Anticipating this doubt, and since Arjuna does not know the true nature of Yoga as union with the Lord who is of the form of Krishna, the Lord, in order to impart that knowledge, declares its nature with the words 'nehābhikramanāśaḥ.' On the path of devotion to the Lord, actions are to be performed for the Lord's sake, as His command, and not as means to a result; hence none of the previously mentioned faults can arise here. The Lord explains: here, of an action performed as My command, there is no abhikramanāśa, no loss of what has been begun, that is, it does not become fruitless. Nor does a contrary result exist. For even a little of this Dharma, when performed, protects, that is, saves one from the great fear. The meaning is this: elsewhere, remembrance of the Lord is required to render performed actions fruitful and complete, as in 'by whose mere remembrance...' (Vishnu Purana); how then could actions performed directly for the Lord's own sake ever become fruitless?

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
The Lord now establishes this very point with 'neha' (not here). Here, in Karma Yoga undertaken for the removal of the bondage of action -- abhikrama meaning that which is pervaded, that is, the commencement of action, or action itself -- its destruction does not exist. Other action perishes after yielding its fruit, but this does not, since it does not produce a desired fruit at all. Objection: since even this action, being included among desire-prompted acts, would still incur the contrary result arising from the non-performance of an obligatory duty -- for a single performance cannot serve both purposes, the removal of bondage and the avoidance of a contrary result. To dispel this, the Lord adds 'pratyavāyo na vidyate' (nor does transgression exist). By the principle that connection implies distinct injunction -- as when 'nitya' (obligatory) curd, prescribed in one context, serves in another as a substance for one desiring a particular result, so too obligatory actions themselves, by the force of their applicability, come to serve also as means to the desire to know, as taught by the scripture 'Brahmanas seek to know That through the study of the Veda, through sacrifice, through charity, through austerity that consists in fasting.' Hence, since the obligatory duty is thereby accomplished even through its performance with a desire-prompted purpose, no transgression arises either from the non-performance of the obligatory duty or from the incompleteness of a desire-prompted rite for want of all its components, since only what is obligatory is here truly enjoined, and partial observance according to one's ability is permitted even in obligatory acts...

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
In this endeavor there is no loss or diminution, and a little advancement on this path can protect one from the most dangerous type of fear.

 Activity in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, or acting for the benefit of Kṛṣṇa without expectation of sense gratification, is the highest transcendental quality of work. Even a small beginning of such activity finds no impediment, nor can that small beginning be lost at any stage. Any work begun on the material plane has to be completed, otherwise the whole attempt becomes a failure. But any work begun in Kṛṣṇa consciousness has a permanent effect, even though not finished. The performer of such work is therefore not at a loss even if his work in Kṛṣṇa consciousness is incomplete. One percent done in Kṛṣṇa consciousness bears permanent results, so that the next beginning is from the point of two percent, whereas in material activity without a hundred percent success there is no profit. Ajāmila performed his duty in some percentage of Kṛṣṇa consciousness, but the result he enjoyed at the end was a hundred percent, by the grace of the Lord. There is a nice verse in this connection in Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam (1.5.17) : tyaktvā sva-dharmaṁ caraṇāmbujaṁ harer bhajann apakvo ’tha patet tato yadi yatra kva vābhadram abhūd amuṣya kiṁ ko vārtha āpto ’bhajatāṁ sva-dharmataḥ “If someone gives up his occupational duties and works in Kṛṣṇa consciousness and then falls down on account of not completing his work, what loss is there on his part? And what can one gain if one performs his material activities perfectly?” Or, as the Christians say, “What profiteth a man if he gain the whole world yet suffer the loss of his eternal soul?” Material activities and their results end with the body. But work in Kṛṣṇa consciousness carries a person again to Kṛṣṇa consciousness, even after the loss of the body. At least one is sure to have a chance in the next life of being born again as a human being, either in the family of a great cultured brāhmaṇa or in a rich aristocratic family that will give one a further chance for elevation. That is the unique quality of work done in Kṛṣṇa consciousness.
