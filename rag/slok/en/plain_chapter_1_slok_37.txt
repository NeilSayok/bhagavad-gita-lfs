Speaker: Arjuna

Life Application:
Seek peaceful resolution over conflict whenever possible, realizing that victory built on destruction brings no joy.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
It appears that Arjuna's arguments are scripturally sound. Great harm comes to the science of philosophy because of people who, knowingly or unknowingly, interpret the scriptures in a contrary sense. Taking his own arguments to be correct and feeling satisfied with them, Arjuna arrives at the dangerous conclusion that he ought not to kill these aggressors -- yet the Lord still remains silent. Disturbed all the more by Krishna's silence, he now pleads with Him in a pitiable tone, seeking confirmation of his foolish decision. Having remained close companions for so long, an affectionate bond had grown between the two, and it is for this reason that Arjuna addresses Lord Krishna by the name Madhava and asks how a person could ever be happy after killing his own kinsmen. The Lord, even now, remains silent.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
One might ask: how then does it happen that others engage in the destruction of their own family and violence against their own kinsmen? To this he answers, in 'Yadyapi...': since their intellect is corrupted by greed, they lack any perception of the fault arising from the destruction of the family and the like -- hence such reckless engagement becomes possible for them, is the sense.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Therefore we are not worthy to kill these our own kinsmen, the sons of Dhritarashtra; for how, O Madhava, could we be happy by killing our own people?

Explanation: 'Tasman narha vayam hantum dhartarashtran svabandhavan' -- given all the reasons and arguments I have offered so far (from 1.28 up to here) for not killing our kinsmen, how could we possibly engage in such a calamitous act? The killing of these our own kinsmen, the relatives of Dhritarashtra, is wholly unworthy of us, wholly improper. How could people as good as us ever commit such an improper act?

'Svajanam hi katham hatva sukhinah syama madhava' -- O Madhava! Even the mere apprehension of these kinsmen's death is causing us such great sorrow and anguish; then, were we to actually kill them, overpowered by anger and greed, how much greater would that sorrow be! How could we ever be happy after killing them?

Here, due to the delusion born of attachment -- the thought 'these are my close relatives' -- Arjuna's vision is not even turning toward his proper duty as a Kshatriya. For wherever there is delusion, a person's discernment becomes suppressed. When discernment is suppressed, delusion grows stronger. And when delusion grows stronger, one loses clear awareness of one's own duty.

Transition: A doubt now arises here -- just as Duryodhana and the rest are your own kinsmen, so too are you their own kinsman. Yet from the standpoint of kinship you are contemplating withdrawing from battle, while Duryodhana and the rest are not even considering such withdrawal -- what is the reason for this? Arjuna answers this in the next two verses.

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
Nihatya etc. upto anususruma. Sin alone is the agent in the act of slaying these desperadoes. Therefore here the idea is this : These ememies of ours have been slain, i.e., have been take possession of, by sin. Sin would come to us also after slaying them. Sin in this context is the disregard, on account of greed etc., to the injurious conseences like the ruination of the family and the like. That is why Arjuna makes a specific mention of the [ruin of the] family etc., and of its duties in the passage 'How by slaying my own kinsmen etc'.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
One might ask: how then does it happen that others engage in the destruction of their own family and violence against their own kinsmen? To this he answers: since their intellect is corrupted by greed, they lack any recognition of the fault caused by the destruction of the family and the like -- hence such engagement becomes possible for them, is the sense. And it is thereby refuted that, since even the learned like Bhishma engage in the killing of kinsmen, and since such conduct of the learned is rooted in the Veda, it would therefore be proper for others too to engage in it -- by the maxim 'since a reason for it is seen [elsewhere, the two need not be equated].' For it has been established that, where a cause like greed is visible, one need not presume any Vedic basis for the action. The connection to the next verse is: even though these Duryodhana and the rest do not perceive the fault, how is it that we, who do perceive it, should fail to recognize it?

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
One might object: since the fault of killing kinsmen applies equally to them as well, just as they, accepting the fault of killing kinsmen, engage in battle, so too should you engage -- why this despondency? To this he answers, across two verses, in 'Yadyapi...': those whose minds are corrupted by greed for kingdom, whose discernment is ruined -- these Duryodhana and the rest -- even though they do not perceive the fault, how is it that we, who do perceive the fault, should fail to know that we ought to turn away from this sin? Our resolve ought indeed to be for turning away, is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Having thus established the fruitlessness of war and its being the cause of harm, he concludes, in 'Tasmat...'. Hinting that, being the Lord of divine wisdom, You ought to engage me in the means to attain that, and not in this act which causes only affliction, he addresses Him as 'Madhava.' Since sovereignty, the husband of Lakshmi, whose happiness depends on the happiness of one's own kinsmen, cannot be a source of happiness once those very kinsmen are destroyed -- how could we be happy after killing our own kinsmen? By this, Arjuna hints, in addressing Him as 'Madhava,' that even Your own lordship over Lakshmi exists for the sake of Your kinsmen. Some say the sense is: since You are the Lord of Lakshmi, You ought not to engage me in an inauspicious act.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Since we belong to You, we are unworthy of killing them -- he says this, in 'Tasmat...'. Hence we are not worthy, not fit, to kill our own kinsmen, the sons of Dhritarashtra, is the sense. O Madhava, having killed our own kinsmen, how could we become happy -- this is the sense. By the word 'we,' the Lord's own being included among us is expressed; hence it is conveyed that Your company alone is our happiness, that You alone are our true kinsman. It is thereby hinted: destruction of one's own kinsman would follow from an offense against one's own kinsman, and since You alone are our kinsman, in the absence of relationship with You, how could we ever be happy? By the address 'Madhava,' it is conveyed that we have no expectation whatsoever of Lakshmi or the like.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Aggressors: 'the incendiary, the poisoner, the one who attacks with weapons, the plunderer of wealth, the seizer of land, and the abductor of another's wife -- these six are aggressors.' 'One may slay an approaching aggressor without hesitation; no fault attaches to the slayer of an aggressor.' Even though this is so, still, by killing these, sin alone would attach to us. For the killing of an aggressor is enjoined by political science (artha-shastra), whereas 'one should not injure any being' is sacred law (dharma-shastra), and that is stronger than the former. As Yajnavalkya states: 'when the smritis conflict, reasoning grounded in worldly practice prevails; and dharma-shastra prevails over artha-shastra.' An alternative construction: 'having killed us, sin alone would attach to these aggressors' -- meaning: let these very ones perish through killing us, but let us not perish through killing them, is the sense.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
O Janardana, although these men, overtaken by greed, see no fault in killing one's family or quarreling with friends, why should we, who can see the crime in destroying a family, engage in these acts of sin?

 A kshatriya is not supposed to refuse to battle or gamble when he is so invited by some rival party. Under such an obligation, Arjuna could not refuse to fight, because he had been challenged by the party of Duryodhana. In this connection, Arjuna considered that the other party might be blind to the effects of such a challenge. Arjuna, however, could see the evil consequences and could not accept the challenge. Obligation is actually binding when the effect is good, but when the effect is otherwise, then no one can be bound. Considering all these pros and cons, Arjuna decided not to fight.
