Life Application:
Guard your mind against deceptive promises that lure you away from deep self-discipline and quiet focus.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Maharshi Vyasa was the first bold revolutionary who, in his own time, revived Hindu culture from a most lamentable state of decline. The Gita, which he composed, is that scripture of revolution. His sharp critique of the conditions of his age is evident in these words of the Lord, where Sri Krishna calls the ritualistic portion of the Vedas 'flowery speech.' To appreciate the courage Vyasa showed in sharply criticizing ritualism, we must imagine the conservative, tradition-bound atmosphere of that era and mentally place ourselves in that time.

The ritualistic portion of the Vedas is meant for those who are deeply attached to sense-enjoyment and to worldly power and prosperity -- those whose capacity for right discrimination has been lost through desire for, and anxiety over, the fruits of action. Forgetting the supreme goal, they remain engrossed only in the means, that is, in ritual actions.

Vedic rites have to be performed with great effort, and only after death do they yield their fruit in the form of heaven, where celestial objects of enjoyment can be experienced. In all these endeavours, because of desires and anxieties, there is no opportunity for the growth of one's true personality; hence Vyasa holds that, from the spiritual standpoint, these desire-driven actions are futile. The person attached to ritualism, forgetting the supreme goal of life, remains entangled only in the means.

As the exponent of the infinite Supreme Truth, Sri Krishna gently mocks those who mistake the means for the end. Through the mere performance of prescribed duty as taught in the ritual portion, purity and one-pointedness of mind are attained; and having thus gained fitness for the practice of meditation, one attains, through the nididhyasana described in the Upanishads, the direct realisation of the Self -- which is the true goal of life. Those engrossed only in action never find peace in life.

What result do such unwise, desire-ridden people obtain? The Lord now explains.

Commentary by Swami Adidevananda:
O! Partha, the unwise, who rejoice in the letter of the Vedas, say, 'There is nothing else.' They are full only of wordly desires and they hanker for heaven. They speak flowery words which offer rirth as the fruit of work. They look upon the Vedas as consisting entirely of varied rites for the attainment of pleasure and power. Those who cling so to pleasure and power are attracted by that speech (offering heavenly rewards) and are unable to develop the resolute will of a concentrated mind.

Commentary by Swami Gambirananda:
One-pointed conviction does not become established in the minds of those who delight in enjoyment and affluence, and whose intellects are carried away by that (speech).

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
Their intellect, characterized by correct and well-reasoned determination, is not established for the sake of concentration (samadhi). For those whose object has been rightly ascertained, the fixing of the mind on the Lord occurs properly; and that indeed is the means to liberation. This has been stated elsewhere too: 'Even the most excellent words alone cannot bring about direct grasp of the Truth for one who has not, of his own accord, inferred through reasoning -- as in a dream -- the desirability of renouncing the happiness of household life.' (Bhagavatam 5.11.3)

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
One might object: even for those devoted to ritual action, through the purification of the intellect there can arise, within the mind, a combination of the twofold understanding of goal and means, and hence liberation could still result. To refute this, the Lord says 'tesham' (of them). This refers to those who are outward-turned, identified with, and firmly fixed in mind upon, enjoyment and lordship -- regarding them as objects to be pursued as one's own duty. One might further suppose that, nevertheless, a determinate intellect in accordance with scriptural discrimination could still arise in them; to remove this doubt, the word 'taya' (by that) is used. Again, it may be asked: since samadhi is said to be of two kinds -- with cognition (samprajnata) and without cognition (asamprajnata) -- and since neither kind of intellect strictly applies here, how can its occurrence be denied at all? To this the word 'samadhiyate' (is fixed/established) provides the answer.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Those whose mind has been carried away by that flowery speech, i.e. those who are drawn towards worldly enjoyments, and who are deeply attached to enjoyment and prosperity, do not develop a resolute intellect fixed on the Supreme.

Commentary: 'Tayapahrtachetasam' -- Those whose mind has been carried away by the flowery speech described in the previous verses -- speech describing great pleasures in heaven, the divine Nandanavana, celestial nymphs, and nectar -- and whose mind is thus drawn towards such enjoyments.
'Bhogaishvaryaprasaktanam' -- Deriving pleasure through the five objects of sense (sound, touch, form, taste, smell), bodily comfort, honour and fame is called enjoyment (bhoga). The accumulation of wealth, property, houses, etc. for the sake of such enjoyment is called prosperity (aishvarya). Those who are attached to, fond of, and drawn towards this enjoyment and prosperity -- who regard them as supremely important -- are called 'bhogaishvaryaprasaktanam'.

Those who remain absorbed only in enjoyment and prosperity possess a demoniac nature; for 'asu' means the vital breath, and those devoted to nourishing and preserving that vital breath are called 'asuras'. Giving prime importance to the body, they wish to enjoy pleasures here or in heaven.
'Vyavasayatmika buddhih samadhau na vidhiyate' -- Such people do not develop the resolute intellect directed towards attaining the Supreme, which is the true purpose of human birth and the very reason the human body has been obtained. This means that because of the impressions left by pleasures already enjoyed, pleasures that can be enjoyed, pleasures heard of, and pleasures that could be heard of, their intellect remains impure; and due to that impurity they never form the firm resolve to renounce the world entirely and turn wholly towards the Supreme. Similarly, those who take pride in accumulating various worldly skills and knowledge -- thinking 'I am learned, I am knowledgeable' -- and who become attached to the pleasure born of such pride, also never form a firm resolve to attain the Supreme.

Special note: The most compassionate Lord has, out of grace, endowed this human body with a unique power of discrimination by which one may rise above pleasure and pain, liberate oneself, serve all, and bring even the Lord under one's sway -- this alone is the true purpose of human life. But to disregard this God-given power of discrimination and become attached to perishable enjoyment and accumulation is the mentality of an animal; for animals and birds too remain engrossed in enjoyment -- if man does the same, what difference remains between man and beast?
Animals and birds belong to the species meant only for enjoyment, so for them the question of duty does not arise. But the human birth has been obtained solely to fulfil one's duty and thereby attain liberation, not for the sake of enjoyment. Therefore whatever favourable or unfavourable circumstances come before a person are all means for practice (sadhana), not objects of enjoyment. Those who regard them as objects of enjoyment do not develop a resolute intellect fixed on the Supreme.
In truth, worldly objects do not obstruct one's movement towards the Supreme; rather, it is the importance presently given to enjoyment in the heart that obstructs. Enjoyments themselves do not hinder as much as the importance attached to them hinders. The prominence of one's own inclination and intent is what causes the obstruction. If one retains a taste for enjoyment and accumulation while wishing to attain the Supreme, then attaining the Supreme is far off -- one cannot even form a firm resolve towards it. For wherever there is inclination towards moving to the Supreme, there enjoyment's inclination is also present; as long as there remains a taste for enjoyment, accumulation, honour and comfort, one cannot fix oneself with firm resolve upon the Supreme, because one's mind has been carried away by the taste for enjoyment -- the very power that could have been applied to the Supreme has instead gone into enjoyment and accumulation.

Connection: To emphasize a point, both sides of it are first presented and then it is reinforced. Here the Lord wishes to reinforce the attitude of desirelessness (nishkama bhava); therefore, having described in the preceding three verses those attached to desire-driven action, He now, in the following verse, inspires the attitude of desirelessness.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
The ignorant, whose knowledge is little, and who have as their sole aim the attainment of enjoyment and power, speak the flowery language i.e., having its flowers (show) only as fruits, which look apparently beautiful at first sight. They rejoice in the letter of the Vedas i.e., they are attached to heaven and such other results (promised in the Karma-kanda of the Vedas). They say that there is nothing else, owing to their intense attachment to these results. They say that there is no fruit superior to heaven etc. They are full of worldly desires and their minds are highly attached to secular desires. They hanker for heaven, i.e. think of the enjoyment of the felicities of heaven, after which one can again have rirth which offers again the opportunity to perform varied rites devoid of true knowledge and leads towards the attainment of enjoyments and power once again.

With regard to those who cling to pleasure and power and whose understanding is contaminated by that flowery speech relating to pleasure and lordly powers, the aforesaid mental disposition characterised by resolution, will not arise in their Samadhi. Samadhi here means the mind. The knowledge of the self will not arise in such minds. In the minds of these persons, there cannot arise the mental disposition that looks on all Vedic rituals as means for liberation based on the determined conviction about the real form of the self. Hence, in an aspirant for liberation, there should be no attachment to rituals out of the conviction that they are meant for the acisition of objects of desire only.

It may be estioned why the Vedas, which have more of love for Jivas than thousands of parents, and which are endeavouring to save the Jivas, should prescribe in this way rites whose fruits are infinitesimal and which produce only new births. It can also be asked if it is proper to abandon what is given in the Vedas. Sri Krsna replies to these estions.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
And vyavasayatmika, one-pointed; buddhih, conviction, with regard to Knowledge or Yoga; na vidhiyate, does not become established, i.e. does not arise; samadhau, in the minds  the word samadhi being derived in the sese of that into which everthing is gathered together for the enjoyment of a person ; bhoga-aisvarya-prasaktanam, of those who delight in enjoyment and wealth, of those who have the hankering that only enjoyment as also wealth is to be sought for, of those who identify themselves with these; and apahrta-cetasam, of those whose intellects are carried away, whose discriminating judgement becomes covered; taya, by that speech which is full of various special rites.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
To dispel a possible misunderstanding of 'tayapahrta' etc., the word 'tesham' (of them) is used. Their intellect, that is, the disposition of mind, does not become truly resolute -- that is, characterized by correct and well-reasoned determination. Hence it is not directed toward proper fixation of the mind on the Lord. If one asks why the absence of samadhi follows from the absence of correct determinate understanding, the word 'samyak' (properly) answers this, and thereby the meaning of the word 'samadhi' itself is also clarified. If one asks what need there is for fixing the mind on the Lord, such that its absence would be blameworthy, the word 'taddhi' (that indeed) answers this -- for the absence of liberation is indeed a grave fault, as will be stated later. This refers to those whose object has been correctly ascertained; and this is not merely a matter of inference but is also stated in the Puranas, as shown by the quotation beginning 'uktam cha.' Here 'variyasih' means 'excellent' (words), the case-ending elided by grammatical rule. 'Vachah' refers to Vedic words. 'By reasoning as in a dream' means through the example of what is experienced in a dream; 'heyanumitam' means inferred as fit to be renounced. This indeed is what constitutes true, correct ascertainment of the object -- namely, that through discrimination between what is to be renounced and what is to be accepted, the renouncing of the former and the acceptance of the latter become the means to liberation; and this is well established in the scriptures.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
For those who are of such a nature -- whose minds have been carried away by that speech, and who are devoted to desire-prompted action -- a single resolute intellect directed toward samadhi does not arise; or, it is not firmly established. Their 'samadhi' is merely in the heart, that is, a superficial fixation, not true concentration.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
One might ask: since the means of valid knowledge is the same for all, why does resolute determination not arise even in those who lack it? Because an obstruction is present, it does not arise -- this is explained through these three verses. The construction is: those unwise people whose minds have been carried away by this speech which they proclaim do not attain a resolute intellect. This 'speech,' well known as prescribed by the injunctions of Vedic study, is called 'flowery' because, like a flowering but fruitless plant, it is pleasing only at first sight, showing an apparent but false connection between means and end, and lacking any supreme, unsurpassed fruit. Why does it lack an unsurpassed fruit? Because it bestows only the fruit of birth and action -- birth meaning a new, unprecedented body and senses, action dependent on that birth and determined by identification with caste and life-stage, and fruit dependent on that action, such as sons, cattle, and heaven -- all perishable, given in unbroken succession like a water-wheel. Why is it so? Because it is 'abounding in various rites' directed toward the attainment of enjoyment and lordship: enjoyment consisting in things such as drinking ambrosia, sporting with celestial nymphs, and the fragrance of the heavenly Parijata tree; and lordship consisting in dominion like that of the gods -- the various rites, such as Agnihotra, Darsha-Purnamasa, and Jyotishtoma, are the means to attaining these, and hence the speech is called 'abounding' or extensive, being of great abundance. Indeed it is well known that the ritualistic portion of the Veda is, in every respect, far more extensive than the portion dealing with knowledge. Such people proclaim this ritualistic speech, and accept it as bestowing the highest attainable fruit, such as heaven. Who are they? The unwise, those devoid of any settled understanding born of reflection. Hence they delight in the mere statements of the Veda -- the eulogistic passages (arthavada) found in the Veda, such as 'the merit of one who performs the four-monthly sacrifices is imperishable' -- and, satisfied with the false conviction that these statements are literally and ultimately true, they say, O Partha, that there is nothing else, meaning that, compared to the ritualistic portion, there is no other, higher portion of knowledge, since the entire Veda is concerned with enjoining action, and that, compared to the fruit of action, there is no other unsurpassed fruit of knowledge -- thus they speak at great length in a manner contrary to the import of the knowledge-portion of the Veda. Why do they hate liberation? Because, being 'kamatmanah,' their minds are consumed by desire, agitated by hundreds of desired objects. Given this, why do they not desire liberation? Because they are 'svargaparah,' regarding heaven alone -- endowed with things like the nymph Urvashi -- as the highest goal; deluded into thinking there is no higher human end than heaven, and, lacking discrimination and dispassion, unable even to bear hearing talk of liberation. For such people -- attached to the aforementioned enjoyment and lordship, their minds fixed firmly on these through not perceiving their perishable and defective nature, and whose discriminative knowledge has been covered and carried away by that speech abounding in various rites -- the eulogistic Vedic passages, though meant merely as praise, remain something they are unable to properly understand; and hence, for them, a resolute intellect does not become fixed within, for the sake of concentration (samadhi). Alternatively, it may mean that a resolute intellect directed toward samadhi does not arise in them. By the etymology 'that in which everything is fixed,' samadhi may mean either the inner faculty (antahkarana) or the Supreme Self -- this is not an arbitrary construal. In the explanation where 'samadhi' is taken as the state of abiding in the realization 'I am Brahman,' it is this more established, traditional sense that is followed. The purport is this: although desire-prompted rites such as Agnihotra are not different, as acts, from those performed merely for purification, still, because of the defect of being motivated by desire for fruit, they do not bring about the destruction of past sin in a truly purifying way. Purity that is oriented toward enjoyment is not conducive to knowledge. Whereas actions performed without any desire for fruit do produce a purity that is conducive to knowledge -- thus is established the distinction in result between the wise and the unwise. This will be explained further, at length, later.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
And so, 'bhogaishvaryaprasaktanam' means those who are attached, firmly fixed, to enjoyment and lordship. Those whose minds have been carried away -- drawn away -- by that flowery speech: for them, a determinate intellect directed toward samadhi, that is, one-pointedness of mind oriented toward concentration on the Supreme Lord, does not arise. The verb is used in a passive construction with the action as subject; the meaning is, it does not come into being.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Of those who are deeply attached to enjoyment and lordship, whose minds have thereby been carried away and covered by that speech -- for them, the resolute intellect belonging either to Sankhya or to Yoga does not become fixed, that is, does not become stable, in samadhi, the inner faculty (antahkarana) in which everything is gathered for the enjoyment of the self. One might ask: why have the commentators not shown both explanations -- namely, that samadhi is the state of abiding in the realization 'I am Brahman,' for the sake of which the intellect fails to arise, and that samadhi is one-pointedness of mind directed toward concentration on the Supreme Lord? The answer to be understood is that both the abiding in 'I am Brahman' and one-pointedness directed toward the Supreme Lord are intended to be included within the resolute intellect belonging to Sankhya and Yoga.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Therefore, for those who are attached to enjoyment and lordship, whose minds have been carried away by that speech, the intellect -- being in a state of distraction -- does not become fixed in samadhi, that is, in contemplation of the Lord. This is the meaning.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
'Bhoga' etc. Of those men whose minds have been carried away by that flowery speech, the intellect, at the time of practicing samadhi, does not become resolute -- that is, does not take the form of pure consciousness alone, which is what 'vyavasaya' (resolve), meaning knowledge, in essence signifies; it simply does not arise. The verb is used in a passive construction with the action as subject. It is quite clear that the intellect of one who is dispassionate takes the form of pure consciousness in samadhi, but not that of one attached to enjoyment and the like. In the Bhashya (of Shankara), however, it is explained that a resolute intellect does not arise with regard to samadhi, understood as the inner faculty (antahkarana). Alternatively, it may be explained that their intellect does not become resolute even for the sake of the practice of samadhi.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
In the minds of those who are too attached to sense enjoyment and material opulence, and who are bewildered by such things, the resolute determination for devotional service to the Supreme Lord does not take place.

 Samādhi means “fixed mind.” The Vedic dictionary, the Nirukti, says, samyag ādhīyate ’sminn ātma-tattva-yāthātmyam: “When the mind is fixed for understanding the self, it is said to be in samādhi .” Samādhi is never possible for persons interested in material sense enjoyment and bewildered by such temporary things. They are more or less condemned by the process of material energy.
