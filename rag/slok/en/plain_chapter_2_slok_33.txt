Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
If you turn away from this war, you will not only lose your own duty and fame, but will surely also become a sinner; failing to resist the unrighteous is as grave a sin as slaying an innocent person. The word dharma has been discussed earlier. Every being takes birth in this world in a particular body, with previously acquired impressions (vasanas), for a special purpose - namely, to exhaust those impressions and recognise its own true nature. The impressions with which each person is born constitute that person's own nature, called svadharma. Arjuna's svadharma is that of a Kshatriya, whose special quality is honoured and glorious valour. To lose the opportunities that come in life for exhausting these impressions is to create an obstacle on the path of growth; if they are not exhausted, the pressure of impressions upon the mind only increases, since fresh impressions keep accumulating along with the old. Even if Arjuna were to flee the battlefield at this moment, he would later only repent having lost this opportunity, for such flight is utterly contrary to his Kshatriya nature, which can find lasting peace only through battle. A child with a natural inclination and aptitude for the arts can never become a successful merchant; and if parents, out of love, force their own wishes upon their son, his personality is often seen to remain fragmented. Such examples are found in every field in the world, and especially in the spiritual field: many people, driven by momentary dispassion arising from a little sorrow or hardship, leave their homes in search of God and go into the forests, only to find restlessness and sorrow there throughout their lives. The mind still holds desires for sense-enjoyment that could be fulfilled within family life; but by leaving home and sitting in the caves of the Himalayas, they neither fulfil these desires nor find it possible to meditate upon God. Naturally, agitations increase in their minds, and these are called sin. According to Hindu tradition, the mistakes a person commits by forgetting his own true nature as the Self are called sin. It is natural for agitation to arise in the mind through a person's efforts to attain pleasure through sense-enjoyment, and this itself is sin, since in it there is forgetfulness of the blissful nature of the Self. Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Having shown that performing one's own duty of war with faith brings the attainment of heaven and other great fruits, the commentator now explains, in view of the sin that follows from not performing it, the meaning of the word 'atha' found in the next verse, with the word 'evam' (thus). Its being enjoined and its being fruitful are meant by this manner [of explanation]; that is the purport of the connection. The word 'punaścet' is explained as a repetition of the conditional. By the term 'Mahadeva and others' is meant Mahendra and the other gods.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Now if you will not fight this righteous war, then, forsaking your own duty and fame, you will incur sin.

Explanation - 'atha cettvamimam ... papamavapsyasi' - Here the word 'atha' is used in the sense of an alternative, and 'cet' in the sense of supposition. Their purport is that although you cannot really refrain from fighting - overpowered by your Kshatriya nature you will fight in any case (Gita 18.60) - yet, granting for argument's sake that you do not fight, you would be abandoning your Kshatriya duty. By abandoning that duty sin would attach to you, and your fame too would be destroyed.
What will you gain by forsaking the duty that has come to you unsought as your own? By giving up your own duty you would have to adopt another's duty, which would bring you sin. And by giving up the fight, other people would think that even a hero like Arjuna became afraid of death - and this would destroy your fame.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
If in delusion, you do not wage this war, which has started and which is the duty of a Ksatriya, then, owing to the non-performance of your immediate and incumbent duty, you will lose the immeasurable bliss which is the fruit of discharging your duty and the immeasurable fame which is the fruit of victory. In addition, you will incur extreme sin.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Arjuna had objected that he had no desire for the fruit of the war - 'I do not desire victory, O Krishna... nor even the kingdom of the three worlds' - and so questioned why he should perform it. To this the commentator now states the fault in not performing it, with the word 'atha', meaning 'on the other hand'. 'This' righteous war refers to the one to be fought against heroes like Bhishma and Drona; 'dharmya' means untainted by faults such as foul play, i.e., not deviating from the conduct of the righteous. Manu likewise lays down the code of righteous warfare: one engaged in battle should not slay enemies with barbed, poisoned, or fire-tipped weapons; nor one who has climbed to safety, nor an emasculate, nor one with joined palms in surrender, nor one with loosened hair, nor one seated, nor one who says 'I am yours'; nor one asleep, unarmoured, naked, or unarmed; nor one not fighting and merely watching, nor one engaged with another, nor one afflicted by a weapon-mishap, nor one wounded, nor one afraid, nor one who has turned back - remembering always the conduct of the righteous. One who fights transgressing this conduct becomes sinful indeed. But you, though invited by the enemy to this righteous war, will not fight it - if, out of fear of dharma or of the world, you turn back, then you will incur only the sin arising from failing to perform the war enjoined by scripture ('having conquered the enemy's forces, one should protect the earth with dharma') and from forsaking your own duty and the fame earned through encounters with Mahadeva and others, and from turning away from battle as forbidden by scripture ('one should not turn back from battle') - you will not, in that case, be gaining dharma and fame [as you suppose]. Or else, it means that you will abandon the merit accumulated over many births and instead incur only the sin created by the king for desertion. Since these enemies will surely slay you should you turn back, the point is: struck down while fleeing, do not, by forsaking the merit long accumulated by yourself, come to bear only the sin created by another. Manu confirms this: one who, through fear, turns back and is slain in battle by the enemy takes upon himself whatever sin his lord has incurred, while his lord takes whatever merit he had accumulated for the next world. Yajnavalkya likewise says that the king takes the merit of those slain while fleeing. By this, the statement earlier made - that only sin would accrue from slaying these aggressors, and that Arjuna did not wish to slay them even though they might slay him, O Madhusudana - stands refuted.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Having thus stated that war, being righteous, is the means to supreme happiness, the commentator now states the fault that follows from its non-performance, with the words 'atha cet'. Since the non-performance of war is not a cause of sin for Brahmins and others, the word 'you' is understood to refer specifically to your status as a Kshatriya - hence it is explained as 'which is the Kshatriya's own duty'. The word 'this' is explained as referring to the war 'already undertaken'. 'Out of delusion' means through the mistaken notion that even a righteous act is unrighteous. Mere non-performance of this one war would not by itself be a cause of sin even for a Kshatriya, since that would imply he must fight at all times; hence it is qualified as 'of the duty already begun'. 'The fruit of one's own duty' - here the word dharma refers to its fruit, for otherwise it would be a mere redundant repetition without leading to the undesirable consequence being shown. 'By victory' is said with reference to the fame yet to come. The purport of the sentence is that you would incur not merely the loss of the supreme good, seen and unseen, but sin itself, which is the cause of extreme suffering.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having thus considered your own duty, the commentator states that an obstacle would arise for you if you fail to wage the war spoken of by Me, with the words 'atha cet'. The meaning is: even after considering your own duty, if you still do not undertake this righteous war before Me, which is in the form of My command, then, forsaking your own duty and fame, you will incur sin.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
The commentator states that by abandoning the war, the desired good is lost and the undesired is gained, with the words 'atha cet'.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
If, however, you do not perform your religious duty of fighting, then you will certainly incur sins for neglecting your duties and thus lose your reputation as a fighter.

 Arjuna was a famous fighter, and he attained fame by fighting many great demigods, including even Lord Śiva. After fighting and defeating Lord Śiva in the dress of a hunter, Arjuna pleased the lord and received as a reward a weapon called pāśupata-astra. Everyone knew that he was a great warrior. Even Droṇācārya gave him benedictions and awarded him the special weapon by which he could kill even his teacher. So he was credited with so many military certificates from many authorities, including his adoptive father Indra, the heavenly king. But if he abandoned the battle, not only would he neglect his specific duty as a kṣatriya, but he would lose all his fame and good name and thus prepare his royal road to hell. In other words, he would go to hell not by fighting but by withdrawing from battle.
