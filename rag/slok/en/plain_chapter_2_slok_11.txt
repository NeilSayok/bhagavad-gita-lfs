Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
When we try to properly understand Arjuna's despondency, it is not hard to recognize that, though its immediate cause is the challenge of battle, these symptoms of mental anguish in truth stem from some other, deeper cause. Just as a skilled physician does not merely treat the symptoms of a disease but seeks to remove the disease's root cause, so too here Lord Sri Krishna seeks to remove the very root cause of Arjuna's grief and delusion — ignorance of the Self.

Ego arises from ignorance of one's pure Self-nature. This ignorance not only veils the divine nature but also generates delusion about that very truth. Arjuna's ego-intellect — the individual's sense that he is limited or bounded by the conditionings of body, mind and intellect — is in truth the cause of his delusion, from which, through attachment to his kinsmen, this despondency and compassion have arisen in his mind. He feels himself incapable and helpless. The deluded person must pay for his attachment in the currency of sorrow and grief. Because of false identification with these conditionings of the body and the rest, we continue to receive suffering; when we gain knowledge of our pure Self-nature, that suffering comes to an end.

The eternal, conscious Self, through false identification with the gross body, experiences itself as bound in relation to countless things and persons. That same Self, in identification with the mind, experiences numerous emotions, as though that emotional world were its own. Then this same consciousness, endowed with the intellect-conditioning, hopes and desires, holds ambitions and ideals, on account of which it must also suffer. Desire, ambition and the rest are properties of the intellect alone.

Thus the pure Self, endowed with senses, mind and intellect, having attained individual selfhood, becomes the slave and prey of external objects, emotions and thoughts. Life's countless sorrows and momentary joys exist only on account of this sense of individual selfhood. It was this very individual selfhood, on account of which Arjuna was experiencing anguish. Sri Krishna knew that the root cause of this delusion in the form of grief is the veil of ignorance of one's own Self-nature, and hence, to remove Arjuna's despondency at its very root, he now imparts the Self-knowledge expounded in the Upanishads.

India gave to the world, thousands of years ago, the knowledge of re-educating the mind through psychological and spiritual means. Here Sri Krishna's teaching in the Gita is exactly this endeavor. Following the traditional method of teaching Self-knowledge, the World-Teacher Lord Sri Krishna directly imparts the truth of the Self.

Because Bhishma's and Drona's inner beings were pure, the light of consciousness shone clearly in them. Both these great men were incomparable. That they would attain a lower state upon death in this battle is a thought only an immature intellect could entertain. Through this verse, Lord Sri Krishna draws Arjuna's attention to the higher nature of the individual self.

Our personality has many facets, and through identification with each of them, we view life from that very standpoint. Through the body we see the external or physical world, which differs from the emotional world experienced through the mind, and likewise, through the intellect, we experience the world of thought.

From the physical standpoint, whom I regard as merely a woman, through the mind I see as my own mother. If, through the intellect, one conducts a purely scientific examination, her body is merely a particular mass composed of countless cells with protoplasm and nucleus and the like. Sorrows arising from the flaw or imperfection of a physical object can be removed if my feeling toward it is transformed. Similarly, what is ugly and shameful from the physical and emotional standpoint, if viewed through the intellect from the philosophical standpoint, can, through this shift in our outlook, remove our sorrow.

Carrying this same fact further, we come to know that if I could view life from the spiritual standpoint, the despondency arising from the physical, mental and intellectual standpoints could be transformed into joy and inspiring vigor. Here the Lord teaches Arjuna exactly this — that he should abandon the standpoint of his ignorance and endeavor to see and understand his elders, his own kinsmen, the battlefield, and all the rest, from the spiritual standpoint.

This great transcendent truth is here taught so unexpectedly and suddenly that it struck Arjuna's intellect like a shock. Reading the following verses, we shall understand what a beneficial effect this shock, which the Lord delivered to Arjuna's intellect, had upon Arjuna's mind. Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
He now cites the Lord's actual words, in 'Shri Bhagavan said...' Having earlier explained the intended sense of the whole preceding passage, he now marks its division into sentences: the opening verse, 'On the field of dharma, at Kurukshetra' and the rest, forms one sentence, since it merely sets the narrative context; the whole portion from 'having seen' up through 'he fell silent' forms a second sentence; and from here through 'these words' constitutes a third — the introduction proper. He explains why this middle portion, despite spanning so many verses, counts as a single sentence: because its entire purport is unified around one single point — namely, demonstrating that grief and delusion (along with their subordinate forms) are the very seed of the sorrowful cycle of worldly existence, and that their root cause is egoism and possessiveness born of ignorance. He elaborates this at length: the 'kingdom' refers to a king's duty of governance; the 'venerable elders' are Bhishma, Drona and the rest; sons, brothers-in-law, kinsmen, and relations by marriage are all listed by name to show the full range of attachment-objects whose loss, real or feared, gives rise to grief and delusion — this is what is meant by 'these two, grief and delusion, are the seed of worldly existence.'

One might object: how can such delusion be attributed to Arjuna, a man of renowned wisdom and discrimination, naturally engaged in his own dharma? Anticipating this, he explains that grief and delusion, by causing the abandonment of enjoined duty and the taking up of forbidden conduct through obscuring discrimination, are indeed causes of ruin — and it is precisely this ruinous potential that makes their presence in Arjuna, despite his qualities, entirely plausible. He further shows, by generalizing from Arjuna's own case, that grief and delusion are the common seed of worldly bondage for all beings whatsoever: whoever acts under their influence, even while nominally performing prescribed duties, does so with egoism and desire for fruit, and this generates the endless round of merit and demerit, birth and death, pleasure and pain that constitutes samsara. Having thus shown that the first chapter, together with this portion of the second, serves to display the seed-causes of bondage that Self-knowledge alone (preceded by renunciation of all action) can remove, he now shows the intent of the teaching that follows: since the Lord wishes to impart saving knowledge for the benefit of the whole world, and Arjuna, though the immediate occasion, is not Himself yet fit to receive it in that very moment — the instruction being properly fruitful only after the purification of mind that follows from selfless performance of one's own duty — the Lord speaks 'taking Arjuna as the occasion.' This settles that the true purport of the Gita's teaching is: liberation is attained through Self-knowledge alone, preceded by the renunciation of all action — not through knowledge combined with ritual action.

He then takes up, in order to refute, the view of certain other commentators, who hold that liberation is never attained from steadfastness in knowledge alone, but only from knowledge combined with obligatory Vedic and smriti-based rites such as the agnihotra — citing as evidence verses like 'if you will not wage this righteous war' (2.33), 'you have a right to action alone' (2.47), and 'therefore you must perform action' (4.15), and arguing that since scripture itself declares the omission of one's own dharma to be sinful, and battle, though violent, to be the Kshatriya's own dharma, Vedic rites involving violence such as animal sacrifice cannot on that account be considered adharma either — hence a combination of ritual action and knowledge is intended throughout the Gita.

This view, he argues at very great length — following almost exactly the same line of reasoning already given in extenso at the close of the previous verse's commentary [by the teacher Shankara] — is untenable: because the Lord Himself distinguishes two separate, mutually exclusive disciplines, of knowledge and of action, suited to two different types of aspirants (Sankhyas and Yogis respectively), as shown by 'this understanding has been declared to you in Sankhya; now hear it with regard to Yoga' (2.39), and by his statement that he taught the steadfastness of knowledge 'in ancient times' to the Sankhyas and separately taught the steadfastness of action to the Yogis through karma-yoga (3.3); because this same twofold division of aspirants by their fitness for either knowledge or action, according to their inner state, is corroborated by an ancient scriptural text (cited from the Brihadaranyaka/Shatapatha tradition) distinguishing those who, still possessed of desire and ignorance, are enjoined in ritual action for the sake of the fathers' world or the gods' world, from those who, having become free of desire, are enjoined instead in the path of complete renunciation for the sake of the Self, which is their true world; because Arjuna's later question, 'if you deem knowledge superior to action...' (3.1), and his further question, 'tell me decisively which one of these two is better for me' (5.1), would be entirely nonsensical if a combination of the two, applicable to all including Arjuna, had truly been the Lord's earlier teaching — for one does not ask a physician who has already prescribed a combined regimen to now specify only one component of it; because the Lord's actual reply to that later question addresses it directly, by clarifying that two distinct paths were taught long ago (3.3), rather than by correcting a false premise, which He surely would have done had combination truly been His intended teaching; and because various passages, such as 'though acting, the knower is not bound' (13.31 area) and 'Janaka and others attained perfection through action alone' (3.20), are properly understood — not as evidence of combination — but either as describing the outwardly action-resembling conduct that a knower, already free of egoism and desire for fruit, continues purely for the sake of guiding ordinary people (exactly as the Blessed Vasudeva's own conduct as a Kshatriya is not, for that very reason, combined with His own knowledge), or as describing action undertaken by one not yet a knower, whose fruit — purification of mind, or the very arising of knowledge — is offered to the Lord as a mere preparatory means, not as a co-equal partner with knowledge in producing liberation itself. Hence the settled conclusion of the entire Gita scripture is that liberation is attained through knowledge of the Self alone, arising after the renunciation of all action — a conclusion he promises to demonstrate section by section as the text proceeds. It is with this very purpose — to lift Arjuna, sunk deep in the ocean of grief, his mind utterly confused about dharma, out of this bondage through the one true means, Self-knowledge — that the Blessed Lord now begins to speak, in 'Ashochyan...'

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
The Blessed Lord said: You have grieved for those not to be grieved for, and you speak words of learning; yet the wise grieve neither for those whose life-breath has departed nor for those whose life-breath has not departed.

Explanation: In all the world there are two things — the imperishable and the perishable, the embodied Self and the body. Of these, the embodied Self is indestructible, while the body is destructible. Neither of these is fit to be grieved for. The imperishable never perishes, so there is no occasion for grief over it; and the perishable is bound to perish — it does not remain even for a moment — so grief over it too is not fitting. Hence grief cannot properly be directed either at the embodied Self or at the body.

Whatever circumstance a person faces — birth and death, gain and loss — is simply the fruit of his own past actions (prarabdha). To grieve or rejoice over such favorable or unfavorable circumstances is mere folly, for every circumstance, favorable or unfavorable, has a beginning and an end — it did not exist before and will not remain in the end. What does not exist at the beginning or the end cannot be permanent even for a moment in between. Grieving or rejoicing over such momentarily perishing circumstances is folly alone.

'Prajnavadamsh cha bhashase' — on one hand you speak like a learned man, and on the other you also grieve. So you merely make speeches; in truth you are not learned, for the truly learned never grieve for anyone. Your very own learned words — that the destruction of the family destroys the family dharma, that this leads to the corruption of women and the birth of mixed offspring, and that this drags the family-destroyers and their ancestors to hell for want of offerings — themselves prove that the body is perishable while the embodied Self within is imperishable. If the body itself were not perishable, there would be no fear of hell for the family-destroyer, no anxiety over the fall of the ancestors. That you fear the fall of your ancestors and the family proves that the body is perishable while its indweller is eternal. Hence your grief over the destruction of bodies is improper.

'Gatasun agatasunsh cha' — for everyone, separation of the body from the life-breath is inevitable; some have already undergone it, some are yet to. Hence none of them should be grieved for; that you have grieved is your own error. To grieve for those who have died is a great error, for grief over the dead only causes suffering for them. Just as the funeral rice-offerings and water offered for the departed soul reach it in the next world, so too the phlegm and tears shed for it must, against its will, be consumed by it. Nor should one grieve for the living — for them one should instead provide sustenance and make proper arrangements. Worry itself never helps.

The root cause of physical symptoms such as trembling limbs and a parched mouth is identification with the body; from this identification arises a sense of 'my own' toward those who sustain the body, and it is this very sense of ownership that gives rise, in Arjuna's mind, to anxiety and grief at the fear of his kinsmen's death — and it is this anxiety and grief that produces the bodily symptoms described earlier. The Lord identifies precisely this grief over 'gatasun' (those whose breath has departed) and 'agatasun' (those whose breath has not departed) as the cause. Both these anxieties concern the body alone, and hence, in essence, they are one and the same — for both the dead and the living are equally perishable in their bodies.

For both 'gatasun' and 'agatasun,' performing one's proper duty is no cause for anxiety: offering rice and water, performing the shraddha rites, is the duty for the departed; making arrangements for their sustenance is the duty toward the living. Duty is not a matter for anxiety but for right thinking; right thinking gives rise to awareness of duty, while anxiety destroys right thinking.

'Na anushochanti panditah' — the intellect that discriminates between the real and the unreal is called 'panda'; those in whom this discriminative faculty has fully developed — who have gained clear discrimination between the real and the unreal — are the truly learned. Such learned persons feel no grief regarding either the real or the unreal, for regarding the real as real brings no grief, nor does regarding the unreal as unreal. The Self alone is of the nature of the real, while the changing body is of the nature of the unreal. Grief arises only from mistaking the unreal for the real — that is, from the wish that this body and the rest should remain forever unchanged, never dying. Grief never arises in regard to the real.

Transition: to resolve the doubt as to why grieving over the real principle is improper, the Lord speaks the next two verses.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
'Those not to be grieved for': Bhishma, Drona and the rest are not to be grieved for, since they are of virtuous conduct and, in their ultimate reality, eternal. You have grieved for them, thinking: 'they die on my account; what shall I do, bereft of them, with kingdom and pleasure?' Yet you speak the words of the wise, the intelligent. In this you display, in yourself, folly and wisdom that contradict one another, like a madman — this is the sense. Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Now, at the point where the exposition of the Gita truly begins, he states the purport of the preceding text, from 'On the field of dharma' (1.1) up to 'those not to be grieved for you have grieved' — since its meaning has so far remained hidden — in 'Tatra...': it is said, in the song, through certain verses. One might object: since neither dharma nor ultimate truth is expounded there, why is it included in the Gita at all? Not so — since that portion of the text shows the occasion for the Lord's instructing Arjuna, its inclusion is entirely fitting for that very purpose. Delusion regarding kinsmen and the rest consists of false notions such as 'they are mine, I am theirs; they will perish on my account; how could I live without them; sin will come to me; victory itself is doubtful' — or simply affection for them. Being, as it were, entangled in a net by this, Arjuna sinks into despondency. Despondency is a weakness of mind arising from grief born of delusion, in which all activity ceases. One might ask: why does delusion arise in Arjuna only now — did he not already know these were his kinsmen before he made his great effort to prepare for battle? He says, in 'Senayoh...': though anger, sustained by the memory of great wrongs, persists, it is well known that in tender-hearted people it recedes before kinsmen at the moment of their destruction, and affection arises instead — hence delusion. It should be understood that, Arjuna being a man of wisdom, his being caught in this web of delusion was but slight. Some explain 'prajnavadan' as 'the words of the wise, the intelligent' — but this is not correct, for no wise utterance is to be found in Arjuna's earlier statements such as 'having seen these kinsmen' (1.28); the wise do not speak of restraining or opposing devotees of Narayana and their followers as adharma, nor does mere discourse on dharma and adharma constitute wise speech, since even Buddhists and others speak of such matters. Hence it is explained otherwise: 'prajnavadan' means words arising from one's own mere opinion, not derived from the teaching of scripture or a teacher — 'prajna' being used in the sense of 'one's own,' as in 'ab-bhaksha' (water alone as food), a determinate compound, otherwise being pointless. 'Gatasun' — those whose destruction is imminent — how are they not to be grieved for? One might object: has it not already been stated, 'you have grieved,' referring merely to the previously established fact, not that they are not-to-be-grieved-for — how then does this become an objection? Not so — the sense of the sentence here is not to be taken merely at face value, since it would otherwise involve the fault of stating what is already established while leaving what is unestablished unproven; rather, the sense is: those for whom you have grieved are themselves not to be grieved for, hence grief for them ought not to be undertaken; and those words which you speak are mere personal opinion, hence ought not to be spoken. Thus the question — 'how are they not to be grieved for?' — is entirely appropriate.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Now, for the removal of man's delusion and grief, the Sankhya wisdom is stated. Sankhya, though of many varieties, has here one true, authoritative form: that in which the twenty-eight principles (tattvas) have Hari Himself as their very nature. Others are excluded even in the source texts. Yoga too is a single, ever-honored path, in which meditation on the Lord — even in its objectless, seedless form — awakens knowledge of the Self. Through dispassion, knowledge, yoga, love, and austerity — through even one of these, firmly pursued — worshipping the Lord, one attains perfection. Hence, to destroy false understanding, Sankhya and Yoga are together proclaimed. Sankhya is described in terms of renunciation and non-renunciation: in Sankhya, renunciation is proclaimed — the destruction of egotism and possessiveness, complete freedom from self-centeredness — when the individual soul, established in its true nature, has taken refuge in Hari, its purpose fulfilled. In non-renunciation lies the path of Yoga; here too, renunciation occurs, but only mentally; the disciplines (yamas and the rest) are still to be practiced, and fulfillment comes upon the perfection of Yoga. Yoga has the Lord as its support; having generated such devotion, it surely brings forth bhakti after the ripening of many births. In Sankhya too, when the mind is fixed on the Lord, this same fruit results, and not otherwise; through the surrender of actions, steadfast devotion arises. Hence, steadfastness in dharma is first expounded through the wisdom of Sankhya. Accordingly, the Blessed Lord Hrishikesha Vasudeva, at that time, not accepting Arjuna's grief on His own path, since it arises from Arjuna's very failure to discriminate the truth of the Self, states that this is not—

'Gatasun' means those who have attained death; 'agatasun' means the living — their wives and the rest. For the wise, since there is no true cause for grief, they do not grieve over what fate the dead have met in the next world, nor over what shall become of the living here. Or alternatively: 'gatasun' refers to inert, non-Self bodies, and 'agatasun' to the conscious individual souls; the wise, so defined, grieve for neither. What kind of learned man are you, then, that you grieve? — this is the sense. The heart of the matter is this: the Self, being of the nature of the imperishable, conscious Person as will be described, is of a single, unchanging kind; and the body, being a product of non-conscious material nature, is well known to be impermanent.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Arjuna's engagement in his own dharma of battle, though naturally arisen, is obstructed by a twofold delusion and the grief occasioned by it; this twofold delusion must be dispelled. One form — common to all beings — consists in the appearance of reality, selfhood, and the like in a worldly existence that is, in truth, illusory, owing to failure to discriminate the three conditionings (the gross body, the subtle body, and their cause, ignorance) from the Self, which is of the nature of self-luminous supreme bliss, untouched by any worldly property. The other — peculiar to Arjuna alone, rooted in the fault of misplaced compassion — consists in the false appearance of adharma in his own dharma of battle, owing to its abundance of violence. Of these, the dawning of knowledge of the pure Self's true nature, through discrimination of the three conditionings, removes the first delusion, and this remedy is common to all; but the removal of the second requires the specific realization that battle, though involving violence, is nevertheless one's own dharma and hence not adharma — a remedy peculiar to Arjuna. Grief itself, however, is removed simply by the removal of its cause, requiring no separate additional means. With this intent, addressing each of the two delusions in turn, the Blessed Lord said: you — though in fact learned — have grieved for Bhishma, Drona and the rest, who, along with the very Self, are not fit to be grieved for at all, thinking 'they die on my account; what shall I do, bereft of them, with kingdom and pleasure' — as shown in your own words, 'having seen these kinsmen' (1.28) and the rest. Thus, this illusion of grievability in what is not grievable — an illusion common even to animals — is unbecoming in one as supremely learned as you. Likewise, though upon reflection at my own words, 'whence has this weakness come upon you' (2.2), you might well have realized 'I have acted improperly,' yet you, though yourself possessed of intelligence, speak words unfit even for the intelligent to utter, such as 'how shall I fight Bhishma in battle' (2.4) — rather than falling silent out of shame. The word 'cha' (and) here suggests: what, then, is not improper in your conduct? Thus, this peculiar delusion — mistaking adharma for dharma and dharma for adharma — is likewise unbecoming in one so exceedingly learned as you. Alternatively: you speak the discourses of the wise, but do not truly understand them; or, since the act of grieving necessarily precedes the act of speaking about it, the past tense is used for the former and, since the speaking is immediately subsequent and hence practically simultaneous, the present tense for the latter — or, by a Vedic-style interchange of verb forms, 'anushochasi' (you grieve) is to be understood in the present sense throughout. One might object: is grief over the loss of kinsmen truly improper — did not even great souls like Vasishtha grieve so? Anticipating this, he says: the truly wise, possessed of Self-knowledge born of right inquiry, do not grieve over bodies conventionally regarded as kinsmen, whether their life-breath has departed or not — they are not bewildered by thoughts such as 'what has become of these who have died, where do they now dwell,' or 'how shall these living ones survive, bereft of their kinsmen' — for just as the illusion of a snake, once the true nature of the rope is directly perceived, leaves behind no trace of the fear or trembling it caused, or as one whose senses are disordered by bile, even while momentarily perceiving sugar as bitter, does not act on that false perception because the settled certainty of its sweetness remains stronger — so too, the illusion of grievability, rooted entirely in ignorance of the Self's true nature, cannot persist, along with its effects, once that very ignorance is dispelled by knowledge of the Self. As for Vasishtha and others, their apparent grief, arising from the force of their own prior karma, does not thereby establish a general precedent to be followed by others as proper conduct — for only that extraordinary behavior of the wise which is undertaken with the deliberate sense of dharma constitutes proper precedent; otherwise, even involuntary acts like spitting would have to be considered exemplary conduct. Since this is so, the intent is: you too, becoming truly wise, should not grieve.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Since Arjuna's grief arises from failure to discriminate between body and Self, the Blessed Lord speaks in order to reveal that discrimination, in 'Ashochyan...': you have grieved for kinsmen who are not truly objects of grief at all — as when you said, 'having seen these kinsmen, Krishna, arrayed and eager for battle' (1.28), and again, when I had awakened you with 'whence has this weakness come upon you at this critical moment?' Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Thus, Arjuna, though naturally engaged in the Kshatriya's dharma of battle, overwhelmed by grief and delusion caused by the false notion of 'I am theirs, they are mine' regarding his teachers and the rest, and by the pain of severed affection, withdrew from that battle and adopted another's dharma of living by alms. Now, since even those engaged in their own duty act with the three kinds of bodily, verbal, and mental activity driven by desire for fruit and egoism, worldly bondage — the cycle of desired and undesired birth and death arising from the accumulation of merit and demerit — does not cease. Hence, wishing to rescue this person, whose mind is confused about dharma and who is sunk deep in the ocean of grief, and seeing no other means of freedom from these two, which are the very seed of worldly existence, than the knowledge of truth gained through desireless action that purifies the mind, and wishing to teach the two disciplines that serve as the means to that end, the Blessed Lord Vasudeva, taking Arjuna as the occasion, speaks, in 'Ashochyan...' By this, Arjuna's twofold delusion is to be dispelled. Here it should be noted: the theory that one form of delusion — arising from failure to discriminate the three bodily conditionings, whereby illusory worldly existence appears real — is common to all beings, while the other — the notion that battle, one's own dharma, is adharma — is peculiar to Arjuna alone, is mistaken; for both forms of delusion are found generally among people in the world, as the commentator himself states when he says 'of all beings,' and as he himself establishes that the Lord's teaching, though occasioned by Arjuna, is truly meant for all the world — this is confirmed by such statements as 'there exists nothing better for a Kshatriya than a righteous war' and 'in this world' elsewhere in this very text, which would otherwise be inconsistent if Arjuna's delusion were held to be unique to him. Thus: you have grieved for those not deserving of grief, since they are truly existent and, in their ultimate nature, eternal; and you speak the words — the discourses — of the wise, the intelligent, such as 'what use is a kingdom to us' and 'how shall I fight Bhishma' — both of these together being the conduct of a madman, is the intent. For the wise, the knowers of the Self, do not grieve for the dead nor for the living — that is, they do not entertain the anxious thought, 'alas, how unfortunate that these have died,' or 'these will die.' As for the further remark that you speak words unfit even for the wise to utter, that need not detain us, since the sense of fitness is naturally implied and requires no separate grammatical marker.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Having said 'you have grieved for those not to be grieved for,' one might mistakenly think that the not-to-be-grieved-for are themselves grieving, and that Arjuna too now grieves for that reason; to dispel this confusion it is clarified as 'toward those not to be grieved for.' The use of the past-tense form 'anvashochah' may seem improper, since the grief is a present matter, as shown by 'you speak' (present tense) — to this he says: it should be read as 'anushochasi' (you grieve), the past-tense-with-preverb form here conveying a present action of long standing; or else, per the grammatical principle of interchangeability of tense-forms in a present sense, the past tense stands for the present. Regarding 'prajnavadamsh cha bhashase,' by the rule governing use of the past tense for an action just completed or as though present, the past tense is used, and hence there is no contradiction with 'he fell silent' (2.9). Here, by the word 'prajna,' meaning superior knowledge, is meant knowledge of the true nature of body and Self; 'prajnavada' means discourse informed by such knowledge — the word 'nimitta' (cause) here serving to clarify the sense of the compound. Since only upon knowledge of the distinction between body and Self does the confident practice of rites such as the offering of rice-balls and water to the ancestors, and the fear of transgression from their omission, make sense, this is indicated by the reference to 'they fall' (referring to the ancestors' fall, as Arjuna himself argued). The upshot is stated: the distinction between body and Self. In explaining the intended referent of 'gatasun' and the rest, and construing the word 'pandita' as relevant to the present context, he states the construction: although the words 'gatasun' and 'agatasun' literally mean 'the lifeless' and 'the living,' still, since that literal sense would be irrelevant here — as seen from passages describing the wise as grieving over neither the living nor the dead in other contexts, and since it will be explained at length below, in verses such as 'this Self is unmanifest' (2.25) and 'even if you think' (2.26), that the eternal Self and the non-eternal body are both, in their own way, not to be grieved for — the same reference is intended here too, is the sense. Bodies indeed are not to be grieved for, since they are perishable; Selves too are not to be grieved for, since they are imperishable. Those possessing the intellect capable of such discriminative reasoning are, in this context, the 'pandits.'

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having first, through knowledge of scriptural meaning, made the intellect steady, the teaching of devotion is then to be given — hence, having first stated all scripturally-declared knowledge, and now, in order to teach devotion, the Blessed Lord states the knowledge of Self and non-Self, in 'Ashochyan...': you have grieved for those not deserving of grief, since, being possessed by demonic dispositions, they are fit only to be slain by Me for the removal of the earth's burden, not being My devotees. Moreover, having grieved for them, you speak the words — the discourses — of the wise, the learned; you merely repeat their discourses, you yourself are not truly wise. For the truly wise, the learned, know that everything happens only by My will; hence they do not grieve over what fate awaits the departed in the next world, nor over how the living shall fare here. Hence scripture too says: 'He alone impels one toward good action whom He wishes to raise up; He alone impels one toward evil action whom He wishes to cast down...' (Kaushitaki Upanishad 3.9). Hence the sense is: why should grief be felt over what is done for My sake?

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Arjuna's two delusions are: thinking that the destruction of the body is the destruction of the Self, and thinking that battle, his own proper dharma, is adharma. To dispel the first of these, wishing to impart, in twenty verses forming the very seed of Self-knowledge, the Blessed Lord said, in 'Ashochyan anvashochas tvam...': since scripture declares, 'this body indeed, bereft of the living Self, dies — the living Self itself does not die,' Bhishma and the rest, though the conditioning of body and the like perishes, remain, like space, free of destruction — hence they are not to be grieved for, yet you have grieved for them, thinking 'how could I possibly slay these teachers?' and 'how could I possibly live without them?' Thus, though deluded, you nonetheless speak the words — the discourses — of the wise, those who know the Self as distinct from the body, such as 'in hell shall dwell...their ancestors fall' and the rest — yet you yourself are not truly wise. The reason for this is stated, in 'Gatasun...': the wise do not grieve for 'gatasun,' the departed bodies — indeed, they carry them out for cremation. By this it is shown that it is the life-breath alone that is truly cherished, not the body — as scripture says, 'the life-breath indeed is the father, the life-breath is the mother, the life-breath is the teacher,' and so forth. Hence, it is a matter of common knowledge, both worldly and scriptural, that a man who disregards these very persons while they still breathe is called their slayer and is cursed, while one who cremates them once their breath has departed is never spoken of in such terms. Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: While speaking learned words, you are mourning for what is not worthy of grief. Those who are wise lament neither for the living nor for the dead.

 The Lord at once took the position of the teacher and chastised the student, calling him, indirectly, a fool. The Lord said, “You are talking like a learned man, but you do not know that one who is learned – one who knows what is body and what is soul – does not lament for any stage of the body, neither in the living nor in the dead condition.” As explained in later chapters, it will be clear that knowledge means to know matter and spirit and the controller of both. Arjuna argued that religious principles should be given more importance than politics or sociology, but he did not know that knowledge of matter, soul and the Supreme is even more important than religious formularies. And because he was lacking in that knowledge, he should not have posed himself as a very learned man. As he did not happen to be a very learned man, he was consequently lamenting for something which was unworthy of lamentation. The body is born and is destined to be vanquished today or tomorrow; therefore the body is not as important as the soul. One who knows this is actually learned, and for him there is no cause for lamentation, regardless of the condition of the material body.
