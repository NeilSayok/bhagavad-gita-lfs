Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
A person endowed with self-restraint, though engaging with many objects in the course of life, harbours neither attachment nor aversion toward any of them, and thus attains peace and contentment. Mere flight from sense-objects does not bring peace, because inner disturbance does not depend on whether external objects are present or absent; its real cause is the craving to obtain what is pleasant or the urge to reject what is unpleasant. But the person of complete self-mastery and true knowledge moves about entirely free from these causes of agitation. Just as a shadow follows us wherever we go according to the play of light, yet can neither bind us through attachment nor destroy us through aversion, so too the external world of objects torments only the one who himself lends it the power to crush him. If a madman strikes his own body with a whip and cries out in pain, his suffering will end only when he lets go of the whip, or, if he keeps it, refrains from turning it upon himself. In the same way, it is the mind that projects beauty and desirability onto objects, then toils to obtain them, and so brings suffering upon itself. The teaching here is that the self-controlled person, being free of attachment and aversion, does not lend objects any power over him, and so they cannot afflict him. With self-restraint and freedom from attachment and aversion present together, the mental agitation caused by the pull of sense-objects naturally begins to subside. This agitation-free state of mind is itself called peace, or prasada. The practice of distributing prasad at the end of worship reflects this very principle: one performs worship or sacrifice with restraint and remembrance of God, and as a result experiences an extraordinary inner peace, which is truly the grace (prasada) of God. Vedantins understand purity of mind (chitta-shuddhi) as prasada; in the absence of attachment and aversion, the absence of mental agitation follows naturally, and that itself is purity of mind. What is attained once this prasada is reached? That is explained next.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
In this and the following verse, the fruit of mastering the senses is described. 'Vidheya atma' refers to one whose self is fully governed even while experiencing sense-objects — that is, one who has conquered the self (jitatma). 'Prasada' here means serenity of mind (manah-prasada).

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
One might object: if the mere memory of sense-objects can be a cause of harm, then surely their actual enjoyment is all the more so — so how does a person who partakes of objects merely to sustain life avoid falling into wrongdoing? Anticipating this doubt, the commentator restates the point to explain the purport of the following verse. The word 'now' marks the transition after the root cause of all harm has been stated. Having determined what is to be given up, he now shows the inquiry into the means of giving it up — hence 'now'. The word 'indeed' points to the fact, drawn from common experience, that activity is preceded by attachment and aversion. To exclude scripturally enjoined activity from this, it is called 'natural', used here with reference to one who is not yet a qualified seeker. This covers unavoidable acts such as eating and drinking that are necessary merely to sustain the body. If the senses' engagement with objects is described in this way, a doubt could arise whether this permits engagement even with forbidden objects for lack of restraint — this doubt is addressed by the qualification 'governed by the Self'. And since the senses, though subordinate to the inner faculty (the mind), may still escape restraint if that faculty itself is unregulated, doubt could persist about whether true control exists — this is settled by the phrase 'whose self is fully disciplined' (vidheyatma).

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Summary: A karma-yoga practitioner whose inner faculty (antahkarana) is under control, and who partakes of sense-objects through senses that are free from attachment and aversion and kept under his own restraint, attains purity of the inner faculty. Once this purity is attained, all of the practitioner's sorrows are destroyed, and the intellect of one with such a purified mind quickly becomes firmly established in the Supreme.

Explanation of key points: The word 'tu' (but) marks a contrast — the previous verse described how merely dwelling on objects with attachment leads to downfall, whereas this verse shows that engaging with objects without attachment leads to inner elevation; there, the ruin of the intellect was described, here its establishment in the Supreme.

'Vidheyatma' means the practitioner's inner faculty must remain under his own control; without mastering it, karma yoga cannot be perfected, and there remains the danger of attachment to objects and consequent downfall even while performing action. Controlling the inner faculty is essential for every practitioner, and especially so for the karma yogi.

'Atmavashyaih ragadveshavimuktaih indriyaih' — just as 'vidheyatma' refers to mastering the inner faculty, 'atmavashyaih' refers to mastering the senses. This means that in ordinary dealings the senses must remain under one's control, and for that, they must be free of attachment and aversion. Thus neither should any object be taken up with attachment nor rejected with aversion — for what matters is not so much the taking or leaving of objects, as ensuring that attachment and aversion do not arise in the senses at all. This is why, in chapter 3, the practitioner is cautioned that attachment and aversion lodge in the domain of every sense-object, and one should not fall under their control, since both are the practitioner's enemies; and in chapter 5 it is said that one who is free from such dualities as attachment and aversion attains liberation with ease.

'Vishayan charan' — a practitioner whose inner faculty is self-controlled and whose senses are free from attachment/aversion and self-mastered engages in all worldly dealings with the senses, but does not indulge in objects for the sake of enjoyment. It is engagement with objects driven by the intent to enjoy them that causes downfall; the terms 'vidheyatma' and 'atmavashyaih' here are meant precisely to negate that enjoyment-seeking attitude.

'Prasadam adhigacchati' — by engaging with objects free from attachment and aversion, the practitioner attains inner clarity and contentment (prasada) of the inner faculty. This contentment is a form of mental austerity (Gita 17.16), higher than physical or verbal austerity. Hence a practitioner should neither indulge objects out of attachment nor reject them out of aversion, since both attachment and aversion bind one to the world. If the contentment arising from sense-engagement free of attachment/aversion is not itself clung to or enjoyed, it leads on to attainment of the Supreme.

'Prasade sarva-duhkhanam hanir asyopajayate' — once purity/contentment of mind is attained, all sorrows are destroyed, because it is attachment that gives rise to distress of mind; distress gives rise to craving, and craving gives rise to all sorrow. But when attachment dissolves, contentment arises in the mind, and through that contentment all sorrows dissolve. All sorrow ultimately arises from entanglement with the body and the world (which are products of nature), and that entanglement arises from the thirst for pleasure, which itself arises from mental distress. When contentment arises, distress disappears; when distress disappears, the thirst for pleasure disappears; and when that thirst disappears, entanglement with body and world ceases, and with it all sorrow ceases. Thus contentment brings about two things: severance of worldly entanglement and steadiness of intellect in the Supreme — the same point made earlier (verse 53) through the words 'unwavering amid the world' and 'unmoving in the Supreme'.

The phrase 'destruction of all sorrows' does not mean that painful circumstances will never arise for such a person — according to one's past actions, painful events may still occur — but rather that no distortion such as sorrow or agitation can arise within his inner faculty because of them.

'Prasannachetaso hy ashu buddhih paryavatishthate' — the intellect of one whose mind is contented and clear quickly becomes established in the Supreme, meaning the practitioner himself becomes established in the Supreme, with not the slightest doubt remaining in his intellect.

A further subtle point: whether it is contentment or intense longing directed toward God, if either becomes sufficiently strong it swiftly leads to attainment of the Supreme — as when the Gopis, prevented by parents, brothers, and husbands from going to Krishna and locked indoors, felt such intense longing to meet Him that their sins were destroyed, and the joy they felt in remembering Him destroyed their merits too; free of both sin and merit, they left their bodies then and there and were the first to reach Him. But contentment or distress arising from worldly objects only deepens the impressions of enjoyment, i.e., strengthens worldly bondage — as seen in ordinary beings who remain entangled in the world through such contentment and distress. In both intense contentment and intense longing the inner faculty becomes 'molten,' and just as color poured into molten wax becomes permanently fixed in it, so whatever disposition — divine or worldly — arises when the inner faculty is thus molten becomes fixed, and that fixed disposition then either elevates or degrades the practitioner. It is therefore proper for the practitioner neither to feel elated on obtaining even the most cherished worldly thing, nor to feel agitated on encountering even the most unwanted thing. (This continues the theme of the previous two verses, restated here in reverse/negative form in the two verses that follow.)

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
The purport of this pair of verses concerns the fruit of conquering the senses. Given that attachment and aversion have been abandoned as described, what follows from this? The fruit of conquest of the senses is now stated: it results in knowledge, as was said earlier ('one who has mastered the senses...'). One might ask whether this is the direct fruit of sense-conquest or an indirect one; the text explains how conquest of the senses leads to knowledge. Just as freedom from attachment and aversion was described as leading to engagement with sense-objects being a means to conquering the senses, so too, to prevent the false idea that abstaining from food and drink altogether is what constitutes sense-conquest (which would be impossible), the verse implicitly accepts that experiencing only those objects necessary for maintaining the body causes no fault; this alone is what the phrase 'one whose self is disciplined' conveys, since no separate word for 'conquest of the senses' occurs here. By these three elements — freedom from attachment/aversion, restrained enjoyment of necessary objects, and self-discipline — the fruit of abandoning attachment and aversion, namely conquest of the senses, is expressed. Although this reads like mere repetition, since the point had not yet been established, it must be understood as continuing the sense of the sentence: one who has thus abandoned attachment and aversion, using senses not driven by attachment and aversion but by the sheer necessity of maintaining the body, becomes a person whose self is disciplined, and consequently his outer senses also come under his control. For this reason a separate independent purport for the two half-verses was not stated. Since this answers the second inquiry, it is shown that conquest of the senses yields knowledge as an indirect fruit, while the direct fruit stated is that the disciplined self attains prasada (serenity). Since prasada might appear to be a property of the self, the text clarifies that it is instead what will later be called 'a mind that has become serene'.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Objection: since the senses are by nature inclined toward their objects, it is impossible to restrain them, so this flaw seems unavoidable — how then can wisdom be said to be firmly established? Anticipating this doubt, the commentator answers with these two verses. One who is self-controlled, and who, even while making use of sense-objects through senses free from attachment and aversion, attains prasada (tranquility) — such a person of serene mind should be understood to have wisdom that is firmly established.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Someone may say that if the mind alone is restrained, there is no fault even without restraining the outer senses; anticipating the question 'then what should one do?', the commentator answers across the next eight verses. One whose mind is not composed, even after restraining the outer senses, still dwells upon objects with a mind corrupted by attachment and aversion, and thereby falls from the true goal of life. But one whose self is disciplined — here 'tu' marks a contrast with the previous case — has a mind that is mastered, and therefore engages, through senses free from attachment and aversion, with unforbidden objects such as sound merely as they present themselves, and thereby attains prasada, meaning the serenity and clarity of mind that fits one for direct realization of the Supreme Self. Senses driven by attachment and aversion become causes of fault, but when the mind is under one's own control, attachment and aversion do not arise; and in their absence, the senses do not act under their influence. Contact with objects that is unavoidable for sustaining the body does not, in that case, cause any fault or obstruct purity of mind. This also answers the earlier doubt that even the memory of objects can cause harm, so how much more their actual use for sustaining life — and it answers the question of what one should do, namely that one attains objects only through senses that are under one's own control.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Objection: since the senses are by nature inclined toward objects, it is impossible to oppose them, so this flaw seems unavoidable — how then can one be said to have steady wisdom? Anticipating this doubt, the commentator answers across these two verses. Through senses free from attachment and aversion and devoid of pride, one attains prasada, or peace, even while making use of sense-objects. Freedom from attachment and aversion itself is explained by the phrase describing one whose mind is under the control of the Self, that is, one whose mind is obedient and governed. This itself answers the fourth question — 'how should one move about, how should one partake of things?' — namely, that such a person attains objects only through senses that remain under his own control.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Having thus explained that dwelling upon sense-objects is the means to ruin, the commentator now states that a mind free from meditation upon objects and under one's own control is the means to the highest human goal, answering the question 'what should one do?'. One whose self is disciplined, that is, whose mind is self-controlled, and therefore whose senses are under his own control, and therefore free from attachment and aversion — the natural causes that drive the senses toward their objects — engages with objects that are merely necessary for sustaining life, such as food and clothing, without craving them, and thereby attains prasada, that is, serenity and well-being.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
This verse answers the question about one established in samadhi. The word 'tu' (but) sets this apart from what was described before. 'Vidheyatma' means one whose governed and obedient self is the Lord Himself; such a person, through senses that are free from attachment and aversion, self-controlled, and yet ever subordinate to the Lord's will, makes use of sense-objects that come to him only by the Lord's will, and thereby attains prasada, the grace of the Lord. The sense is this: the devotee, recognising that the enjoyments he receives — given so that he may taste the divine essence of his own true nature, by the Lord's own wish — are bestowed by those who long for that divine essence and are themselves given for imparting it, partakes of them only to the extent needed to fulfil his purpose; and the Lord, seeing this, bestows His grace upon him. This very idea is stated in the Bhagavatam (11.14.18), from 'though assailed by the senses' up to 'is not overcome by sense-objects'.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Objection: even for a yogi who does not dwell upon sense-objects, attachment of the senses to their objects is, by their very nature, unavoidable when the senses become active, and so, by the reasoning given earlier, even such a yogi would be liable to ruin. Anticipating this doubt, the commentator answers. One whose self is disciplined — whose mind has been made wholly subservient — moves among objects, seeing them, through senses that are themselves under the control of that governed mind and hence free, like the mind's master, from desire and anger; he regards objects with the same indifference one has toward a blade of grass lying on the road. Because no craving arises in him from this contact, he attains prasada — the cleansing of the mind's coating of resolve and doubt, that is, the mind's own clarity. This clarity of mind is itself the clarity of the inner Self, since the Self's nature is essentially reflected through the mind's quality. For one who has conquered the mind, contact with objects does not afflict him as it would afflict one whose mind is unconquered; therefore conquest of the mind must always be undertaken.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
But a person free from all attachment and aversion and able to control his senses through regulative principles of freedom can obtain the complete mercy of the Lord.

 It is already explained that one may externally control the senses by some artificial process, but unless the senses are engaged in the transcendental service of the Lord, there is every chance of a fall. Although the person in full Kṛṣṇa consciousness may apparently be on the sensual plane, because of his being Kṛṣṇa conscious he has no attachment to sensual activities. The Kṛṣṇa conscious person is concerned only with the satisfaction of Kṛṣṇa, and nothing else. Therefore he is transcendental to all attachment and detachment. If Kṛṣṇa wants, the devotee can do anything which is ordinarily undesirable; and if Kṛṣṇa does not want, he shall not do that which he would have ordinarily done for his own satisfaction. Therefore to act or not to act is within his control because he acts only under the direction of Kṛṣṇa. This consciousness is the causeless mercy of the Lord, which the devotee can achieve in spite of his being attached to the sensual platform.
