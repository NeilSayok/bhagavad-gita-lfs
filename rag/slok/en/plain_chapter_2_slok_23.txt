Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
An unseen thing can always be explained only through seen things. By mere definition alone, that thing would remain unknown. Here too, Lord Krishna describes the unmodified, eternal Self-principle to Arjuna and to us through the familiar, ever-changing world of modification. It is well known that objects can be destroyed by weapons and the like, or by nature's agents of destruction -- fire, water, and wind. By none of these means can the Self be destroyed.

'Weapons cannot cut it' -- it is well known that an axe can cut a tree and the like, but it cannot in any way injure water, fire, wind, or space. The principle is that a gross instrument cannot destroy something subtler than itself. Hence it naturally follows that the Self, which is subtler than even the subtlest element, space, cannot be destroyed by weapons.

'Fire cannot burn it' -- fire can burn things other than itself, but it can never burn itself. Burning is fire's own nature, and it cannot destroy its own nature, its own true form. It is worth reflecting that fire, dwelling within space, can burn objects located in space, but never space itself. How much more powerless, then, would it be in attempting to burn the Self, which is subtler even than space?

'Water cannot wet it' -- by the very same principle stated above, we can also understand that water cannot moisten or drench the Self, nor can it drown it. Both of these are possible only for some substantial, form-possessing thing, and not for the all-pervading, formless Self-principle.

'Wind cannot dry it' -- only that which is wet can be dried by evaporating its moisture. Nowadays many means exist for preserving vegetables and foodstuffs by drying out their water content. But in the Self-principle there is no element of water at all, since it is of non-dual nature; hence there remains no possibility whatsoever of its destruction through drying by wind.

Beyond the simple meaning of these words, this verse also carries a deeper meaning, which Lord Krishna clarifies further in the next verse -- why and how the Self-principle is eternal.

In what way is this Self eternal? Why and how can it be recognized through its eternal nature?

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
Although the Self is, of itself and generally, indestructible, one might yet suspect that it could be destroyed by some particular cause, as wood is by an axe -- to this doubt he responds by denying precisely such particular causes, in 'na enam...' By denying it in the present tense, it is implied that the same will hold in the future too, he says, in 'achchhedyah...' Why this unfitness for destruction? Because it shares the nature of the Lord, marked by qualifiers such as eternal, all-pervading and the rest. The word 'shashvatah' expresses mere uniformity alone. The word 'sthanu' (immovable) also rules out even incidental change.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
The doubt is raised that the Self's unmodifiability remains unproven, since it would be subject to modification produced by the four elements beginning with earth, in 'kasmat...' He answers that, since the elements are incapable of making the Self their object, its unmodifiability, like that of space, is entirely fitting, in 'aha...'

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Weapons cannot cut this embodied one, fire cannot burn it, water cannot wet it, and wind cannot dry it.

Explanation -- 'Nainam chindanti shastrani' -- weapons cannot cut this embodied one, because these material weapons cannot even reach it.

All weapons whatsoever arise from the earth-element. This earth-element cannot produce any kind of modification in this embodied one at all. More than that, the earth-element cannot even reach this embodied one -- let alone the question of modifying it!

'Nainam dahati pavakah' -- fire cannot burn this embodied one, because fire cannot even reach it. When it cannot even reach it, how could burning by it be possible at all? The sense is that the fire-element can never produce any kind of modification whatsoever in this embodied one.

'Na chainam kledayanty apah' -- water cannot wet it, because water cannot even reach it. The sense is that the water-element cannot produce any modification in this embodied one...

'Na shoshayati marutah' -- wind cannot dry it, that is, wind has no power to dry this embodied one, because wind cannot even reach it. The sense is that the wind-element can never produce any kind of modification in this embodied one.

Earth, water, fire, wind and space are called the five great elements. The Lord speaks here of only four of these elements -- that earth, water, fire and wind cannot produce any modification in this embodied one -- but makes no mention at all of the fifth element, space. The reason for this is that space has no power to perform any action at all. The power to act, that is, to produce modification, belongs only to these four elements; space merely provides room for all of them.

Earth, water, fire and wind -- these four elements arise from space itself, yet they cannot produce any modification even in space, their own originating cause -- that is, earth cannot cut space, water cannot wet it, fire cannot burn it, and wind cannot dry it. Since these four elements cannot harm even space, their own cause, nor the great principle (Mahat-tattva) that is the cause of space, nor Prakriti, the cause of the Mahat-tattva, how then could they possibly reach this embodied one, which is utterly beyond Prakriti altogether? How could these qualified substances possibly reach that unqualified (nirguna) principle? They cannot (Gita 13.31).

The embodied one is an eternal principle. It is from this very principle that earth and the other three elements derive their existence and vitality. How, then, could these elements, from which very source they derive their existence, possibly modify it? This embodied one is all-pervading, and earth and the other three elements are pervaded by it -- that is, they exist within it. How, then, could a pervaded thing possibly harm its own pervader? Harming it is simply not possible.

Here the context is war. Arjuna is grieving over the thought, 'all these kinsmen will die.' Hence the Lord says: how could they possibly die? Because the action of weapons and missiles cannot reach that far at all -- that is, even when the body is cut by a weapon, the embodied one is not cut; even when the body is burned by a fire-missile, the embodied one is not burned; even when the body is dissolved by a water-missile, the embodied one is not dissolved; and even when the body is dried by a wind-missile, the embodied one is not dried. The sense is that even when the body dies by weapons and missiles, the embodied one does not die, but remains exactly as it is, unmodified. Hence to grieve on this account is utter foolishness on your part.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Weapons, fire, water and air are incapable of cleaving, burning, wetting and drying the self; for the nature of the self is to pervade all elements; it is present everywhere; for it is subtler than all the elements, and hence not capable of being pervaded by them; and cleaving, burning, wetting and drying are actions that can occur only by pervading a substance. Therefore the self is eternal. It is stable, immovable and primeval. The meaning is that it is unchanging, unshakable and ancient.

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Why does It verily remain unchanged? This is being answered in, 'Weapons do not cut It,' etc. Sastrani, weapons; na, do not; chindanti, cut; enam, It, the embodied one under discussion. It being partless, weapons like sword etc. do not cut off Its limbs. So also, even pavakah, fire; na dahati enam, does not burn, does not reduce It to ashes. Ca, and similarly; apah, water; na enam kledayanti, does not moisten It. For water has the power of disintegrating a substance that has parts, by the process of moistening it. That is not possible in the case of the partless Self. Similarly, air destroys an oil substance by drying up the oil. Even marutah, air; na sosayati, does not dry; (enam, It,) one's own Self. [Ast. reads 'enam tu atmanam, but this Self', in place of enam svatmanam.-Tr.]

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Since incidental destruction was already denied earlier, what purpose does the verse 'na enam...' serve? To dispel this very doubt, he states the purport, in 'svatah...' 'Svatah' means by time itself. 'Prayah' (generally) is an indeclinable used to exclude suspected special causes -- the remaining sense being that destruction might occur through some particular cause such as a weapon. Since this doubt is not fully resolved, he gives an illustration, in 'like the cutting of the saw and the rest': just as the beheading of Daksha Prajapati did not occur of itself, nor through the weapon wielded by Virabhadra as an ordinary cause, but rather through the special cause termed 'conceiving him as a sacrificial animal,' so too this could occur here -- this is the sense. It is owing to precisely this doubt that particular causes are here addressed.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Again, to make the indestructibility of this Self readily comprehensible, scripture is cited, in 'na enam...,' spanning two and a half verses. By this, indestructibility with respect to earth, water, fire and air is established.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
One might ask: when the body is destroyed, why is the Self residing within it not destroyed too, as a man within a burning house is destroyed? He answers: even the sharpest weapons cannot divide this present Self into parts, cutting it in two. Even the most blazing fire cannot reduce it to ashes. Even the swiftest water cannot moisten it so as to separate its parts. Even the strongest wind cannot render it dry, that is, sapless. Since, at the time of war, the fear of total destruction arises, and since it is weapons and the like that are relevant to war, only earth, water, fire and air are mentioned here, not space, since only these four are well known to be destructive.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Showing the absence of any means of slaying, as already stated in 'how does he slay,' he clarifies the Self's indestructibility, in 'na enam...': water does not moisten it, that is, does not soften and loosen it.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
He again states its unmodifiability in another way, in 'na enam...' And 'kledayanti' means: does not separate its parts. The reason for this is its being without parts.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Avoiding the redundancy with what was said before, by a difference of purpose -- namely, firmness and ease of comprehension -- he brings down these two verses beginning 'na enam...,' in 'punarapi...' In this verse and a half, beginning 'na enam...,' the incapacity of the instrument and the unfitness of the object, though stated separately for the sake of clarity, are, being mutually correlative, subsumed one within the other elsewhere, and hence not taken up separately in the commentary. He explains the word 'sarvagata' (all-pervading) as a restatement of the reason already given, in 'sarvagatatvat atmanah.' On the doubt as to how the atomic Self can be all-pervading, he answers, in 'sarva-tattva...' Here, the word 'sarvagata' does not mean the all-pervasiveness of the individual soul, which would contradict the many scriptural texts, but rather its special capacity to enter into all things -- this is the sense of using the word 'svabhava' (nature).

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Hence, even in removing the body that must be given up, since this Self is possessed of the attributes of indestructibility and the rest, weapons and the like cannot cut it, he says: 'nainam chindanti...' Weapons do not cut it, because it has no solidity. Fire does not burn it, because it lacks the property of dryness. Water does not moisten it, does not loosen it, because it lacks hardness and the like. Wind does not dry it, because it lacks fluidity -- this is the sense. Hence, even under the assault of weapons and the like, nothing whatsoever will happen to it. This too is a form of My own play; hence war and the like should be undertaken solely for My pleasure -- this is the sense. That all these act as they do is due to My will alone -- this is the sense, since it is the Lord's will alone that empowers everyone to perform their own respective duties. Hence it is said in the Shrimad Bhagavatam (3.25.42): 'it is from fear of Me that this wind blows,' and so on.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
What kind of embodied one is this? He answers, in 'na enam...' Weapons do not cut it, do not divide it into two parts, since it is not gross. One might then suspect that, like an earthly atom, it might be the substrate of forms produced by heat and the like; to this he answers, in 'nainam dahati pavakah' -- since it is not an atom in that sense. And water does not moisten it, since it is without touch; for water can moisten only a substance possessing tangibility, not one devoid of it. Wind does not dry it, since it is devoid of oiliness. By this, the qualities well known from scripture -- 'without length, without bulk, without atomicity, without sound, without touch, without form, imperishable, and likewise without taste, eternal, without smell' -- should also be understood as summarized here.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
The soul can never be cut to pieces by any weapon, nor burned by fire, nor moistened by water, nor withered by the wind.

 All kinds of weapons – swords, flame weapons, rain weapons, tornado weapons, etc. – are unable to kill the spirit soul. It appears that there were many kinds of weapons made of earth, water, air, ether, etc., in addition to the modern weapons of fire. Even the nuclear weapons of the modern age are classified as fire weapons, but formerly there were other weapons made of all different types of material elements. Fire weapons were counteracted by water weapons, which are now unknown to modern science. Nor do modern scientists have knowledge of tornado weapons. Nonetheless, the soul can never be cut into pieces, nor annihilated by any number of weapons, regardless of scientific devices. The Māyāvādī cannot explain how the individual soul came into existence simply by ignorance and consequently became covered by the illusory energy. Nor was it ever possible to cut the individual souls from the original Supreme Soul; rather, the individual souls are eternally separated parts of the Supreme Soul. Because they are atomic individual souls eternally ( sanātana ), they are prone to be covered by the illusory energy, and thus they become separated from the association of the Supreme Lord, just as the sparks of a fire, although one in quality with the fire, are prone to be extinguished when out of the fire. In the Varāha Purāṇa, the living entities are described as separated parts and parcels of the Supreme. They are eternally so, according to the Bhagavad-gītā also. So, even after being liberated from illusion, the living entity remains a separate identity, as is evident from the teachings of the Lord to Arjuna. Arjuna became liberated by the knowledge received from Kṛṣṇa, but he never became one with Kṛṣṇa.
