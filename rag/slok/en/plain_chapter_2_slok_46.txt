Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The beautiful simile of the reservoir given here is most apt for the context. In the scorching heat of summer, when rivers dry up, people are forced to draw water from some nearby well. Though a source of water lies beneath their very feet within the earth, it remains unavailable for use. But when, in the rainy season, floods rise everywhere in the rivers, these small reservoirs merge into the flood and lose both their separate existence and their purpose.

In the same way, so long as a man fails to recognize his own blissful nature, he keeps searching for happiness among sense-objects out of delusion. At that stage the Vedas -- that is, their ritualistic portion (karma-kanda) -- appear extremely useful to him, since they prescribe numerous means of attaining pleasures such as those of heaven. But once a sincere seeker directly realizes, through the Upanishads, the blissful Self, ritualistic action ceases to hold any purpose for him; all the small pleasures born of enjoyment become absorbed into his own blissful nature.

This does not mean that Vyasa is here condemning the ritualistic portion of the Vedas. Rather, Krishna is admonishing those unwise people who mistake the means for the end and hope to attain the Infinite through impermanent actions. When actions are performed without attachment to their fruit, a person's character is purified, and to one of such a pure heart, the experience of the boundless, infinite Self comes easily; thereafter he feels no attraction for transient pleasures.

The Vedas serve to make us aware of our own pure, conscious nature. As long as the ego born of ignorance persists, study of the Vedas remains indispensable. But once Self-knowledge dawns, the authority of the Vedas is itself established through that realized person -- just as one who has mastered the highest branch of mathematics no longer needs to recite the multiplication tables, since that elementary knowledge is already contained within his complete understanding. Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
Still, since one might think that the results attained by those who perform action for desired fruits are equal to those of the wise (because the wise gain no separate fruit), the verse beginning 'yavan artha' is spoken to address this. Just as whatever use a well has is included within the water when it is flooded everywhere, so too whatever result is obtained from all the Vedas is included within the result attained by the wise Brahmana who has direct knowledge. One who realizes Brahman is called a Brahmana, one possessed of immediate knowledge; he indeed attains Brahman. The word 'vijanatah' (the knowing one) shows that this is the fruit of knowledge.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Thinking that, since there is no desire for fruit even while performing one's own duty with the intention of dedicating it to the Lord, the path of Yoga (action) would therefore be fruitless, an objection is raised beginning 'sarveshu'. In reply, it is asserted that the path of action does indeed bear fruit, explained beginning 'uchyate'. To show what that fruit is, the verse beginning 'shrnu' (listen) is introduced.

Just as whatever benefit -- such as bathing or sipping -- arises from a small quantity of water in a well or the like becomes included within the vast, unbounded, flooding waters of the ocean, because a limited quantity of water is itself a part of the unlimited quantity, so too whatever specific pleasure, tinged with a particular object, arises from the performance of all the actions enjoined by the Vedas becomes included within the very bliss of the knower of the Self -- since limited joys are understood to be included within unlimited bliss, as declared by the shruti, 'other beings live on a fraction of this very bliss.' Thus, since the path of Yoga culminates in the attainment of the unlimited bliss of the Self, it cannot be said to be fruitless -- this is expressed by the verse beginning 'yavan'.

The word 'udapana' (reservoir), by its derivation ('that in which water is drunk'), refers to a limited body of water such as a well, indicating application to a limited object. Since it may be doubted how something limited to a well can be included in the ocean, it is explained that the word 'artha' here refers to a specific purpose or use, not a limited quantity of water itself -- just as the space enclosed in a pot is included within the great sky, once the greater is attained the smaller need no longer be sought separately.

Having thus explained the first half of the verse by way of illustration, the second half -- the principle illustrated -- is then explained: whatever result arises from Vedic actions becomes included, for the knowing Brahmana, in the very fruit of knowledge, as stated in the shruti: 'Whatever good deeds people perform, all of that he attains who knows that which Raikva knows.' Though this shruti text concerns meditation on the qualified Brahman, to remove the doubt that the present verse deals only with that, it is clarified again from the Gita itself that the same conclusion holds even for knowledge of the unqualified, absolute Brahman. Hence there is no contradiction in the fruits of action being included in the fruit of knowledge.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Just as a person has no real need for small pits of water when a vast reservoir, full on every side, is available, so a knower of Brahman, who has realized the truth of the Vedas and scriptures, has, in effect, no real need for anything from the Vedas either.

Explanation: 'Yavan artha udapane sarvatah sampluto-dake' -- When a vast lake, entirely full, clean and pure, becomes available, a person no longer has any use for small ponds. This is because if one washes one's hands and feet in a small pond, the water becomes muddied and is no longer fit for bathing; and if one bathes in it, it is no longer fit for washing clothes; and if clothes are washed in it, it is no longer fit for drinking. But when a great lake is obtained, no matter what is done in it, its purity, cleanliness and sanctity remain unaffected.

'Tavan sarveshu vedeshu brahmanasya vijanatah' -- In the same way, for great souls who have attained the Supreme Reality, all the meritorious acts prescribed in the Vedas -- sacrifice, charity, austerity, pilgrimage, vows and so on -- hold no real significance for them; these meritorious acts become, for them, like small ponds. A similar illustration is given later in verse seventy, where the man of knowledge is compared to the ocean in its depth and steadiness: however many pleasures come before him, they can produce no disturbance in him.

One who knows the Supreme Reality, and who also knows the essence of the Vedas and scriptures, is referred to here by the words 'brahmanasya vijanatah'.

The word 'tavan' implies that upon attaining the Supreme Reality, a person becomes free from the three gunas. He becomes free of dualities such as attachment and aversion, becomes established in the eternal Truth, and becomes free from the anxiety of acquisition and preservation. He remains ever devoted to the Supreme.

Connection: Having in verse thirty-nine instructed Arjuna to hear of this equanimity (samabuddhi), the Lord now, in the following verse, instructs him to perform action in order to attain that equanimity.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
As to what use there is in stating the fruit of knowledge in the context of teaching Yoga, this is explained beginning 'tathapi'. In the passage beginning 'yam imam' (2.43), those who perform action for desired results were condemned, and in 'nistraigunyo bhava' (2.45) their abandonment was enjoined. Here one may ask, on what basis is this said? For it was stated that those who act with desire, lacking concentration, also lack knowledge, and hence cannot attain liberation. To this it is replied: although the fruit of knowledge does not belong to those who act with desire, their condemnation would still not be justified merely because the heavenly fruit gained by desire-prompted action is not attained by the wise -- for that would only imply an equality between action and knowledge, raising an objection against the necessity of practicing Yoga. This is answered through the present verse.

Just as whatever use arises from bathing, sipping and so on in a limited body of water such as a well is included within the unlimited water of the ocean when it floods -- because the limited waters are themselves portions of the unlimited -- so too whatever specific pleasure arises from the various actions enjoined by the Vedas is included, for the knower of the Self, within the bliss which is his very own nature, since limited pleasures are accepted as included within unlimited bliss. The word 'brahmana' here denotes one possessed of direct realization, not merely indirect knowledge; hence the further qualification 'vijanatah' (the knowing one) is not redundant, since it specifically denotes the fruit of indirect knowledge culminating in direct realization.

Although there is, properly speaking, no comparability of fruit between the knower and the man of action, the fruit of the knower is like the water of a vast ocean because of its greatness, whereas the fruit of the man of action is like the water of a small well, being extremely limited. Hence there is no true equality between the two; and it is therefore fitting that the Lord condemns the small and impermanent fruit of action so as to direct Arjuna towards Yoga, the means to the great and endless fruit of knowledge.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
It is not that everything prescribed by the Vedas -- pertaining both to the qualified and to the unqualified Brahman -- is to be adopted all at once; rather, only as much as is needed, as illustrated here beginning 'yavan artha'. Just as, in a reservoir devised for every purpose and flooded with water on all sides, a thirsty person takes only as much water as is needed for his purpose, and not all of it, so too, of all that is contained in the Vedas, the seeker who is qualified for Vedic study and who discerningly knows their purpose -- the yogin -- should adopt only that which serves as the means to the perfection of the Self, and not everything.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
One need not fear that by renouncing all desire while performing action, one would be deprived of the various joys born of action. For just as whatever purpose -- bathing, drinking and so on -- is served by a small reservoir of water is served all the more fully when a vast body of water is obtained (just as mountain streams, flowing from every side, converge in a valley, and the purpose served individually by each stream is served, all the more completely, by their combined waters, since all the streams become included within that single lake) -- so too, whatever purpose, up to and including the bliss of Brahma (Hiranyagarbha), is served by all the desire-prompted actions enjoined in the Vedas, is fully served for the Brahmana who directly realizes the truth of Brahman and aspires for Brahman itself -- because the small joys are but portions of the bliss of Brahman and are therefore included within it, as declared by the shruti: 'other beings live on a mere fraction of this very bliss.' Though bliss is one, it appears divided into parts, like the seeming division of space by the limiting adjunct of a pot, because of the various limiting conditions superimposed upon it by ignorance. Thus, when the mind is purified through desireless action and Self-knowledge dawns, one attains the bliss of the Supreme Brahman, and having attained all bliss through that alone, there remains no need to strive separately for the small joys. Hence the import is: perform desireless actions for the sake of that knowledge which grants the highest bliss.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
One might doubt that a resolute intellect (vyavasayatmika buddhi) directed toward worshipping the Lord without desire, by abandoning the manifold fruits declared in the Vedas, would itself be a mistaken intellect. This doubt is answered here. 'Udapana' refers to a well, tank or similar body of limited water in which water is drunk; since the whole purpose cannot be served at once from such a small body of water, one must move about it to obtain, bit by bit, whatever purpose -- bathing, drinking, etc. -- it can serve; but that same purpose, taken in its entirety, is served at once in a great flooded lake. In the same way, whatever fruit consisting of the various results of action is contained in all the Vedas belongs, in its entirety, to the Brahmana who is established in Brahman and possessed of a resolute intellect -- because the small joys are included within the bliss of Brahman, as declared by the shruti, 'other beings live on a mere fraction of this very bliss.' Therefore this very intellect, of one who acts without desire, is indeed the true intellect.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
If the fruits declared for Vedic actions are not to be desired, one may ask, for what purpose then should action even be performed for the sake of the Lord? Anticipating this doubt, it is explained that for one who acts with desire for fruit, action carries the possibility of manifold undesirable results; but for one free from any craving for fruit, through the attainment of knowledge all the fruits of action become included within the bliss of Brahman, and thereby all undesirable results also cease. Thus: just as whatever purpose -- washing the hands, bathing, drinking, and so on -- is served, in limited measure, by a small reservoir such as a well, is fully served, in that same measure, within a completely full, flooded body of water, the purpose being included within it -- so too, whatever fruit is obtained from the actions enjoined by the Vedas is obtained, in that same measure, by the Brahmana who has realized the Supreme Reality, since all fruits are included in Brahman, the fruit of knowledge that is comparable to the vast flooded water, as declared by the shruti, 'other beings live on a mere fraction of this very bliss.' The specific mention of 'Brahmana' indicates that the Brahmana has the primary eligibility for the knowledge of Brahman.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
If Vedic rites are not performed, one may doubt how their fruit could still be attained; this is answered here. 'Udapana' means a vessel or reservoir in which water is drunk; whatever purpose is served there is also served, though with greater trouble of carrying and guarding the water, in a large flooded tank. Similarly, whatever fruit of Vedic action exists in the Vedas belongs likewise to the Brahmana who is established solely in Brahman and who knows the true nature of Brahman. This is not in conflict with the shruti; indeed the shruti itself declares: 'The knower of the bliss of Brahman... having known That alone, one goes beyond death.'

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
One might object that the realization of the Self arises only when the mind is purified, and that such purity is attainable only through the performance of all the actions enjoined by the Vedas -- making freedom from the three gunas difficult to attain. This is answered here. Just as, when a person obtains a vast reservoir of water flooded on every side, the purpose he actually needs -- such as bathing or drinking, achievable with a single pot of water -- does not require the expenditure of the entire reservoir, so too, for the discerning Brahmana who aspires to know Brahman, the purpose achievable merely by hearing a single portion of the Vedas, namely the Upanishads, is sufficient; he has no need to perform the actions enjoined by the whole of the Vedas in order to attain that end -- since it is not possible, within a single lifetime, to perform all the actions enjoined by the entire Veda. Whether in this life or in a future one, once the mind is purified through repetition (japa) and the like, freedom from the three gunas becomes possible through hearing the Upanishads -- this is the purport. Some elder commentators, however, explain it differently: for a person who has attained Self-knowledge -- comparable to the vast flooded reservoir -- the entire fruit obtainable from the many well-like actions enjoined by the Vedas is obtained in full, since the small joys are included within the bliss of Brahman; the shruti likewise shows that all the fruits of action are included in knowledge, as it says, 'Whatever good deeds people perform, all of that he attains who knows that which Raikva knows,' and it will also be said later, 'All action, in its entirety, culminates in knowledge.' On this view, before the dawning of knowledge comparable to the Ganga, well-like actions must indeed be performed.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
All purposes served by a small well can at once be served by a great reservoir of water. Similarly, all the purposes of the Vedas can be served to one who knows the purpose behind them.

 The rituals and sacrifices mentioned in the karma-kāṇḍa division of the Vedic literature are meant to encourage gradual development of self-realization. And the purpose of self-realization is clearly stated in the Fifteenth Chapter of the Bhagavad-gītā (15.15) : the purpose of studying the Vedas is to know Lord Kṛṣṇa, the primeval cause of everything. So, self-realization means understanding Kṛṣṇa and one’s eternal relationship with Him. The relationship of the living entities with Kṛṣṇa is also mentioned in the Fifteenth Chapter of Bhagavad-gītā (15.7) . The living entities are parts and parcels of Kṛṣṇa; therefore, revival of Kṛṣṇa consciousness by the individual living entity is the highest perfectional stage of Vedic knowledge. This is confirmed in the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam (3.33.7) as follows: aho bata śva-paco ’to garīyān yaj-jihvāgre vartate nāma tubhyam tepus tapas te juhuvuḥ sasnur āryā brahmānūcur nāma gṛṇanti ye te “O my Lord, a person who is chanting Your holy name, although born of a low family like that of a caṇḍāla [dog-eater], is situated on the highest platform of self-realization. Such a person must have performed all kinds of penances and sacrifices according to Vedic rituals and studied the Vedic literatures many, many times after taking his bath in all the holy places of pilgrimage. Such a person is considered to be the best of the Āryan family.” So one must be intelligent enough to understand the purpose of the Vedas, without being attached to the rituals only, and must not desire to be elevated to the heavenly kingdoms for a better quality of sense gratification. It is not possible for the common man in this age to follow all the rules and regulations of the Vedic rituals, nor is it possible to study all of the Vedānta and the Upaniṣads thoroughly. It requires much time, energy, knowledge and resources to execute the purposes of the Vedas. This is hardly possible in this age. The best purpose of Vedic culture is served, however, by chanting the holy name of the Lord, as recommended by Lord Caitanya, the deliverer of all fallen souls. When Lord Caitanya was asked by a great Vedic scholar, Prakāśānanda Sarasvatī, why He, the Lord, was chanting the holy name of the Lord like a sentimentalist instead of studying Vedānta philosophy, the Lord replied that His spiritual master had found Him to be a great fool and thus asked Him to chant the holy name of Lord Kṛṣṇa. He did so, and became ecstatic like a madman. In this Age of Kali, most of the population is foolish and not adequately educated to understand Vedānta philosophy; the best purpose of Vedānta philosophy is served by inoffensively chanting the holy name of the Lord. Vedānta is the last word in Vedic wisdom, and the author and knower of the Vedānta philosophy is Lord Kṛṣṇa; and the highest Vedāntist is the great soul who takes pleasure in chanting the holy name of the Lord. That is the ultimate purpose of all Vedic mysticism.
