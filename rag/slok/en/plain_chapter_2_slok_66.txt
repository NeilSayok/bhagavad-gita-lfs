Life Application:
Set aside quiet time for daily reflection, understanding that lasting happiness requires a peaceful, disciplined mind.

The unsteady (undisciplined) person has no knowledge of the Self, and the unsteady one lacks the capacity for contemplation and meditation. One without such contemplation attains no peace; and for the person without peace, where is happiness?

Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
This verse explains why the scriptures place such emphasis on peace of mind. In the absence of mental peace, the intellect lacks the capacity for the reflection necessary for cultural and spiritual growth. Without peace there is no intellectual readiness to understand life's problems, and hence no opportunity to properly evaluate life and gain Self-knowledge or practise meditation. Just as a ship without a guiding pole-star wanders aimlessly on the ocean and is eventually wrecked upon some great rock, a person without a great goal in life drifts without direction. Such a directionless person never finds peace, and for one who is not peaceful, where is happiness? To remain composed amid the calm and turbulent waves of life's ocean, in both pleasure and pain, the goal of the Supreme must never be lost from sight -- just as a dancer's feet cannot keep rhythm and movement in control without a drummer. [The next verse explains] why the unsteady (undisciplined) person lacks this knowledge.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
In the next two verses the Lord describes the fault that arises from the absence of grace (prasada). Without grace there is no yukti -- no restraint of the mind. The unsteady person has no correct knowledge (samyag-jnana). This is further explained by 'na cayuktasya...'. Here 'santi' (peace) means liberation (mukti), since it is said that 'santi is moksa, that is, nirvana.'

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
[A sub-commentary on Sankara's bhasya.] The question is raised whether the intellect described earlier could arise merely through purity of mind without concentration; the answer is that even in an unconcentrated mind only a semblance of understanding appears, not true knowledge of the Self, since a distracted mind cannot give rise to genuine insight into the Self's nature. When knowledge of the Self arises merely verbally from hearing, 'bhavana' means the earnest, sustained effort of remembrance directed toward direct realization, which cannot succeed for one whose mind is distracted. Even if realization does not occur through such contemplation, there is still a loss -- because without a continuous current of remembrance following the initial hearing, one fails to remove the root of worldly trouble for lack of direct knowledge; this failure is the lack of 'upasama' (inner peace). For one whose trouble is not removed, cut off from the ocean of supreme bliss and immersed instead in the ocean of transmigration, no happiness can arise. One might object that sensory enjoyment could still bring such a person happiness, but the text clarifies that true happiness is only the cessation of the senses' thirst for objects -- enjoyment driven by that same thirst is nothing but suffering, as confirmed by both scripture and direct experience.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
A person whose mind and senses are not restrained does not possess a resolute, decisive intellect, and without such an intellect there is no sense of devotion to duty. Lacking that sense of duty, he cannot attain peace; and without peace, how can such a person find happiness?

Explanation: This concerns the practice of Karma Yoga, in which restraining the mind and senses is essential. 'Nasti buddhir ayuktasya' -- a person whose mind and senses are unrestrained lacks the single-pointed resolve 'I have only to attain the Supreme.' Because his mind and senses are uncontrolled, he remains absorbed in transient worldly enjoyments and possessions -- sometimes craving honor, sometimes comfort, sometimes wealth, sometimes pleasure -- his desires are many and scattered, so his intellect never becomes resolute. 'Na cayuktasya bhavana' -- one whose intellect lacks resolve also lacks the conviction 'I must only perform my duty, giving up desire for its fruit, longing, and attachment'; this absence stems from not having a fixed goal. 'Na cabhavayatah santih' -- one who is not devoted to his own duty cannot attain peace; just as monks, teachers, or people of any calling who are not diligent in their respective duties do not find peace, because lack of firmness in performing one's duty itself breeds restlessness. 'Asantasya kutah sukham' -- how can a restless person be happy? His heart is in constant turmoil, and no amount of favorable outer circumstances can remove that inner agitation, so he cannot be happy. [The next verse explains why the unsteady person's intellect fails to become resolute.]

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
[Sub-commentary defending Madhavacharya's gloss.] Since the point had already been made, one may ask why the verse 'nasti buddhih...' is needed at all; the answer is that it explains the fault that follows from the absence of grace (prasada) -- one might think that meditation aided by hearing and reflection is itself sufficient to bring direct knowledge of Brahman, making such grace superfluous, but this is refuted. In the absence of grace there is no yukti, i.e. no one-pointedness of mind, hence 'nasti buddhih' -- there is no correct direct knowledge of Brahman for the ungraced. It is then asked why the verse further adds 'na cayuktasya' if the fault of the ungraced state is already stated; the answer is that without grace there is also no bhavana, meditation, since knowledge does not arise from meditation alone, and hence no knowledge -- and no peace -- for one who does not meditate. And though santi (peace/grace) was earlier said to be a means to knowledge, here it is shown also to follow after knowledge, indicating that this state removes not only all suffering in liberation but grants the supreme happiness unattainable by worldly beings -- a point also implied in Sankara's commentary referring to 'brahma-adi-pada'.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Vallabhacharya establishes, by the negative method, that control of the mind is the means to steady wisdom (sthitaprajnata). 'Nasti' -- the unsteady person, one devoid of the discipline of restraint, does not possess a single, resolute intellect; nor does he have bhavana, i.e. reflective contemplation on truth. The rest is self-evident.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Madhusudana Sarasvati reinforces this same point negatively: for the unsteady person, one whose mind is uncontrolled, the intellect concerning the Self -- born of Vedantic inquiry through hearing and reflection -- does not arise. In the absence of that intellect, such a person also lacks bhavana, i.e. the sustained meditation (nididhyasana) consisting of a continuous flow of Self-related thoughts uninterrupted by dissimilar ones (the negation 'na' applies throughout the verse). One who does not meditate on the Self does not attain santi -- the direct realization of the oneness of Brahman and the Self, arising from the Vedantic statements, which removes ignorance along with its effects. For one lacking peace -- devoid of direct realization of the Self -- where is happiness, meaning the bliss of liberation?

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Sridhara Swami establishes negatively that control of the senses is the means to steady wisdom. For the unsteady person, one who has not mastered his senses, the intellect -- the wisdom concerning the Self arising from the teaching of scripture and teacher -- does not arise; how then could it become firmly established? Hence it is said that the unsteady person also lacks bhavana, meditation, since it is through meditation that the intellect becomes established in the Self, and this the unsteady person lacks. One who does not meditate on the Self also lacks santi, the resting of the mind in the Self. For one without peace, where is the happiness of liberation?

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Dhanapati takes up the question whether purity of mind (citta-prasada) is directly the means for establishing the intellect, or only indirectly through meditation (nididhyasana); the earlier statement's intent -- that this does not occur without purity of mind -- is clarified here as continued praise of mental clarity. For the unsteady person, one whose mind is not composed or purified, there is no intellect regarding the true nature of the Self, i.e. no realization of the identity of Brahman and Self. Nor does the unsteady person have bhavana -- sustained contemplation directed toward that end -- even if hearing and reflection have occurred, because the mind still wanders toward objects. One who does not restrain such object-oriented thought lacks santi, the cessation of craving (trsna, desire). For one without peace, whence happiness? Since ignorance and its effects, including craving, remain, neither the bliss of Brahman nor even ordinary sensory happiness is possible for such a person, for craving underlies even apparent worldly pleasure and taints it with suffering; craving itself is the root of all suffering, as declared in the Yoga Vasistha: 'the endless, unripening, exalted fruits of the creeper of craving are, O Raghava, nothing but suffering here; as the arising of desire is suffering, so the cessation of desire is happiness -- this is felt neither in hell nor even in the world of Brahma; as much as desire arises in a creature, so much does the seed of suffering sprout,' and so on.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
One might ask: since steady wisdom (sthitaprajnata) was already described even for one absorbed in meditation, what is the distinction here? The answer: for the unsteady person -- one lacking union (yoga) with Me [Krishna] -- there is no true intellect at all; that is, if union with Me does not follow even after intellectual understanding, that state is not genuine steady wisdom. Hence, without communion with the Lord, even absorbed meditation accomplishes nothing. If one asks what further fruit even such absorbed union yields, the answer is: the unsteady person, one lacking a relationship with the Lord, also lacks bhavana -- the longing for a body fit for divine, loving relish (rasa) with the Lord. If one asks what mere longing alone achieves, the answer: one who does not cultivate that longing also lacks santi -- the attainment of a body fit for that divine relish; such a person's soul does not experience direct bliss. And for one lacking that peace, deprived of such a body and hence not free from affliction, how can there be happiness -- meaning the direct blissful experience of loving devotional relationship with the Lord?

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Having stated the fault of not restraining the senses along with the mind, it is now asked: what fault is there if the intellect itself remains unsettled? The answer: for the unsteady person, one not devoted to hearing and reflection, there is no firm conviction of the identity of Brahman and the Self, because doubts regarding both the means of valid knowledge and its object remain unresolved. Likewise, for the unsteady person of uncomposed mind, there is no bhavana -- the continuous flow of mental modifications shaped in the form of Brahman -- since an unsteady mind produces an unsteady intellect. For one who does not meditate, there is no santi, the cessation of all suffering, because an unsettled mind inevitably brings suffering. For one who is not at peace and whose suffering never ceases, there can be no happiness whatsoever -- the bliss of the non-dual inner Self -- precisely because of this suffering. (Note: the first 'ayukta' derives from the root 'yuj' meaning union/yoga, the second from 'yuj' meaning concentration/samadhi.) Hence, the settling of the intellect is essential.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
One who is not connected with the Supreme [in Kṛṣṇa consciousness] can have neither transcendental intelligence nor a steady mind, without which there is no possibility of peace. And how can there be any happiness without peace?

 Unless one is in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, there is no possibility of peace. So it is confirmed in the Fifth Chapter (5.29) that when one understands that Kṛṣṇa is the only enjoyer of all the good results of sacrifice and penance, that He is the proprietor of all universal manifestations, and that He is the real friend of all living entities, then only can one have real peace. Therefore, if one is not in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, there cannot be a final goal for the mind. Disturbance is due to want of an ultimate goal, and when one is certain that Kṛṣṇa is the enjoyer, proprietor and friend of everyone and everything, then one can, with a steady mind, bring about peace. Therefore, one who is engaged without a relationship with Kṛṣṇa is certainly always in distress and is without peace, however much he may make a show of peace and spiritual advancement in life. Kṛṣṇa consciousness is a self-manifested peaceful condition which can be achieved only in relationship with Kṛṣṇa.
