Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Thus, as Lord Sri Krishna showed him the army, Arjuna saw, standing in the enemy ranks, his own dear kinsmen -- family members he recognized, among them brothers, nephews, elders, grandsires, and other acquaintances and well-wishers. Not only in the enemy ranks but equally in his own army he saw similarly familiar and close relatives. Perhaps, seeing this sight, for the first time he could estimate the terrible, sorrowful consequence of a family feud, and it shook him to his very depths. Being a man of action, perhaps until now he had not even considered to what extent the entire society would have to sacrifice itself in order to fulfil his ambitions and avenge Duryodhana's injustices.

Whatever the reason, it is clear that at the sight of this scene his heart filled with compassion and grief. But his compassion at this moment was not genuine. Had the feelings of compassion and grief in him been as real and natural as those of Gautama Buddha, he would have behaved quite differently long before the war. It is not fitting that Sanjaya names this feeling of Arjuna's 'compassion.' It is generally human nature to seek greatness by presenting one's own weaknesses as some divine virtue -- just as a wealthy man who builds a temple in his own name is still called generous, even though secretly his desire is to immortalize his own name. Similarly here, the rise of despondency in Arjuna's mind arose from the complete collapse of his self-control, which has been mistakenly named compassion.

In Arjuna's mind there lay a long train of countless suppressed feelings, actively seeking an occasion to burst forth with full force. At this moment Arjuna's mind and intellect had become mutually disjointed, because, considering himself the greatest of warriors, his mind held an intense eagerness to be victorious in battle. Because of past suppressed feelings and the present anxiety over victory, his discriminating faculty was thrown off balance.

Further on in this chapter it is described how Arjuna begins to behave like a person suffering a mental breakdown. In the first chapter of the Gita, an attempt is made to trace the case-history of a person afflicted with what may be called 'Arjuna's disease.' As I have said before, the sure remedy for this self-destructive Arjuna's disease is the treatment of Sri Krishna.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Seeing all those kinsmen standing there in their own places, Kunti's son Arjuna was overcome with utter faint-heartedness, and, becoming despondent, spoke these words.

Explanation: 'Tan sarvan bandhunavasthitan samikshya' -- besides those Arjuna had already seen in the previous verse, he now also saw, standing in both armies, great-grandfathers such as Bahlika; brothers-in-law such as Dhrishtadyumna, Shikhandi, Suratha; sons-in-law's fathers such as Jayadratha; and many other kinsmen.

'Sa kaunteyah kripaya parayavishtah' -- these words convey: those very kings whom mother Kunti had sent a message urging to fight, and about whom Arjuna had wished to know -- 'who among these valorous ones will engage me in battle?' -- for the sake of seeing whom he had ordered Lord Sri Krishna to station the chariot between the two armies -- that same Kunti's son Arjuna is now overcome with utter faint-heartedness!

Seeing, in both armies, relatives connected by birth and by learning at every turn, the thought arose in Arjuna's mind: whether men of this side die or that side die in the battle, the loss will be ours alone -- our own lineage will be destroyed, our own relations will be slain! With this thought, Arjuna's desire for battle vanished, and inner faint-heartedness set in. The Lord later (2.23) calls this faint-heartedness 'kashmalam' and 'hridayadaurbalyam' (weakness of heart), and Arjuna himself (2.7) acknowledges it, calling himself one 'whose nature is overcome by the fault of pitiable weakness.'

That Arjuna is 'overcome by faint-heartedness' ('kripayavishtah') proves that this weakness was not present before, but has only now arisen -- it is thus an adventitious fault. Being adventitious, it will not remain permanently. But valor is natural to Arjuna; hence that will indeed remain.

What is this utter faint-heartedness? To see, standing arrayed before oneself ready for battle, men like Duryodhana, Duhshasana, Shakuni -- who slander, insult, and cause pain without cause, who bear enmity, who strive to destroy -- and yet feel no inclination to slay them, no effort to destroy them -- this is the fault called utter faint-heartedness. Here, this fault of faint-heartedness has so overtaken Arjuna that he now feels compassion (Gita 1.35-46) even toward those unrighteous, sinful men who wish him ill and repeatedly strive to harm him -- and thereby he is falling away from his own dharma as a kshatriya.

'Vishidannidamabravit' -- thinking of what will become of the family, the lineage, the country as a result of this war, Arjuna is overwhelmed with grief, and in that state he speaks the words described in the following verses.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
With the Lord's sanction to view the assembled armies granted for the sake of beginning battle, Partha then saw those stationed in both armies -- this is the construal; 'atha' is a synonym of 'tatha.' In the enemy army: fathers -- paternal uncles such as Bhurishrava; grandfathers -- Bhishma, Somadatta and the rest; teachers -- Drona, Kripa and the rest; maternal uncles -- Shalya, Shakuni and the rest; brothers -- Duryodhana and the rest; sons -- Lakshmana and the rest; grandsons -- the sons of Lakshmana and others; friends -- Ashvatthama, Jayadratha and other companions; fathers-in-law -- the fathers of his wives; well-wishers -- friends such as Kritavarma, Bhagadatta and the rest. By 'well-wishers' should be understood all who had done him kindness, including maternal grandfathers and the like; the same should be inferred, by extension, for his own army as well. Things being thus, with the perverse notion, born of delusion, that this violence -- though enjoined by scripture and hence righteous -- was a great wickedness, and with his discrimination overwhelmed by the mental disturbance called grief and delusion rooted in possessiveness ('these are mine'), Arjuna's desire arose to withdraw from the very battle-duty he had earlier undertaken -- a withdrawal culminating in great calamity; this is what is shown. The epithet 'Kaunteya' (Kunti's son), invoking his mother's feminine nature, is used with reference to Partha's present bewilderment, to suggest that his compassion is spontaneous, self-arisen, and not induced by any external cause -- to make this vivid, the qualifier 'supreme' is used, or, read as 'apara' (unequalled), meaning: compassion had existed in his own army before too, but now an equally great compassion arose even toward the Kaurava army. 'Vishidan' (becoming despondent) -- by stating grief and speech as simultaneous, the verse indicates that at the very moment of speaking, symptoms of despondency such as a choked voice and falling tears were already manifest.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Venkatanatha now states the condensed sense of the remainder of the chapter, in 'sa tu...': the word 'tu' (but) signals the contrast between Duryodhana as described earlier and the character of Partha about to be described. His epithet 'high-souled' (mahamana) conveys that, out of unwillingness to slay even enemies who bear the mere designation of kinsmen, he counts victory -- even sovereignty over all three worlds -- as no more than a blade of grass (as he says later, 'I do not desire victory,' 1.31). That he cannot bear even his enemies' suffering is conveyed by 'supremely compassionate' ('overcome by the deepest compassion'). The phrase 'fathers, then grandfathers' -- expanded by the list 'teachers, fathers, sons' (1.34) and the like -- conveys the sheer breadth of those toward whom his affection extends, expressed through this long compound; or, the sense is that even when a kinsman does one grave wrong, one's bond with him does not loosen. (Compare 'all these kinsmen, indeed our own people,' 1.37.) Since even those on the side of the aggressors -- teachers and the rest -- are, on reflection, not to be slain, and since Arjuna foresees the chain of unrighteousness born of the destruction of a family, he is called 'supremely righteous.' His intent is that any sanction to slay an aggressor applies only to persons other than teachers and the like. 'Sabhratrika' (along with his brothers) conveys that this is not Arjuna alone, but all the Pandavas together, feeling this way -- this gathers up all the plural forms used later ('us,' 'we,' 'by us,' etc.). Or, the sense is: it is not merely out of disregard for personal injury that he overlooks their killing him, but even in the face of wrongs done to Dharmaraja, Draupadi and others -- for whom he has the deepest reverence, affection and compassion, as though they stood in the place of his own teachers. Arjuna's intent is that no fault of killing a teacher should fall even upon his brothers. To indicate that they are indeed to be slain, the word 'ghnatah api' ('even while being slain,' 1.35) is used. This is elaborated in 'bhavadbhih...' and the like; the term 'aggressor' (atatayin) is also explained there, with reference to acts such as the burning of the house of lac. As the law-books say: 'The poisoner, the arsonist, the one who attacks with weapon in hand, the plunderer of wealth, the seizer of land, and the abductor of another's wife -- these six are aggressors' (Manu 8.350); and 'one may slay an approaching aggressor without hesitation; no fault whatever attaches to the slayer of an aggressor' (Manu 8.351). The repeated use of 'et cetera' shows that such grounds for being deemed an aggressor had been committed by them many times over, and had not ceased even now -- as indicated by the present-tense phrase 'even while being slain' (Mahabharata 1.1.14). By the word 'bhavadbhih' ('by you all'), Arjuna implicates even Dhritarashtra, who out of fatherly affection consents to and is thus complicit with the wrathful Duryodhana ('I follow the deluded, wrathful Duryodhana,' Mahabharata 1.1.145). Thus it is shown that Duryodhana and the rest, their minds overpowered by excessive greed, stand in stark contrast to what is meant by 'high-souled.' The phrase 'Supreme Person' is used to produce grief at the certainty that Dhritarashtra's people, including allies like Shakuni and Karna, are doomed to be slain regardless. The compound may mean 'one whose ally is the Supreme Person,' or 'one who is merely the instrument, the Supreme Person being the true agent' -- as the Lord will later say, 'these have already been slain by Me; be merely the instrument, O ambidextrous one' (11.33). And Arjuna, even before this, had determined that the mere presence of the unarmed Supreme Person was the true cause of victory, surpassing even the presence of a thousand mighty armies, and had chosen Him for that very reason. To indicate that this is not the proper occasion for such affection, the phrase 'seeing your own people' is used. The passage beginning 'out of affection for kinsmen' and the rest shows that Arjuna's dejection was unlike Duryodhana's -- his was not born of hatred toward kin, cruelty, or fear of the enemy, nor from any weakness in one who has the Supreme Person as his ally. The sense of the passage from 'the limbs sink' (1.28) through 'the mind' (1.30) is condensed with the word 'excessively,' etc. 'Sakhin' means age-mates; 'suhridah' means well-wishers irrespective of age. 'Senayorubhayorapi' conveys that nearly all of these relations were found, for the most part, within a single army -- i.e., looking at them thoughtfully, as befits scriptural and worldly propriety. 'Sarvan bandhun' conveys that not a single kinsman remained absent from that gathering.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having stood beside him, seeing those who deliberate on their own true welfare, what did he do? To this he answers, in 'Tan...': having seen them, Kaunteya (Kunti's son), becoming despondent, spoke this forthcoming statement. One might object: seeing kshatriyas' battle-enthusiasm, only eagerness would be fitting for Arjuna -- why does despondency arise in him? To this he answers: 'overcome by the deepest compassion' -- compassion in the form of supreme devotion, of the sort described as 'one who sees the same in all beings' (Bhagavatam 11.2.45). One might further object: but if the kingdom is thus lost, how will the protection of the people be assured -- how then does this compassion also extend there? To this he answers, in 'bandhun...': these very kinsmen of his are themselves capable of protecting the kingdom; he himself, however, is solely devoted to the Lord's feet.
