Life Application:
See God outside and inside all beings, unmoving and moving, incomprehensible due to subtlety, far away yet near.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Here, the omnipresence of the Supreme Self is indicated in the inimitable style of the Upanishads. He is without and within all beings; the Consciousness expressed in all individualizing equipment (Upadhis) is all-pervading. 'Without and within' implies that where there are equipment like the body, He exists particularly expressed; but even where there is no equipment, He exists as the absolute Truth. Just as where there is a radio, the existence of sound waves is clearly known, but where there is no radio, the absence of those waves cannot be claimed. He is moving and also unmoving; that which moves by its own will is a moving creature, and a motionless object falls into the unmoving category. This statement is also interpreted to mean that the Self, while being unmoving, is also moving. This implies that the Self, being all-pervading, is unmoving from the standpoint of Its own nature, but the same Self appears to move as if limited by moving equipment. For example, a person sitting in his own place (unmoving) in a moving vehicle covers a journey of miles. Thus, the essential factor of our personality is one, eternal, and complete, which pervades everywhere, within and without. No action is possible without It, and therefore It is present in all actions. It exists everywhere in the form of Existence (Sat). Then, what is the reason that we cannot see It through the senses, or experience It with the mind and intellect? The Lord says, It is incomprehensible because of Its extreme subtlety. An object with qualities is gross. An object with more qualities is grosser and can be perceived by more than one sense organ. For instance, the earth is perceived by all five senses, whereas air is perceived only by the ears (hearing) and skin (touch). Therefore, the earth is the grossest element, and space, having only the quality of sound, is the subtlest. The cause is always subtler than the effect. Space, being a created object, must also have a cause. The cause of space is that eternal substratum, Brahman, from which the five great elements originate. Naturally, since Brahman is subtler than space, It cannot be known in a visible form through our available instruments; It is incomprehensible. It is far away and yet near. A finite object with a form can be said to be located at a specific place, here or there. Its distance can be measured from the observer's position to call it far or near. But that which is all-pervading is at once here and there, and therefore it is both far and near. These two words can also be explained as: the Supreme Self is far away as It is free from the limitations of all names and forms, but the same Supreme Self is near as It exists in these names and forms. Sri Shankaracharya writes in his commentary that this Self appears extremely far to the ignorant, whereas the wise experience It as extremely near, as their own Self. In short, this verse, full of the beauty of paradoxical language, suddenly awakens those readers who are satisfied with only intellectual knowledge. It inspires them to directly experience the all-pervading and transcendental nature of the Supreme Self through contemplation or meditation. Regarding this Knowable, the Lord says further

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Another reason for the existence of the Knowable is stated: 'Moreover'. That Knowable is indeed one in all bodies like space, because there is no evidence of its division, and if it were divided (different in each body), it would result in its being non-Self like a pot. Therefore, it is extremely foolhardy to say that the non-dual Jneya, which is the innermost Self of all, does not exist. Thus, He says: 'And undivided...' How then is there a perception of difference among bodies etc.? Apprehending this doubt, He says: 'in beings' (meaning it is due to mental projection or illusion). The cause for that is 'in bodies'. And since It is the cause of the sustenance of the effects (creation), the Knowable exists; thus He says: 'sustainer of beings'. And because It is the cause of their dissolution and origin as both the material and efficient cause, It exists; thus He says: 'at the time of dissolution...' (devourer and originator). If so, one might object that non-duality is lost because cause-and-effect relationship is real. Anticipating this, He says: 'Just as...' (implying the cause-and-effect relationship is only an appearance, like a rope appearing as a snake).

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
That Supreme Lord is completely filled inside and outside of all beings; He alone exists in the form of moving and unmoving beings; and He alone is the farthest of the far as well as the nearest of the near. Because of His extreme subtlety, He is not an object of comprehension.

Commentary — [The description of the Knowable (Jneya) principle has been given in six verses, from the 12th to the 17th verse. Among them, this 16th verse is the fourth. Within this verse, the essence of the preceding three verses and the following two verses is also included. Therefore, this verse is the essence of this section.]

"bahirantaśca bhūtānāmacaraṃ carameva ca" — Just as when pots made of ice are placed in the ocean, there is water outside those pots, water inside them, and they themselves (being made of ice) are also water. Similarly, the Supreme Lord is outside all moving and unmoving beings, He is inside them, and they themselves are also of the nature of the Supreme Lord. The significance is that just as there is nothing but water in the pots, i.e., everything is water only, similarly, there is no other element in the universe except the Supreme Lord, i.e., everything is the Supreme Lord alone. The Lord has said this from the perspective of sages as "vāsudevaḥ sarvam" (Gita 7.19) and from His own perspective as "sadasaccāham" (Gita 9.19).

"dūrasthaṃ cāntike ca tat" — A thing is said to be far or near from three standpoints — space, time, and object. The Supreme Lord is the farthest of the far and the nearest of the near from all three standpoints. For example, the same Lord exists in the farthest lands, and the same Lord exists in the nearest place. He was the same Lord before the earliest time, He will be the same Lord after the latest time, and He is the same Lord even now. He exists before all objects, at the end of all objects, and in the form of all objects.

For those who desire the accumulation of transient, perishable objects and sensual pleasures, the Supreme Lord (though essentially very close) is far away. But for one who is devoted only to the Supreme Lord, the Lord is very near. Therefore, a spiritual aspirant, abandoning the desire for worldly pleasures and accumulation, must awaken only the longing to realize the Supreme Lord. As soon as the intense desire to realize the Lord is awakened, the Lord is realized, meaning one experiences the eternal union with Him.

"sūkṣmatvāttadavijñeyam" — That Supreme Lord, being extremely subtle, is not an object of the senses and the mind-intellect; that is, the Lord cannot be grasped by them. Now the question arises: if He is not known, does He not exist? No, He is not non-existent. Just as water in the form of atoms is not visible to the eyes because of its subtlety, yet despite being invisible, it is not non-existent. That water exists in the atmosphere in atomic form, and when it becomes gross, it starts appearing in the form of drops, hail, etc. Similarly, the Supreme Lord, being extremely subtle, is not known through the senses, mind, intellect, etc., because He is beyond them, transcendental.

It is only due to the ignorance of living beings that the Supreme Lord is not known. For example, somewhere the words "Srimad Bhagavad Gita" are written. One who is unlettered sees only lines, while one who is educated sees "Srimad Bhagavad Gita". A person educated in Sanskrit starts seeing from which root the word is formed and what it means. One who contemplates the Gita starts seeing the deep imports of the Gita. Similarly, those who do not have the knowledge of the Divine Principle do not see the Lord and He is not known to them. But those who have attained the knowledge of the Divine Principle see nothing but the Supreme Lord everywhere.

That Divine Principle is called "Jneya" (Knowable) (13.12, 17) and also "Avijneya" (Incomprehensible). The meaning is that He can be known only by the Self (Himself), hence He is Jneya; and He cannot be known through the senses, mind, and intellect, hence He is Avijneya.

To know the all-pervading Supreme Lord, it is necessary for the aspirant to accept that the Lord is all-pervading. Such acceptance is just like knowing. Just as (upon attaining realization) no one can destroy knowledge (knowing), similarly, no one can destroy this belief (acceptance) that the Lord is all-pervading. When no one can destroy worldly beliefs (which are unreal) like "I am a Brahmin," "I am an ascetic," etc., then who can destroy spiritual beliefs (which are real)? The point is that firm belief is also a spiritual discipline. Like knowing, believing also has great glory. When one firmly accepts that the Lord is all-pervading, this belief will not remain as mere belief, but the extremely subtle Lord, who is beyond the senses, mind, and intellect, will be experienced.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Existing, bahih, outside- the word bahih is used with reference to the body including the skin, which is misconceived through ignorance to be the Self, and which is itself taken as the boundary. Similarly, the word antah, inside, is used with reference to the indwelling Self, making the body itself as the boundary. When 'outside' and 'inside' are used, there may arise the contingency of the nonexistence of That in the middle. Hence this is said: acaram caram eva ca, moving as well as not moving-even that which appears as the body, moving or not moving, is nothing but the Knowable, in the same way as the appearance of a snake on a rope (is nothing but the rope).
In all empirical things, moving as also non-moving, be the Knowable, why should It not be known by all as such? In answer it is said: It is true that It shines through everything; still it is subtle like space. Therefore, although It is the Knowable, tat, It; is avijneyam, incomprehensible to the ignorant people; suksmatvat, due to Its intrinsic subtleness. But to the enlightened It is ever known from the valid means of knowledge such as (the texts), 'All this is verily the Self' (Ch. 7.25.2), 'Brahman alone is all this' (Nr. Ut.7), etc. It is durastham, far away, since, to the unenlightened, It is unattainable even in millions of years. And tat, That; is antike, near, since It is the Self of the enlightened.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
He explains that Brahman is of the nature of the universe: 'moving as well as unmoving'. For everything is of two forms, while the One is distinct from them. 'Acharam' (unmoving) refers to the inert/material world, as established by Srutis like: 'In the beginning, my dear, this was Being alone' (Ch. U. 6.2.1) and 'All this is verily Brahman' (Ch. U. 3.14.1). 'Charam' (moving) refers to the sentient/individual soul, according to the Sruti: 'That thou art, O Shvetaketu' (Ch. U. 6.8.16). Because of Its subtlety, It is incomprehensible, and It exists far away yet is very near. As the Mantra declares: 'It moves, and It moves not; It is far, and It is near; It is within all this, and It is outside all this' (Ishopanishad, 5). Here, 'ejati' means it moves, and 'na ejati' means it does not move; 'tat u antike' is the word division.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
For all created beings which are transient effects, the real, unimagined substratum is one alone. 'Without and within' means that, just as a rope completely pervades its falsely imagined snake or stream of water, Brahman pervades all. Therefore, the entire collection of moving and unmoving beings is that substratum alone; meaning, nothing among the imagined effects is separate from It. Even though It is the Self of all, It is incomprehensible due to Its subtlety, being devoid of form, etc., which makes It impossible to be defined clearly as 'this is it'. Consequently, for those who are devoid of the means of self-realization, It is far away, as if separated by millions of leagues, and remains unattainable even in billions of years. But for those who are endowed with the means of knowledge, It is very near, being completely non-different from their own Self. As the Srutis declare: 'It is farther than the far, and yet near at hand; for those who see, it is placed right here in the cave of the heart' (Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.7).

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Moreover, He explains: 'Without and within'. That Brahman alone exists outside and inside of all moving and unmoving beings, which are Its own effects; just as gold is inside and outside of ornaments like bracelets and earrings, or water is inside and outside of waves. All moving and unmoving beings are that Brahman alone, because the effect is essentially of the nature of its cause. Nevertheless, due to Its subtlety, i.e., being devoid of form, etc., It is incomprehensible, meaning It cannot be clearly known as 'this' or 'that'. Hence, for the ignorant, It is far away, as if separated by hundreds of thousands of leagues, because It is beyond the modified Prakriti. But for the wise, being their very inner Self, It is always near. As the Mantra declares: 'It moves, and It moves not; It is far, and It is near; It is within all this, and It is outside all this' (Ishopanishad, 5). Here, 'ejati' means it moves, and 'na ejati' means it does not move; 'tat u antike' is the word division.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
With the intention of showing that 'from this also the existence of the Knowable Brahman is established', He says: 'Without'. With reference to the body, which is falsely imagined to be the Self through ignorance, and taking the skin as its limit, Brahman is said to be 'outside'. Then, with reference to the innermost Self and taking the body as the limit, It is said to be 'inside'. Thus, 'outside' means the external world of objects, and 'inside' means the innermost Self in the midst of all moving and unmoving beings. To prevent any doubt about Its non-existence in the middle, He says: 'moving as well as unmoving'. Even that which appears in the form of various bodies is the Knowable alone, just as a snake appearing on a rope is nothing but the rope; thus, whatever appears in the Knowable is the Knowable itself. If so, why is It not known by everyone as 'this'? To this, He says: 'due to Its subtlety, It is incomprehensible'. Just as when a mango is seen by the eye, its taste is not seen because the eye is not the proper organ for tasting, similarly, although the Knowable is the Self of all and present in everything, It is incomprehensible because It is beyond the senses, like space. By this, the objection that 'by knowing a pot, etc., Brahman is also known since Brahman is of the nature of the pot' is refuted. Therefore, for the ignorant who are devoid of the means of realization, because It is unknown, It is far away, being unattainable even in billions of years. And It is very near for the wise, who know It constantly through scriptural testimonies like 'All this is verily the Self' and 'All this is verily Brahman', being their own Self, without any separation. As the Srutis declare: 'It moves, and It moves not; It is far, and It is near; It is within all this, and It is outside all this' (Ishopanishad, 5), and 'It is farther than the far, and yet near at hand; for those who see, it is placed right here in the cave of the heart' (Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.7).

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
In the state of embodiment, existence within beings is evident. Since a liberated soul is bodiless, its existence outside them is natural; but how is it present within them? In answer to this, he cites: 'Eating, playing, enjoying...' (Ch. U. 8.12.3). The meaning is that the soul exists within them during its spontaneous activities. This embodiment is not caused by Karma, because it is said of the soul whose true nature is manifest: 'He manifests in his own form' (Ch. U. 8.12.2), and because it is said: 'He becomes a self-ruler' (Ch. U. 7.25.2) as his merits and sins are washed away. With this in mind, he says 'spontaneous activities'. Passages like: 'If he becomes desirous of the world of fathers...' (Ch. U. 8.2.1) and 'traversing these worlds, eating what he wishes, assuming what form he wishes...' (Taitt. U. 3.10.5) are implied by the word 'etc.' (adi). For the self, which is inherently changeless, acts like assuming a threefold form, eating, desiring the world of fathers, etc., are impossible without accepting a body, and its body is indeed an aggregate of elements (bhutas), whether material or non-material. Thus, its existence within elements (bodies) is established. The qualities of being 'unmoving' and 'moving' do not apply to the pure state of the self simultaneously; rather, the states of being embodied or bodiless, referred to as 'outside and inside', are the causes for these. This is shown by the phrase: 'by nature unmoving, and moving when in a body'. What is meant here is movement dependent on limbs like feet and the absence of such movement.
To resolve the objection of its non-perception despite being present, it is said: 'It is so subtle that none can comprehend It'. For the self which is constantly perceived as 'I', in what form is it incomprehensible? To this, he says: 'possessed of all powers and omniscient'. This is the meaning referred to by the word 'That' (tat). To indicate the doubt of its perceptibility, and as an illustration of the absence of other reasons for non-perception like distance, he says: 'though present in one's own body (this field)'. Although elements like air, etc., which are subtler than earth, are perceived separately, to rule out such perception here, it is said: 'because of Its extreme subtlety'. Even though the self is perceived as 'I know', the inability to perceive it distinctly is what is meant by 'incomprehensible'. To show this, he says: 'as distinct from the body'. If its distinctness were never perceived by anyone, it would be a non-entity like a hare's horn, and the scriptural injunctions for the practice of yoga would be useless. To address this, he says: 'by bound ones (worldly beings)'. Since even yogis do not have uninterrupted and most vivid perception like the liberated, he generally says 'by bound ones'. Alternatively, those devoid of yoga are meant by 'worldly beings', because yogis, being close to liberation, are almost liberated.
By the phrase 'far away and yet It is very near', all-pervasiveness is not intended, because that has already been stated in 'pervading all' (13.14). Therefore, to resolve the doubt of how that which is incomprehensible to worldly beings due to subtlety is to be known by them, he explains its difficult grasp or easy grasp based on the difference in qualifiers: 'by those who possess modesty (amanitva) etc.'

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Thus, to address the objection that being the enjoyer contradicts all-pervasiveness, He says: 'Without'. Outside the moving and unmoving beings as the enjoyer, and inside as their very form or as the Self, that Brahman alone exists. Thus, to resolve the objection that being outside and inside implies division and contradicts all-pervasiveness, He says: 'unmoving and moving'. By using the word 'eva' (indeed), it is indicated that movingness is co-existent with unmovingness and vice versa, thereby showing that It is the substratum of mutually contradictory attributes. If so, It would be known by all. Then what is the distinction of those who possess the aforementioned means of realization? To this, He says: 'because of Its subtlety...'. That Brahman, assuming subtle forms for the sake of His divine play, is incomprehensible to those who lack spiritual practice, meaning It cannot be known in detail. This is what is meant by 'It is far away and yet It is near'. It is far away for those who are outward-turned, and near, close at hand, for the devotees. By the two 'and' (cha) particles, it is shown that both these states are part of His divine play. Alternatively, It is far away for those on the path of scriptural rules (maryada-marga) and near for those on the path of grace (pushti-marga). Or, for the followers of the path of grace in the state of separation, when they are tormented by intense separation, It is far away in terms of physical separation, yet near in the heart in an unmanifest way. And for the sake of their life-sustenance during their ignorance of It, It is situated nearby. This is in accordance with the statement: 'By Me, who am worshipped in a paroksha (unmanifest) way, I became invisible' (Srimad Bhagavatam 10.32.21).

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
If it is unattached and unrelated, how can it be perceived? Anticipating this doubt, He says: 'Without'. For living beings, the eleven senses and the gross elements, being external due to being mere modifications (vikaras), are called 'without' (bahir). The intellect (mahat), ego (ahankara), five subtle elements (tanmatras), and the unmanifest (avyakta), being closer due to being of the nature of primordial nature (prakriti), are called 'within' (antar). 'Moving and unmoving' (charam acharam): the individual souls (purushas) which are delimited by the moving and unmoving adjuncts (upadhis) are referred to here. By the grammatical coordination (samanadhikaranya) 'moving and unmoving is the Knowable', the identity of the individual souls with the Knowable Brahman is declared. By saying 'the Knowable is without and within', the relationship of the Knowable with the sixteen modifications and the eightfold primordial nature is stated. This relationship is indeed superimposed (adhyasta), determined by the superimposed prakriti and its modifications, in accordance with the maxim 'like god, like offering' (yadrisho yakshah tadrisho balih). Thus, for the Purusha whose essence is pure consciousness, since there exists a superimposed relationship with the qualities (gunas), its being the perceiver of qualities (gunopalabdhritva) is appropriate. Just as the sun, whose nature is pure light, becomes the illuminator in relation to the illuminated object, so it is here.
If It is eternally immediate (nitya-aparoksha) and related to prakriti and its modifications, then why is It not perceived by everyone? To this, He says: 'due to Its subtlety...'. Because It is difficult to perceive, the Knowable is 'avijneyam' (difficult to comprehend). Just as the whiteness of a crystal placed near a red hibiscus flower, though present nearby, is not perceived because it is veiled by the color of the flower, similarly, the eternally immediate and unattached Brahman, due to the overlay of the adjuncts (upadhis), cannot be perceived in Its distinct state by the ignorant; instead, It is perceived only as endowed with the qualities of the adjuncts. For the wise, however, by dissolving the adjuncts, It is easily grasped. This is what He says: 'It exists far away, yet near It is'. Just as a foolish person thinks the reflected sun in the water is far away from the original sun, while the wise person knows that the rays of the eye, reflecting off the water, travel upward to perceive the original sun itself. Knowing that the perception of the reflection as being below is due to the impression of the downward-turned eyes, he sees the reflection in the place of the original sun itself. Thus, Brahman, the original source (bimba), is far away from the reflected souls (pratibimba-jeevas) for the ignorant, but for the wise, It is extremely near.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
The Supreme Truth exists outside and inside of all living beings, the moving and the nonmoving. Because He is subtle, He is beyond the power of the material senses to see or to know. Although far, far away, He is also near to all.

 In Vedic literature we understand that Nārāyaṇa, the Supreme Person, is residing both outside and inside of every living entity. He is present in both the spiritual and material worlds. Although He is far, far away, still He is near to us. These are the statements of Vedic literature. Āsīno dūraṁ vrajati śayāno yāti sarvataḥ ( Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.2.21). And because He is always engaged in transcendental bliss, we cannot understand how He is enjoying His full opulence. We cannot see or understand with these material senses. Therefore in the Vedic language it is said that to understand Him our material mind and senses cannot act. But one who has purified his mind and senses by practicing Kṛṣṇa consciousness in devotional service can see Him constantly. It is confirmed in Brahma-saṁhitā that the devotee who has developed love for the Supreme God can see Him always, without cessation. And it is confirmed in Bhagavad-gītā (11.54) that He can be seen and understood only by devotional service. Bhaktyā tv ananyayā śakyaḥ.
