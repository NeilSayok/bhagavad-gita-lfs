Life Application:
Shake off emotional weakness when faced with difficult decisions today, and stand firm to fulfill your responsibilities.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Lord Sri Krishna, who had stood silent until now, begins to sharply rebuke the grief-stricken Arjuna with forceful words. Each of His words strikes like a sword-blow, sharp enough to overwhelm any person. 'Klaibya' means impotence. The word here refers to that state of mind in which a person can neither summon the courage to face a situation like a man, nor sit down in despair like a soft-hearted, bashful woman. In today's language, when a friend behaves in this manner, others express astonishment by saying, 'Is this man a woman or a man?' Arjuna's condition too had become like that of the eunuchs of the royal court, who, though appearing outwardly like men, dressed as women -- speaking like men but feeling like women within, capable in body but weak in mind.

Until now Sri Krishna had remained silent; His profound silence was itself meaningful. Arjuna, in his state of delusion, having resolved not to fight, was also presenting many arguments in his own favour. Sri Krishna knew that opposing Arjuna in such a state earlier would have been futile. But now, seeing tears in his eyes, He understood that Arjuna's bewilderment had reached its extreme limit.

In the devotional tradition it is rightly believed that so long as we, considering ourselves wise, keep on reasoning, the Lord remains entirely silent, as though unhearing; but the moment we abandon the pride of intellect and, overwhelmed with devotional feeling, go to Him in surrender with tear-filled eyes, the ocean of compassion that is the Lord arrives instantly, uninvited, to lead His devotee out of the darkness of ignorance toward the light of knowledge. In this emotional state, the living being requires the guidance and help of the Divine.

Having received the grace of the Divine, the devotee's inner faculty becomes pure and filled with bliss, which is essential for the direct experience of the self-luminous nature of consciousness. According to this accepted truth, and as is also the experience of devotees, we see in the Gita that as soon as the Lord began to speak, His blazing words fell upon Arjuna's mind like lightning, making him deeply ashamed of his mistaken notions.

Gentle words of sympathy could not have roused Arjuna's despairing mind. Hence, stirring Arjuna with words sharp as arrows dipped in the acid of sarcasm, the Lord finally says: rise up and act.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Again the Lord addresses Arjuna, in 'Klaibyam...': 'klaibyam' means the state of impotence, want of firmness. 'Ma sma gamah' means do not fall into it. O Partha, son of Pritha -- this is not fitting even in you, whose valour is renowned, who once fought even the great Lord Shiva Himself, of great glory. 'Kshudram' means the cause of pettiness. 'Hridaya-daurbalyam' means feebleness, faintheartedness of mind. Cast this aside and rise -- begin your engagement in battle. He is addressed as 'Parantapa,' one who scorches the enemy, in that very sense.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O Arjuna, son of Pritha! Do not fall into this impotence; for it is not proper in you. O Parantapa! Give up this petty weakness of heart and rise up for battle.

Explanation: 'Partha' -- to awaken, by reminding him of the message of his mother Pritha (Kunti), the kshatriya-befitting valour within Arjuna's inner being, the Lord addresses Arjuna by the name 'Partha.' The point is that by falling into cowardice you ought not to transgress your mother's command.

'Klaibyam ma sma gamah' -- Arjuna, out of cowardice, was regarding fighting as unrighteousness and not-fighting as righteousness. Hence, to alert Arjuna, the Lord says that not fighting is not a matter of dharma at all -- it is mere impotence (eunuch-like weakness). Therefore give up this impotence.

'Naitat tvayyupapadyate' -- this eunuch-like weakness ought never to have come upon you; for you are the son of a heroic kshatriya mother like Kunti, and are yourself a great hero. The point is that, both by birth and by your own nature, this impotence is wholly improper in you.

'Parantapa' -- you yourself are 'Parantapa,' that is, one who scorches, who routs the enemy -- so will you now, at this very moment, turn away from battle and gladden your enemies?

'Kshudram hridayadaurbalyam tyaktvottishtha' -- here the word 'kshudram' bears two meanings: (1) this weakness of heart leads only to pettiness -- that is, it grants neither liberation, nor heaven, nor fame. If you do not abandon this pettiness, you yourself will become petty; and (2) this weakness of heart is itself a petty, trifling thing. For a hero like you, abandoning such a trifling thing is no difficult task at all.

What you believe -- that you are righteous and do not wish to commit the sin of war -- is in fact merely a weakness, a frailty of your heart. Abandon it and rise up for battle -- that is, perform the duty that has come to you.

Here, before Arjuna stands the duty in the form of battle. Hence the Lord says: 'rise, stand up, and perform your duty in the form of battle.' In the Lord's mind there is not the slightest doubt regarding Arjuna's duty. He knows that, from every standpoint, fighting is indeed Arjuna's duty. Hence, paying no heed to Arjuna's hollow arguments, He at once commands him to perform his duty: rise up, fully prepared, for battle.

Transition: in the first chapter, Arjuna offered many arguments (pleas) for not fighting. Paying no regard whatsoever to those arguments, the Lord suddenly and forcefully rebuked Arjuna for the fault of cowardice and commanded him to rise for battle. At this, Arjuna too, finding no resolution for his own arguments, suddenly became agitated and spoke up--

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
One might object: seeing my own kinsmen's army, I have become so unsteady that I cannot even hold my bow -- what then can I possibly do? To this he answers: 'klaibyam' means the state of impotence, unsteadiness, the collapse of vigor, vitality and the like. 'Ma sma gamah' means do not fall into it. O Partha, son of Pritha -- since it is well known that supreme valour belongs to one who is the very son of her who received the boon of the gods, you are, precisely as the son of Pritha, unfit for impotence, is the sense. He states his unfitness for it as Arjuna too, in 'Naitat...': this does not befit -- is not proper in -- you, Arjuna, of renowned great glory, who once fought even directly with the great Lord Himself. By calling this very thing 'impotence,' its unfitness is indicated through its extraordinary character. One might object: have I not already said, 'I cannot remain steady, and my mind seems to be reeling'? -- anticipating this, he says, 'kshudram...': the weakness of heart, in the form of the mind's reeling and the like, is 'kshudram' because it is the cause of pettiness, or else it is easily dispelled. Having cast it aside through discernment, rise -- become prepared for battle. He is addressed as 'Parantapa,' one who scorches the enemy, in that very sense -- an address pregnant with reason.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Hence, in 'Klaibyam...': O Partha, do not fall into impotence, cowardice. For this is not fitting -- not proper -- in you. Cast aside this petty, trifling weakness of heart, this cowardice, and rise for battle. O Parantapa, scorcher of the enemy.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Even having heard this much, seeing that Arjuna still did not abandon his impotence, He speaks again, in 'Klaibyam...': cast aside the impotence -- the faintheartedness displayed through such statements as 'seeing these my own kinsmen, O Krishna...' and the rest. That is, do not accept it at all, is the sense. For this impotence does not befit you, Arjuna, of renowned prowess -- it is not proper. Hence cast aside this pettiness -- this weakness, this feebleness, this lack of vigour of heart, the very cause of pettiness -- and rise, become prepared for battle. Suggesting that My own nature is fitting in you, the son of My own paternal aunt Pritha, he addresses him, saying 'O Partha.' Since supreme valour is well known to belong to you, the mere son of her who received the boon of the gods through Pritha, you, as the son of Pritha, are unfit for impotence -- so say some. To convey 'scorch the enemy, not your own kinsmen who are your own well-wishers,' he says 'Parantapa.'

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Now begins the second chapter, whose subject is the removal of grief. Even though there is no break in Sanjaya's speech, the indication 'Sanjaya said' is given to forestall the doubt that this might be someone else's utterance, in the form of marking the start of a new chapter. He explains the three verses beginning 'Tam tatha...', in 'Evam...'. To indicate that everything up to 'vishidantam' is a recapitulation of what was stated in the previous chapter, it is said 'Evam upavishte Parthe' (Partha having thus sat down). 'Tatha' means 'in an improper place.' 'Kripa' is inner despondency; hence, being overcome even by outward grief, his eyes were filled and confused with tears, is the sense. 'Vishidantam' means having attained despondency in the manner described in the previous chapter. By the word 'Madhusudana,' his capacity to destroy the rajas and tamas that are the root of grief is suggested. 'Asthane' (out of place) carries the sense implied by the word 'vishama' (uneven, improper). 'Kashmalam' here is akin to a swoon; this is established from the earlier context, 'shoka-samvigna-manasah' (1.47). The words 'Arjuna,' 'Partha,' 'Parantapa' -- indicating his renowned lineage, valour, learning and the like -- carry, by virtue of his being Kunti's son, an implied reproach directed at you; this is the sense intended by the word 'akshipya' (having reproached). The word 'kutah' (whence) too, imputing causality to what is a mere semblance of a cause, carries an implied rebuke. 'Parantapa' means one who scorches the enemy. 'Klaibyam' here means cowardice; this is elaborated by the word 'hridaya-daurbalya' (weakness of heart). Since the qualifiers used in the previous verse also serve as grounds for the impropriety of cowardice, they are here brought into relevance in meaning, through the phrase 'tam imam vishamastham' and the rest. Those whose discernment has gone far from untruth are the 'aryas,' the wise; others are 'anaryas.' In the word 'asvargyam' (not conducive to heaven), the word 'svarga,' without qualification, denotes merely the next world in general. And since the negative prefix here, functioning to indicate opposition, opposes -- through the fruit intended by the word 'svargya' (conducive to heaven) -- the very thing the word denotes, it is rendered as 'contrary to the next world.' Since the word 'kshudra' (mean, petty) admits of no narrowing here that would restrict it to some relative pettiness, and considering that this quality is attributed to the greater Arjuna in such a state, the word 'atikshudram' (utterly mean) is used to show that a pettiness reaching its extreme limit is intended. Whether by figuratively transferring the cause's name to the effect, or by intending the fruit stated in the surrounding verses, the phrase 'hridaya-daurbalya-kritam' (produced by weakness of heart) is used, meaning: produced by unsteadiness of heart. By the word 'Parantapa,' the contextually intended meaning is conveyed, supplying the elliptical sense: 'rise up for battle.'

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
O son of Pritha, do not yield to this degrading impotence. It does not become you. Give up such petty weakness of heart and arise, O chastiser of the enemy.

 Arjuna was addressed as the son of Pritha, who happened to be the sister of Krishna's father Vasudeva. Therefore Arjuna had a blood relationship with Krishna. If the son of a kshatriya declines to fight, he is a kshatriya in name only, and if the son of a brahmana acts impiously, he is a brahmana in name only. Such kshatriyas and brahmanas are unworthy sons of their fathers; therefore, Krishna did not want Arjuna to become an unworthy son of a kshatriya. Arjuna was the most intimate friend of Krishna, and Krishna was directly guiding him on the chariot; but in spite of all these credits, if Arjuna abandoned the battle he would be committing an infamous act. Therefore Krishna said that such an attitude in Arjuna did not fit his personality. Arjuna might argue that he would give up the battle on the grounds of his magnanimous attitude for the most respectable Bhishma and his relatives, but Krishna considered that sort of magnanimity mere weakness of heart. Such false magnanimity was not approved by any authority. Therefore, such magnanimity or so-called nonviolence should be given up by persons like Arjuna under the direct guidance of Krishna.
