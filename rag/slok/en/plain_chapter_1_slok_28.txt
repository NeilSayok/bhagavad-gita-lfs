Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The symptoms that arise in the body of a mentally distressed person, owing to mental confusion, are described here in detail. What Sanjaya called 'compassion' -- its true nature becomes clear from Arjuna's own words. He says: 'seeing these kinsmen... my limbs tremble,' and so on. In modern psychology, a disorder with the above symptoms in an agitated, unbalanced person is given the name 'anxiety-induced state of despair.'

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
He now cites this very statement referred to by the word 'idam' (this), in 'Drishtva...': seeing his own family, present on the battlefield out of a desire for battle, he shows the onset of grief, in 'Sidanti...'. The sense is: Arjuna, though a portion of the Divine, being unaware of the Self, holding the false notion of self and possession regarding his own body and the bodies of others, and, dear as they were to him, foreseeing their death at the very outset of battle, grief overtook him greatly.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Arjuna said -- O Krishna! Seeing this assembly of kinsmen, eager for battle, present before me, my limbs are giving way and my mouth is drying up; my body is trembling and my hair is standing on end. The Gandiva bow is slipping from my hand, and my skin too is burning. My mind seems to be reeling, and I am unable even to stand.

Explanation: 'Drishtvemam svajanam krishna yuyutsum samupasthitam' -- the name 'Krishna' was very dear to Arjuna. This form of address occurs nine times in the Gita -- no other address for Lord Sri Krishna occurs so often. Likewise, the Lord too held Arjuna's name 'Partha' very dear. So the Lord and Arjuna would use these names with each other in conversation, and this fact was well known among people. It is with this in view that Sanjaya, at the end of the Gita, mentions the names 'Krishna' and 'Partha': 'Where Yogeshvara Krishna is, where Partha the bow-wielder is' (18.78).

Dhritarashtra had earlier said 'samaveta yuyutsavah' (assembled, eager to fight), and here Arjuna too says 'yuyutsum samupasthitam' (eager for battle, present) -- but there is a great difference in their two perspectives. In Dhritarashtra's view, Duryodhana and the rest are 'my sons,' and Yudhishthira and the rest are 'Pandu's sons' -- such a distinction exists; hence Dhritarashtra there said 'mamakah' and 'pandavah.' But in Arjuna's view this distinction does not exist; hence Arjuna here says 'svajanam' (my own people), which includes both sides. The point is: Dhritarashtra fears and grieves only over the possible death of his own sons in the war; but Arjuna grieves over the possible death of kinsmen on both sides -- whoever dies, on whichever side, is still our own kinsman.

The word 'drishtva' (having seen) has occurred three times so far: 'drishtva tu pandavanikam' (1.2), 'vyavasthitan drishtva dhartarashtran' (1.20), and here, 'drishtvemam svajanam' (1.28). The point of these three occurrences is: Duryodhana's seeing remained of one kind throughout -- his was purely a martial sentiment; but Arjuna's seeing was of two kinds. At first, seeing Dhritarashtra's sons, Arjuna, filled with valor, stood up, bow raised, ready for battle; but now, seeing his own kinsmen, he is overcome with faint-heartedness, withdraws from battle, and the bow slips from his hand.

'Sidanti mama gatrani...bhramativa cha me manah' -- anxiety over the war's coming outcome fills Arjuna's mind, causing him great sorrow, and the effect of that anxiety and sorrow spreads through his entire body. Arjuna describes this effect in plain words: each limb of my body -- hands, feet, mouth and the rest -- is giving way! My mouth is drying up, making it hard even to speak! My whole body is trembling! All the hair on my body is standing on end -- my whole body has horripilation! The Gandiva bow, whose very twang strikes fear into enemies, is today slipping from my hand! My skin -- my whole body -- is burning! My mind is reeling -- I cannot even think what I ought to do! I am unable even to stand here on the battlefield, in the chariot! It feels as though I shall faint and collapse! Even standing in such a calamitous war feels like a sin.

Transition: having described these eight symptoms of grief in his body in the previous verse, Arjuna now, in terms of the ominous portents foretelling the outcome, explains why it is improper to fight.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Sanjaya now introduces Arjuna's words spoken to the Lord, in 'Arjuna uvacha...' etc., following the earlier text 'having thus spoken, Arjuna, on the battlefield...'. There, that which obstructs the knowledge of the Self -- the very cause impelling one's own dharma -- and which produces, in Arjuna, unaware of the Self, holding the false notion of self and possession regarding his own body and others', the sight of the impending destruction of both his own kinsmen and the enemy's through battle, brought forth a great grief; this is shown by describing its symptoms, across three verses. Seeing this, his own family, desiring battle and present on the battlefield, as he stood there -- that is, as he looked on -- 'my limbs give way and my mouth dries up completely' -- the use of the intensive prefix 'pari' (entirely) is meant to convey a degree of grief exceeding that caused by mere fatigue and the like.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Overcome, filled with the deepest compassion -- born of affection -- and, thinking 'alas, these fathers and other kinsmen will be slain,' becoming despondent, he spoke this forthcoming statement -- so it is said. He now cites this very statement referred to by 'idam,' in 'Drishtva...': having seen with his own eyes his own kinsmen -- his family circle -- present, truly desiring battle, standing properly arrayed on the battlefield -- not merely arrived out of some ordinary battle-eagerness -- 'my limbs give way' -- this is the construal, connecting with what follows.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Venkatanatha now states the condensed sense of the remainder of the chapter, in 'sa tu...': the word 'tu' (but) signals the contrast between Duryodhana as described earlier and the character of Partha about to be described. His epithet 'high-souled' (mahamana) conveys that, out of unwillingness to slay even enemies who bear the mere designation of kinsmen, he counts victory -- even sovereignty over all three worlds -- as no more than a blade of grass (as he says later, 'I do not desire victory,' 1.31). That he cannot bear even his enemies' suffering is conveyed by 'supremely compassionate' ('overcome by the deepest compassion'). The phrase 'fathers, then grandfathers' -- expanded by the list 'teachers, fathers, sons' (1.34) and the like -- conveys the sheer breadth of those toward whom his affection extends, expressed through this long compound; or, the sense is that even when a kinsman does one grave wrong, one's bond with him does not loosen. (Compare 'all these kinsmen, indeed our own people,' 1.37.) Since even those on the side of the aggressors -- teachers and the rest -- are, on reflection, not to be slain, and since Arjuna foresees the chain of unrighteousness born of the destruction of a family, he is called 'supremely righteous.' His intent is that any sanction to slay an aggressor applies only to persons other than teachers and the like. 'Sabhratrika' (along with his brothers) conveys that this is not Arjuna alone, but all the Pandavas together, feeling this way -- this gathers up all the plural forms used later ('us,' 'we,' 'by us,' etc.). Or, the sense is: it is not merely out of disregard for personal injury that he overlooks their killing him, but even in the face of wrongs done to Dharmaraja, Draupadi and others -- for whom he has the deepest reverence, affection and compassion, as though they stood in the place of his own teachers. Arjuna's intent is that no fault of killing a teacher should fall even upon his brothers. To indicate that they are indeed to be slain, the word 'ghnatah api' ('even while being slain,' 1.35) is used. This is elaborated in 'bhavadbhih...' and the like; the term 'aggressor' (atatayin) is also explained there, with reference to acts such as the burning of the house of lac. As the law-books say: 'The poisoner, the arsonist, the one who attacks with weapon in hand, the plunderer of wealth, the seizer of land, and the abductor of another's wife -- these six are aggressors' (Manu 8.350); and 'one may slay an approaching aggressor without hesitation; no fault whatever attaches to the slayer of an aggressor' (Manu 8.351). The repeated use of 'et cetera' shows that such grounds for being deemed an aggressor had been committed by them many times over, and had not ceased even now -- as indicated by the present-tense phrase 'even while being slain' (Mahabharata 1.1.14). By the word 'bhavadbhih' ('by you all'), Arjuna implicates even Dhritarashtra, who out of fatherly affection consents to and is thus complicit with the wrathful Duryodhana ('I follow the deluded, wrathful Duryodhana,' Mahabharata 1.1.145). Thus it is shown that Duryodhana and the rest, their minds overpowered by excessive greed, stand in stark contrast to what is meant by 'high-souled.' The phrase 'Supreme Person' is used to produce grief at the certainty that Dhritarashtra's people, including allies like Shakuni and Karna, are doomed to be slain regardless. The compound may mean 'one whose ally is the Supreme Person,' or 'one who is merely the instrument, the Supreme Person being the true agent' -- as the Lord will later say, 'these have already been slain by Me; be merely the instrument, O ambidextrous one' (11.33). And Arjuna, even before this, had determined that the mere presence of the unarmed Supreme Person was the true cause of victory, surpassing even the presence of a thousand mighty armies, and had chosen Him for that very reason. To indicate that this is not the proper occasion for such affection, the phrase 'seeing your own people' is used. The passage beginning 'out of affection for kinsmen' and the rest shows that Arjuna's dejection was unlike Duryodhana's -- his was not born of hatred toward kin, cruelty, or fear of the enemy, nor from any weakness in one who has the Supreme Person as his ally. The sense of the passage from 'the limbs sink' (1.28) through 'the mind' (1.30) is condensed with the word 'excessively,' etc. 'Sakhin' means age-mates; 'suhridah' means well-wishers irrespective of age. 'Senayorubhayorapi' conveys that nearly all of these relations were found, for the most part, within a single army -- i.e., looking at them thoughtfully, as befits scriptural and worldly propriety. 'Sarvan bandhun' conveys that not a single kinsman remained absent from that gathering.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Arjuna said: My dear Kṛṣṇa, seeing my friends and relatives present before me in such a fighting spirit, I feel the limbs of my body quivering and my mouth drying up.

 Any man who has genuine devotion to the Lord has all the good qualities which are found in godly persons or in the demigods, whereas the nondevotee, however advanced he may be in material qualifications by education and culture, lacks in godly qualities. As such, Arjuna, just after seeing his kinsmen, friends and relatives on the battlefield, was at once overwhelmed by compassion for them who had so decided to fight amongst themselves. As far as his soldiers were concerned, he was sympathetic from the beginning, but he felt compassion even for the soldiers of the opposite party, foreseeing their imminent death. And while he was so thinking, the limbs of his body began to quiver, and his mouth became dry. He was more or less astonished to see their fighting spirit. Practically the whole community, all blood relatives of Arjuna, had come to fight with him. This overwhelmed a kind devotee like Arjuna. Although it is not mentioned here, still one can easily imagine that not only were Arjuna’s bodily limbs quivering and his mouth drying up, but he was also crying out of compassion. Such symptoms in Arjuna were not due to weakness but to his softheartedness, a characteristic of a pure devotee of the Lord. It is said therefore: yasyāsti bhaktir bhagavaty akiñcanā sarvair guṇais tatra samāsate surāḥ harāv abhaktasya kuto mahad-guṇā mano-rathenāsati dhāvato bahiḥ “One who has unflinching devotion for the Personality of Godhead has all the good qualities of the demigods. But one who is not a devotee of the Lord has only material qualifications that are of little value. This is because he is hovering on the mental plane and is certain to be attracted by the glaring material energy.” ( Bhāg. 5.18.12)
