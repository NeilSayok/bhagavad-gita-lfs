Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The finite intellect of man is not a suitable instrument to perceive the infinite glory of the Universal Form in a single sweep of vision. The sheer vastness of the dimension of this form and its profound significance are enough to make human intellect stagger. The Lord is the sole Truth-Principle that exists as the Consciousness behind the organs of action of all living beings. Arjuna indicates this in these words: 'I see You with many arms, bellies, mouths, and eyes, of infinite forms everywhere.' This should not be misunderstood as a caricature of Truth. A word of caution is necessary for those hasty painters who, inspired by this theme, try to paint this Cosmic Form with their colors and brushes, and fail miserably in their attempt. The unity of the universe is not an object of sight; it is a fact of Truth to be realized. This fact is confirmed by Arjuna's words: 'I see neither Your end, nor middle, nor beginning.' The Cosmic Person could not have been described in a better way. These verses reveal that underlying unity which runs through all finite objects and mortal beings, holding them together like a thread in a garland. Arjuna further says:

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
The word 'aneka' (many) denotes 'infinite'. The Lord Himself will say later, 'of infinite arms' (11.19) and 'with hands and feet everywhere' (13.13) etc. In the Rigveda Khila, it is said: 'With eyes everywhere, mouth everywhere, arms everywhere, and feet everywhere. He, the one God, creating heaven and earth, fanns them with His arms and wings.' And in the Yajurveda: 'With eyes everywhere, mouth everywhere, arms everywhere, and feet everywhere. He, the one God, creating heaven and earth, bends them with His arms and wings.' The word 'visva' also denotes infinite, as it is declared: 'Sarvam (all), samastam (whole), visvam (universe), and anantam (infinite) all mean complete/infinite.' And in the Babhravya branch of the Samaveda: 'He is of infinite arms, infinite feet, infinite forms, and many mouths, yet He is one.' The description of greatness also applies due to His being the self of everything. Otherwise, statements like 'beginningless supreme Brahman' (13.12) would be inconsistent. In one context, He is called 'anantarupah' (of infinite forms) because His forms are infinite. In another context, He is of immeasurable size (aparimana). Both are stated in the Yajurveda: 'That which is higher than the highest, greater than the great, the one, unmanifest, of infinite forms.' Due to the infinity of the unmanifest itself, the immeasurability of the great is established. 'The Pradhana (material nature) exists enveloping the great. But there is no end or count of the infinite One,' as stated in the Aditya Purana. Moreover, each of His forms is infinite. 'His bodies, which are embodiments of knowledge, are countless and devoid of all measure,' in the Rigveda Khila. 'As vast as this outer space is, so vast is the space within the heart. Both heaven and earth are contained within it, both fire and wind, both sun and moon' (Chandogya Upanishad 8.1.3). And in the Bhagavata (10.16.31): 'With his hood-umbrella shattered by the stamping of Krishna's heels, Kaliya was exhausted by the weight of the universe within Krishna's abdomen.' And this is not inconsistent, because Ishvara possesses inconceivable power (achintya-shakti). 'One should not apply logic to those things which are inconceivable,' as in the Sri Vishnu Purana. 'This understanding cannot be attained by logic,' says the Katha Upanishad (1.2.9). Fallacy of over-extension of logic (atiprasanga) is to be discarded by virtue of the supreme purport and the strength of the scriptural statements. A thing established by valid proof (pramana) is not rejected merely because it is not seen elsewhere like a pot. In certain matters, the scriptural arrangement is accepted because of the existence of inconceivable nature. 'In God there are qualities, heard of (in scriptures) and highly contradictory, and even unheard of, without doubt. Likewise, faults, both conceivable and inconceivable, though spoken of (by the ignorant), are not perceived as such by the wise. Thus, in the Supreme Lord and elsewhere, there is a systematic arrangement of qualities and non-qualities (faults) that are heard or unheard,' according to the Jabali Khila Shruti. To avoid the description being considered figurative (upachara), the text says 'no middle' (na madhyam). Otherwise, it would have been established by merely saying no beginning and no end. 'Visvarupah means of complete/perfect form. He is Visvarupa, of undiminished form. Therefore, He is of infinite form, and indeed there is no destruction for Him,' according to the Shandilya Shakha.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O Cosmic Form, O Lord of the universe, I see You with many arms, bellies, mouths and eyes, and of infinite forms on all sides. I see neither Your beginning, nor middle, nor end.

Commentary: 'Vishvarupa', 'Vishveshvara' — The significance of these two addresses is that whatever is visible to me is You alone, and You are also the Lord of this universe. The bodies of worldly humans are insentient (jada) and the indweller (shariri) in them is conscious (chetana); but in Your Cosmic Form, there are no such two divisions as body and indweller. In the Cosmic Form, You alone exist as both the body and the indweller. Therefore, in the Cosmic Form, everything is purely spiritual (chinmaya). The implication is that by addressing Him as 'Vishvarupa', Arjuna says that You are the body, and by addressing Him as 'Vishveshvara', he says that You are the indweller (the Lord of the body).

'anekabāhūdaravaktranetram' — When I look towards Your hands, Your hands are also many; when I look towards Your bellies, Your bellies are also many; when I look towards Your mouths, Your mouths are also many; and when I look towards Your eyes, Your eyes are also many. The meaning is that there is no end to Your hands, bellies, mouths and eyes; they are all infinite.

'paśyāmi tvāṃ sarvato'nantarūpam' — You appear infinite on all sides in terms of space, time, object, person, substance, etc.

'nāntaṃ na madhyaṃ na punastavādim' — Where is Your end, it is not known; where is Your middle, it is not known; and where is Your beginning, it is not known.

The purpose of saying 'nāntam' (no end) first seems to be that when someone looks at something, their gaze first goes to the limit of that object to see how far it extends. For example, on seeing a book, the gaze first goes to its boundary to see its length and width. Similarly, on seeing the Cosmic Form of the Lord, Arjuna's gaze first went towards its boundary (end). When Arjuna did not see its end, then his gaze went to the middle part, and then towards the beginning (origin); but nowhere could he find the end, middle, or beginning of the Cosmic Form. Therefore, in this verse, the sequence 'nāntaṃ na madhyaṃ na punastavādim' has been maintained.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Since the word 'aneka' (many) in 'aneka-bahu-udara-vaktra-netram' can denote two or more, he says 'aneke' (infinite) to state the intended meaning. By this, 'aneka-vaktra-nayanam' is also explained. To the question 'how so?', he says 'ananta' (infinite). The meaning of 'sarvatah' (all around) is to be understood from the Tatparya Nirnaya, etc., as will be explained later. In the verses 'with arms... and with wings...', the words 'dhayati' and 'namati' apply to each. The word 'visva' also denotes infinite, as it is declared: 'Sarvam (all), samastam (whole), visvam (universe), and anantam (infinite) all mean complete/infinite.' To show a clear meaning, he quotes the Shruti: 'anantabahum'. If the Lord is of infinite arms, etc., then He must be of supreme great dimension. Otherwise that would not fit. But in the Gita: 'Your great form' (11.23) and 'many mouths and eyes' (11.23) only speak of greatness and manyness; therefore, the statement of infinite arms, etc., seems inconsistent. To resolve this, he says: 'mahattvad' (due to greatness). This contradiction would exist if only greatness of form and only manyness of limbs were meant. But it is not so, because there is no limiting condition (avacchedaka) heard. The description of greatness and manyness is consistent with His being of supreme great dimension and having infinite limbs. In supreme greatness, greatness is included, and in infinity, manyness is included. If it is asked 'what is the harm in taking intermediate greatness, etc., even if no limiting condition is specified?', he says: 'anyatha' (otherwise). In that case, the statements of supreme greatness and infinite limbs would be violated. To show that the two words 'anantarupa' in 'I see You of infinite forms on all sides' (11.16) and 'the universe is pervaded by You of infinite forms' (11.38) have different meanings to indicate His great glory, he says: 'ekatra tu' (in one place...). In one place, 'anantarupah' means one whose forms are infinite in number. In the other place, it means one of immeasurable dimension (aparimana). Both are stated in the Yajurveda: 'That which is higher than the highest, greater than the great, the one, unmanifest, of infinite forms.' Due to the infinity of the unmanifest itself, the immeasurability of the great is established. 'The Pradhana (material nature) exists enveloping the great. But there is no end or count of the infinite One,' as stated in the Aditya Purana. Moreover, each of His forms is infinite. 'His bodies, which are embodiments of knowledge, are countless and devoid of all measure,' in the Rigveda Khila. 'As vast as this outer space is, so vast is the space within the heart' means the Supreme Self situated within the heart is as vast as the outer space. 'Garbhe' (in the womb) means He who contains the universe in His stomach. 'Avasannah' means bent down. By containing the universe in the stomach, His unlimited dimension is obtained. Now, those who say that Krishna etc. are of unlimited dimension must explain: is the perception of their small dimension an illusion or valid knowledge? If the first, the two-armed or four-armed forms and their actions would be false. If the second, the coexistence of small and large dimensions in the same place at the same time is illogical. Therefore, the statement of unlimited dimension is only figurative. If this is said, he asks: what is this illogicality of having multiple dimensions? Is it because it is not seen elsewhere and hence impossible? Or is He not endowed with small and large dimensions because He is a substance, or is it contrary to the logic that He is not of supreme great dimension because He is of small dimension like a cloth? Or is it due to the fallacy of over-extension, or is it due to lack of proof? Not the first, he says: 'na ca' (and not...). For what is impossible to Him who is endowed with inconceivable power? Not the second, because Ishvara possesses power beyond the scope of logic: 'achintyah' (inconceivable). This refers to Brahman. 'Apaneya' means to be set aside. The over-extension, if it is argued that if Ishvara has contradictory dimensions He would be both sorrowless and sorrowful, is this said concerning Ishvara, or concerning others like Prana, etc.? If the first, he refutes it: 'atiprasangas tu' (but the over-extension...). This over-extension is based on non-discrimination. But this is not so, because the great purport of the Shrutis is that Lord Hari is of faultless infinite qualities, which contradicts sorrow, while the two dimensions do not contradict Him. Sorrow is unauthoritative, whereas the two dimensions are established by the cited scriptural statements. The consequence (prasanga) here does not lead to contradiction: 'na hi' (for not...). Just as the objection 'if a pot has a broad belly, it would not be a substance because no such substance is seen elsewhere' is invalid, and yet it is a substance, so also here. By this, the fallacious nature of the inferences is also declared. He refutes the second (concerning others like Prana): 'kesucit' (in some...). Here too, there is a distinction based on the presence or absence of the cause of possibility, so non-discrimination is unestablished. He refutes the fourth (lack of proof): 'gunah' (qualities...). Qualities heard and unheard, highly contradictory and non-contradictory, exist in God. And faults, heard or unheard, do not exist at all, but are only perceived as such by the ignorant. Thus is the state in the Supreme Lord; in others, there is a systematic arrangement of qualities and faults. Thus, since there is no impossibility, it is not to be assumed that the statement of unlimited dimension in the Lord is figurative. Arjuna also refuted that, as he says: 'upacaratva' (to avoid figurativeness). To avoid the description of having no beginning and no end being considered figurative, the text says 'no middle' (na madhyam). Why should it not be for the negation of middle itself? He says: 'anyathe' (otherwise). If it were not to avoid figurativeness, it would be redundant. Why? Because the middle is dependent on the beginning and the end, and by negating them, its negation is already established. He refutes the idea that 'visvarupa' means one whose form is the universe: 'visvarupa' (Cosmic Form). This is to be understood as repeated.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
He qualifies the possessor of the body in whose body everything was seen. I see You who have many arms, etc., and who have infinite forms everywhere. I do not see Your beginning, end, or middle. To indicate that seeing the universe in Your body is appropriate because You are of the Cosmic Form, the address 'O Cosmic Form' (visvarupa) is used. To show that although the universe has a beginning, middle, and end, You do not have them, even though You are of the Cosmic Form, because You are the Lord of the universe (controller of the universe), the address 'O Lord of the universe' (visvesvara) is used.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
If connected as 'infinite forms on all sides', the word 'sarvatah' (on all sides) would be redundant. But connecting it as 'I see on all sides' (sarvatah pasyami) is appropriate because it is in accordance with his acquisition of the divine eye. With the intention that the word 'visvesvara' acts as the cause of His being the Cosmic Form, he says 'controller of the universe'. Indeed, the controller of the pervaded object is the embodied self (shariri). In the phrase 'neither end nor middle', the non-perception of something that actually exists is not meant, for that would be inconsistent with Arjuna's acquisition of the divine eye. Rather, as in other contexts, due to the attribute 'anantarupam' which is pregnant with the cause, the non-perception is stated because of the non-existence of the object itself (i.e. limits), like a hare's horn. With this intention he says 'yatah' (since). This is also intended by the word 'visvarupa'. Here, the negation of beginning, middle, and end should be understood in terms of space and time. Due to the absence of limitation by beginning and end, the absence of the middle, which is defined by those two, is also established.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
To prevent delusion, he says 'I see You, in whose body I see these' — 'aneka' etc. You who have many arms, etc. I see You who have infinite forms on all sides. Even of You who are of such manifold forms, I see neither the end (fullness/limit), nor the middle (state of sustenance), nor the beginning (origin). O Lord of the universe, O protector of the universe, what more to say, I see the Cosmic Form!

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
That in which there are many (infinite) arms, bellies, mouths, and eyes is 'aneka-bahu-udara-vaktra-netram'. One whose form is infinite (unlimited) all around, i.e., in the four directions, above, and below.
