Speaker: Arjuna

Slok:
arjuna uvāca .
kathaṃ bhīṣmamahaṃ saṅkhye droṇaṃ ca madhusūdana .
iṣubhiḥ pratiyotsyāmi pūjārhāvarisūdana

Life Application:
Acknowledge how personal respect and emotional ties can make doing the right thing feel deeply painful.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The reasoning that has led Arjuna's aim astray, born of cowardice, could seem proper only to an unreflective mind. These arguments of Arjuna's are meaningless to a person who, without losing self-mastery, understands the situation correctly. For such a person, such circumstances create no real problem. In truth, viewed properly, this war is not occurring because of personal enmity between two individuals. At this moment, Arjuna has no existence apart from the Pandava army, nor do Bhishma and Drona have any separate existence -- they are simply part of the Kaurava army. It is on account of certain principles that these two armies now stand arrayed against each other. The Kauravas, adopting the policy of unrighteousness, are ready for war; the Pandavas, on the other side, are eager for war for the sake of the righteous policy expounded in the Hindu scriptures.

Since dharma is the superior cause, and since both armies are but expressions of collective conviction, Arjuna had no right to feel personal reverence or disrespect, nor did he have the right to insist on honouring or dishonouring any particular individual among the upholders of unrighteousness. Failing to view the entire situation from this standpoint, Arjuna regards himself as a separate individual, and it is through that very egotistic viewpoint that he views the entire situation. Arjuna sees himself as Drona's disciple and Bhishma's grandson, while Guru Drona and grandfather Bhishma too were looking at Arjuna, yet no such sentiment arose in their minds -- because they had forgotten their individual personalities and established their complete identity with the Kaurava side. In brief, it may be said that it was Arjuna's own ego that was the cause of his false notions and confusion.

On this portion of the Gita I have discussed with several eminent persons of our country and found that they regard Arjuna's argument as proper and just. This shows that this is an extremely subtle matter, whose resolution is of the utmost necessity. Perhaps Vyasa considered that, for the guidance of future generations, this knot should be untangled through Hindu philosophy. The greater our identification with the small, limited ego called 'I,' the greater the problems and confusion that will enter our lives. When this ego expands and identifies itself with some army, ideal nation, or age, moral weakness and the like begin to erode. A fully moral life can be lived only by that person who has recognized his own pure Self-nature, which is one, non-dual, all-pervading, and present within all names and forms. We shall see further on that Lord Sri Krishna imparts this very truth as a remedy for curing Arjuna's mental malady.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Thus, though being awakened by the Lord, Arjuna, his mind still overcome by grief, remaining unawakened, replied to the Lord present before him with his own view alone, in 'Katham...' and the rest: Bhishma, the grandfather, and Drona, the teacher -- O Madhusudana -- with whom it would be improper even to fight in mere words, in battle, how could I fight with arrows against them, is the sense. How could I fight, with arrows, against them, since they are worthy of worship, deserving of adoration with flowers and the like? He addresses the Lord as 'Arisudana' -- one who has effortlessly slain all enemies.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Arjuna said: O Madhusudana! How shall I fight in battle against Bhishma and Drona with arrows? For, O Arisudana, both of them are worthy of worship.

Explanation: 'Madhusudana' and 'Arisudana' -- the purpose of these two addresses is: You are the slayer of demons and of enemies -- that is, You have slain even demons of wicked nature, such as Madhu and Kaitabha, who committed unrighteous acts and caused suffering to the world; and You have also slain enemies who bore causeless hatred and did harm. But before me now stand grandfather Bhishma and teacher Drona, who are supremely excellent in conduct, who bear the deepest affection for me, and who have lovingly instructed me. How could I possibly kill such a grandfather and teacher, my own greatest well-wishers?

'Katham bhishmam aham sankhye dronam cha' -- I am not turning away from battle out of cowardice; rather, I am turning away from battle upon seeing dharma. But You say, 'from where has this cowardice, this impotence, come upon you!' Just consider: how could I possibly fight with arrows against grandfather Bhishma and teacher Drona? O Great King, this is not my cowardice. Cowardice would be if I feared dying. I do not fear dying -- rather, I fear killing.

In this world there are two principal kinds of relationship -- the relationship of birth and the relationship of learning. By the relationship of birth, grandfather Bhishma is worthy of our worship. Since childhood I have grown up in his very lap. When, as a child, I would call him 'Father, Father,' he would lovingly say, 'I am even the father of your father!' In this way he has always held great love and affection for me. By the relationship of learning, teacher Drona is worthy of our worship. He is my teacher in the martial arts. His affection for me is such that he did not even teach his own son Ashvatthama as he taught me. He taught the use of the Brahmastra to both of us, but he taught only me, and not his own son, how to withdraw it. He has also granted me this boon: 'Among my disciples, none shall ever surpass you in the art of weaponry.' Before such worshipful figures as grandfather Bhishma and teacher Drona, even to address them harshly in speech, saying 'you' in a disrespectful tone, would be a sin equivalent to killing them -- how then great a sin it would be to fight against them with arrows, with the intent to kill!

'Ishubhih prati yotsyami pujarhau' -- being my elders by relationship, both grandfather Bhishma and teacher Drona are revered and worshipful. They have complete authority over me. Hence, they may strike me, but how could I strike them with arrows? To become their adversary and fight against them is, for me, a matter of grave sin! For both of them are deserving of my service, and even more than service, deserving of my worship. How could I kill such venerable persons with arrows?

Transition: in the previous verse, Arjuna, agitated, declared his own resolve to the Lord. Now, the Lord's words having had their effect, Arjuna, weighing his own resolve against the Lord's, says--

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Arjuna said: Again Partha, agitated by affection, compassion, and fear of both righteousness and unrighteousness, not knowing what the Lord had spoken for his own highest good, said this: How shall I slay Bhishma, Drona and the rest, my greatly esteemed teachers? How much more so should I, having slain them, then enjoy those very pleasures which they themselves once enjoyed, having sprinkled those pleasures with their blood, and having seated myself upon their very seats, partake of them!

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
Continuing His rebuke through terms such as 'impotence' and the rest, and showing that this is 'a false conviction of righteousness in what is actually unrighteous,' and so on, He introduces Arjuna's reply, in 'Katham...': through 'How shall I, in battle, fight against Bhishma and Drona' and the rest, and through 'should I then enjoy those pleasures,' Arjuna, in his opening argument (purvapaksha), signals as objectionable both a fixation on the particular nature of the act and a fixation on the particular nature of its fruit. Through 'we do not know this' he likewise states a fixation on the particular nature of the act. Action undertaken without any such fixation is simply not possible. Nor does one engage in battle intending defeat. Yet even victory here would be nothing but calamity for us. Hence he says: without slaying our elders, it would be better even to live by begging alms. And this itself cannot be resolved -- whether we should desire victory or desire defeat -- since even in victory there is the destruction of our own kinsmen.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Having heard this, Arjuna, agitated by affection, compassion, and dharma, not properly understanding the Lord's words, said, in 'Katham...': by addressing Him as 'Arisudana' (slayer of enemies), he suggests: You too have never acted thus in slaying an enemy.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
One might object: this is not an abandonment of my own duty out of grief and delusion; rather, I am abandoning this battle because it lacks the character of dharma and possesses the character of unrighteousness -- Arjuna, not grasping the Lord's true intent, now presents his own view, in 'Bhishmam...': how shall I strike, with arrows, in battle, Bhishma the grandfather and Drona the teacher -- not in any way whatsoever, is the sense. For both are worthy of worship, deserving of adoration with flowers and the like. With those very persons deserving of worship, even a playful mock-battle undertaken merely for amusement in some sporting ground -- even conducted in mere words -- would be improper; how much more so an actual battle on the battlefield, with arrows, whose very fruit is the abandonment of life! The double address 'Madhusudana' and 'Arisudana' arises from grief-stricken confusion, from an inability to properly attend to what precedes and follows; hence there is no fault of redundancy in the meaning of 'Madhusudana' being repeated. That even mere battle is improper here, let alone actual slaying, is suggested by the very word 'prati-yotsyami' (shall I fight against). Or: how shall I fight against those worthy of worship? He then specifies exactly who are worthy of worship: Bhishma and Drona. The construction is like 'feed the two brahmins, Devadatta and Yajnadatta.' The sense is this: Duryodhana and the rest would never prepare for battle without placing Bhishma and Drona at their forefront. Hence, battle with them is, in the first place, not dharma at all, since it is unenjoined, like the performance of worship and the like [without proper qualification]. Nor should it be said that, being unforbidden, it is therefore also not adharma -- for even mere verbal disrespect toward a teacher, such as addressing him rudely, has been forbidden through the demonstration of its evil consequence; how much more, then, is battle against them forbidden and unrighteous!

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
One might object: it is enemies who deserve to be scorched, not one's own teachers -- with this very intent, Arjuna says, in 'Katham...': how shall I fight against Bhishma the grandfather and Drona the teacher of the science of the bow, with arrows, in the battlefield of war -- how shall I, becoming their adversary, act thus? For both Bhishma and Drona are worthy of worship. How could I possibly slay with arrows those two who are worthy of worship with flowers and the like? By addressing Him as 'Madhusudana' and 'Arisudana,' he suggests: You Yourself scorch only the wicked -- hence it is not fitting for You to urge me to slay my innocent teachers, Bhishma and Drona, who are not wicked at all. The double address 'Madhusudana' and 'Arisudana' arises from grief-stricken confusion, from an inability to properly attend to what precedes and follows; hence, some say, there is no fault of redundancy in the meaning of 'Madhusudana' being repeated.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Now, since the beginning of battle enjoined by the Lord is not recognized as truly beneficial -- being the cause, through its proper sequence, of the highest good -- Arjuna's speech, which is a rejection of this, arises; and such ignorance is rooted in confusion caused by misplaced affection and the like. Explaining this, he introduces the reply, in 'Punarapi...': since the subject matter is the same as before, it is said 'again he spoke thus.' In the verse beginning 'Katham...', the word 'cha' (and) indicates an unstated further inclusion, or, through the word 'adi' (and so on), indicates that what is taken up is merely representative of what is not explicitly stated. The word 'gurun' (teachers, elders) used in the following verse is drawn back and applied here, since it conveys the sense of 'worthy of high regard' intended by the word 'pujarhau' (worthy of worship). 'Bahumantavyan' (greatly esteemed) means 'persons of great distinction,' either drawn from the following verse's 'mahanubhavan,' or else they are inherently greatly esteemed in themselves -- by virtue of being a grandfather, a teacher of the science of the bow, and the like, they are exceedingly esteemed, is the sense. Ceasing even the worship of those worthy of worship with flowers and the like would itself be a bold act; to slay them would be an act of extreme boldness. One ought to fight, out of devotion to one's teachers, against those who oppose them -- not against the teachers themselves. This is the sense behind 'how shall I fight against my teachers with arrows?' By the word 'aham' (I), his renowned lineage and the like are implied. That 'I shall fight against them with arrows' carries the sense of fighting through to the point of slaying is clarified by the following verse, where it is stated 'I shall slay.' By the two words 'Madhusudana' and 'Arisudana' it is suggested: are You not also the slayer of Sandipani and others [your own teachers]? The infinitive 'chartum' (to wander, to beg) here carries only the general sense of the action, not a sense tied to any specific verb requiring an object. Although some means of livelihood must indeed be resorted to, still, compared to the pleasures obtained through the slaying of one's teachers, even resorting to begging alms -- a duty proper to another class -- would in this world be preferable, more truly praiseworthy. This is because the suffering in the next world resulting from the slaying of teachers of such great influence is exceedingly great, is the sense. To dispel any illusion of a meaning contrary to the context, and owing to the connection with the word 'katham' (how) used in the previous verse, as well as the extreme cruelty involved, and the contrast indicated by the word 'tu' (but), the word 'kathantaram' (how much more so) is used. This is an optative form used in the sense of censure, per the grammatical rule governing such usage. Here, to prevent confusion arising from a compound construed as a dvandva and the like, the phrase 'bhogeshv atimatra-prasaktan' is explained through both components of the compound taken separately. The analysis is: 'those whose desire is fixed upon objects of enjoyment' -- following the rule that an exocentric bahuvrihi compound ending in a word denoting birth and the like is not to be avoided. Alternatively: 'artha-kamah' means 'those who desire wealth'; were they free from such desire, then the seizing of their enjoyments too might be tolerable -- but this is rather like snatching food from the hungry, is the sense. To convey an even greater cruelty than mere slaying, the meaning is established through words such as 'bhoga' (enjoyment) and 'rudhira' (blood). The word 'tu' (but) indicates the distinctive point conveyed through 'taih' (by them) and the rest. The word 'ihaiva' (in this very place) shows the intended extremity of cruelty, as indicated in 'teshu' (among them) and the rest. The enjoyments obtained through the slaying of one's teachers become, as it were, difficult to partake of, on their own account, since they perpetually recall the blood-stained memory of the teacher -- hence even worldly happiness is absent; this is the intent behind the word 'blood-stained.' For sprinkling itself, when consumed by oneself, becomes the cause of another's eating as well -- here both of these are reversed, is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having thus heard the Lord's uplifting words, Arjuna, wishing to convey to the Lord that 'I have not withdrawn from battle out of cowardice, but out of righteous conviction,' said this, through six verses beginning with 'Katham...': O Madhusudana -- protector of devotees, through the slaying of the demon Madhu and the establishment of Mathura -- how shall I, in battle, fight, with arrows, against Bhishma and Drona -- that is, how shall I fight against them in opposition? Since Bhishma is a devotee, his death would be improper; and since Drona too is a teacher, this is likewise improper -- this is conveyed through the phrase 'and Drona.' Both Bhishma and Drona are worthy of worship, in the manner previously described. O Arisudana -- slayer of enemies -- through this address, it is conveyed: these two are devotees and brahmins, not enemies; how then do You urge me toward the act of slaying them?

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
One might object: enemies, or those of inherently wicked nature, deserve to be scorched -- but not one's kinsmen and virtuous elders. Arjuna says, in 'Katham...': by addressing Him as 'Madhusudana' and 'Arisudana,' he suggests: since You Yourself scorch only enemies -- even the wicked -- it is not fitting for You to tell me to slay Bhishma and Drona, my innocent, worthy-of-worship teachers. Some say this double address, though carrying the same meaning, arises from the speaker's agitation due to grief, and therefore does not constitute the fault of redundancy. By the word 'ishubhih' (with arrows), the sense is: it would be impossible even to fight against them merely in words -- how much more so with arrows!

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Arjuna said: O killer of enemies, O killer of Madhu, how can I counterattack with arrows in battle men like Bhishma and Drona, who are worthy of my worship?

 Respectable superiors like Bhishma the grandfather and Dronacharya the teacher are always worshipable. Even if they attack, they should not be counterattacked. It is general etiquette that superiors are not to be offered even a verbal fight. Even if they are sometimes harsh in behavior, they should not be harshly treated. Then, how is it possible for Arjuna to counterattack them? Would Krishna ever attack His own grandfather, Ugrasena, or His teacher, Sandipani Muni? These were some of the arguments offered by Arjuna to Krishna.
