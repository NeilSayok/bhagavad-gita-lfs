Life Application:
Recognize how living creatures speed into the flaming mouths of destruction, like moths rushing into a blazing fire.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The unity within the creation that has emerged from the unmanifest to the manifest was shown in the previous verse in an extremely beautiful style through the analogy of rivers originating from the ocean. Originating from the ocean, all rivers ultimately merge back into it. No analogy can be complete in itself. In the illustration of the rivers, one limitation remains: since a river lacks its own consciousness, its own free will is not shown in its merging with the ocean. One might doubt that conscious beings, due to their independent discrimination, would not behave like insentient water. Here, to show that living beings also, driven by their nature, are forcibly drawn towards the mouth of death, this illustration is given: just as moths enter the mouth of a blazing fire with great speed for their own destruction. To Vyasaji, the whole of nature appears as an open book of scriptures. Through various events and examples, he explains these fundamental facts: that the projection of the unmanifest into the manifest state is the process of creation, and the merging of the manifest back into its unmanifest nature is destruction or death. When we try to understand this seemingly terrifying or monstrous death from a realistic perspective, it sheds its disguise and reveals its cheerful and blossoming face. Arjuna's mental tension was mainly because he had hastily made an erroneous evaluation of the massive destruction to occur on the battlefield of Kurukshetra. The only remedy for this was to raise his vision to such a height from where he could, in a single glance, see and understand this inevitable natural phenomenon of death. Shri Krishna provided him with exactly this remedy. A close and complete study of any event removes the venomous fangs of its terrifying hoods. It is only when man's discriminating intellect is shrouded in ignorance that the events happening around him choke him and bring him down. Just as rivers enter the ocean and moths enter the mouth of fire rapidly, similarly all forms dissolve back into the unmanifest. By understanding the phenomenon of death in this manner, man can face his life free from fear, because for him, the entire life becomes an unbroken stream of changes. Therefore, as the play of Time, death becomes a stingless event. In the next verse, this death has been glorified with all its terrifying beauty.

Commentary—yathā pradīptaṃ jvalanaṃ pataṅgā viśanti nāśāya samṛddhavegāḥ—Just as moths living in green grass, on seeing a blazing fire somewhere in the dark night of the rainy season, get fascinated by it (thinking that a very beautiful light has appeared, they will benefit from it, and their darkness will be dispelled) and rush towards it with great speed. Some of them are consumed by the blazing fire; some get touched slightly by the flames, so they cannot fly anymore and keep writhing. Yet, their longing remains only for that fire! If some compassionate person puts out that fire, those moths become very sad, thinking that he has deprived them of a great benefit! tathaiva nāśāya viśanti lokās-tavāpi vaktrāṇi samṛddhavegāḥ—To be constantly and eagerly engaged in enjoying sensory pleasures and accumulating possessions, and to continuously dwell in the mind on these pleasures and accumulation—this is the increased worldly speed. Kings like Duryodhana and others, possessing such momentum, are, like moths, entering Your mouths which represent the wheel of Time, with great speed; that is, they are heading towards downfall—towards the eighty-four lakh species of life and hells. The meaning is that generally humans run day and night to acquire worldly enjoyments, happiness, comfort, prestige, respect, etc. In acquiring these, they get insulted, criticized, suffer losses, experience anxiety, feel a burning sensation in their hearts, and the life span on the strength of which they are living also keeps depleting; yet they remain inwardly eager to obtain these perishable enjoyments and accumulation (Footnote p. 593).

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Having given the illustration of the river current for entry without conscious intent, he states the illustration for entry with conscious intent: 'yathā pradīptam' (as moths into a blazing fire), etc. Just as moths (insects), possessing great speed, consciously enter a blazing fire only for their own destruction (death), in the very same way, all these people like Duryodhana and others, with great speed, are entering Your mouths for their destruction, knowingly and inevitably.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
The current of a river was given as an illustration for entry due to helplessness (involuntariness). Now, he states the illustration for entry with conscious intent: 'yathā' (as), etc. Just as moths (a type of tiny winged insects), possessing great speed, enter a blazing fire consciously only for their own destruction, i.e., death, similarly these people also are entering Your mouths.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
The currents of water enter the ocean, but they do not undergo the destruction of their water-nature. However, these people enter for their destruction; therefore, he states another illustration. Just as moths (a type of tiny winged insects), with great speed, enter a blazing fire for their destruction, similarly these creatures (people), with great speed, enter Your mouths also for their destruction.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
To show that their destruction is rooted in their own actions, as implied by the word 'rushing' (tvaramāṇāḥ in 11.27), and to show the dissolution of all of them into the One, who remains in a general form suitable for the destruction of all, two illustrations are given in these two verses (28 and 29) starting with 'yathā' (as). Since the destruction of all (including the Pandavas) is not mentioned, and only the scorching of the world is stated later, the word 'lokāḥ' (worlds/people) here means the same as 'heroes of the world of men' (naraloka-vīrāḥ) mentioned earlier. With this intent, it is summarized as 'these kings'. In 'ambuvegāḥ', the word 'vega' indicates the swift flow (current) of water. Since the word 'pataṅga' has multiple meanings (like birds, etc.), the word 'śalabhāḥ' (moths) is used to exclude birds. Although the word 'abhivijvalanti' (blazing) belongs to the previous verse, it is read after the fire illustration to show its utility in the following illustration of fire. The word 'samṛddhavegāḥ' (with increased speed) has the same meaning as the previously mentioned 'tvaramāṇāḥ' (rushing), implying that they are 'rushing on their own accord'. Since moths might cause the lamp to extinguish by the flapping of their wings, to exclude that possibility and to indicate that they enter only for their own destruction, it is said 'into a blazing fire for their own destruction' (ātma-nāśāya). The two illustrations show a difference in the manner of destruction: for a river current, destruction means giving up its separate identity as a current (merging into the ocean, though the water remains the same substance), whereas for moths, it means being reduced to ashes, which is a different substance altogether. Alternatively, the river illustration implies the inability to turn back even if one wishes to, whereas the moth illustration indicates that this destruction is caused by one's own voluntary action. Also, the ocean is cited to show that the Lord remains complete (undiminished) despite everyone entering Him, while the blazing fire is cited to show sudden destruction.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Since destruction is not visibly apparent in the river illustration, he states another illustration for entry for the purpose of destruction: 'yathā' (as), etc. Just as moths (tiny insects), intoxicated by the speed of their own wings, enter a blazing fire for their own destruction, i.e., death; in the same way, these previously mentioned people, intoxicated by their own great speed, are entering Your mouths also for their destruction, i.e., death.
