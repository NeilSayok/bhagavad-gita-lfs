Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Arjuna still believes that fighting against his elders and grandfather is a terrible act. It seems he has either forgotten the Lord's teaching or never truly grasped it. Sri Krishna had made it clear that in the war of the Mahabharata, Arjuna would not really be 'killing' his revered teachers, because the war was not a conflict between individuals but between two principles - the Pandavas standing for righteousness and moral order. Unfortunately, unable to rise above his own ego, Arjuna could not fully identify himself with his righteous cause; to the extent that he failed to identify with that ideal, his ego persisted, and he continued to see a moral fault in fighting. In this verse Arjuna implies that although Krishna's argument seemed to endorse the path of renunciation through knowledge, he was nevertheless being driven into this terrible action.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
In the previous chapter, the nature of the Self and the means to knowing it were described. Having censured action as inferior to knowledge as a means, action is now enjoined in this chapter. The Lord had earlier declared that knowledge is far superior to action ('action is indeed far inferior to the yoga of the intellect,' 2.49). Given this, Arjuna asks: if you hold knowledge to be superior to action, why then do you engage me in this terrible action, namely war, instead of prescribing the path of renunciation for one not bound by such duties? This is the sense of the verse beginning 'jyayasi.'

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
(Sub-commentary explaining Shankara's introduction) This passage explains the connection between chapters two and three. In chapter two the Lord taught two disciplines needed for the start of the Gita's teaching - knowledge grounded in the vision of the true Self (Sankhya), and the discipline of action (Yoga) as a means toward it. Some interpreters hold that the Gita intends a single combined path (samuccaya) of knowledge and action for one and the same person as the means to liberation. Anandagiri examines and rejects this reading. If a combination were intended, Arjuna's question - distinguishing knowledge as 'superior' to action - would be senseless, since within one combined means, no single component can be called superior to the whole in terms of result. The Lord's own words that 'action is far inferior to the yoga of the intellect' (2.49) show that knowledge alone, preceded by the complete renunciation of works, is the means to liberation for one who directly seeks it, while action remains prescribed only for those not yet qualified for that path (such as householders, for whom Vedic duties combined with knowledge are enjoined only until true qualification for renunciation arises; this is not a real conflict, since the two apply to different classes of aspirants). Some rival explanations propose that duty-bound (smarta) action alone, not Vedic ritual action, is to be combined with knowledge for everyone; Anandagiri shows this too leads to an internal contradiction with the Lord's own later teaching that renunciates need not perform even routine (nitya) rites, since omission causes no transgression for one who has renounced the very basis of action through knowledge. He concludes that the Lord's teaching intends knowledge alone, preceded by renunciation, as the direct means of liberation for the seeker, while action serves as a preliminary purification for those not yet ready; Arjuna's perplexity - that the Lord seems to have praised knowledge as supreme, yet still directs him toward the terrible act of war - is thus a genuine and consistent question, one which sets up the teaching of this chapter.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Arjuna said: O Janardana, if you consider knowledge (buddhi) superior to action, then, O Keshava, why do you engage me in this terrible action? With your seemingly conflicting words you appear to bewilder my understanding; therefore tell me with certainty that one path by which I may attain the highest good.

Commentary: Arjuna addresses Krishna as 'Janardana' - the one who fulfils the prayers of all beings - implying that Krishna will surely grant his request as well. It is a common human weakness that when a person asks a question, he secretly wants the answer to validate his own view; true courage lies in following the speaker's direction whether or not it suits one's liking, and anything short of that is weakness. Here, unable to bear the prospect of violence against his own kinsmen, Arjuna has let this 'goodness' (non-violence) mask an actual lapse of duty, and so he now considers knowledge superior to action, questioning why, if that is so, Krishna urges him toward the terrible act of war. Earlier, in chapter two verse 39, Krishna had used the word 'buddhi' in the context of the yoga of equanimity, but Arjuna understood it simply as 'knowledge.' Krishna had also said that 'action is far inferior to the yoga of the intellect' (2.49). If action is truly inferior to knowledge, Arjuna reasons, he should not be engaged even in ordinary scripturally-enjoined acts like sacrifice, charity and austerity, let alone in war, the cruellest of all actions, which requires killing people the whole day. Earlier, full of enthusiasm, Arjuna had asked Krishna to place his chariot between the two armies; but once he saw his elders and kinsmen arrayed for battle, his attachment to family surfaced and his mind turned away from action toward knowledge, since the path of knowledge requires no such terrible deeds. Ramsukhdas notes that here 'buddhi' must mean 'knowledge,' not 'equanimity,' since Krishna had already instructed Arjuna in chapter two, verse 48, to act while established in equanimity; only if Arjuna holds two distinct notions (knowledge versus action) does his mixed, confused question make sense - a reading confirmed later, when Krishna distinguishes the path of knowledge for men of knowledge from the path of action for yogis. A question asked with genuine faith alone receives its true answer, and Arjuna's deep faith in Krishna shows through even in this seemingly reproachful question - he remains ready to undertake even terrible action once Krishna, after due reflection, directs him. He repeats his earlier request (2.7) that Krishna tell him decisively the one course that ensures his ultimate good.

For what purpose, then, do you engage me in this terrible deed, which consists in the activities of all the senses, and is thus an obstacle for the vision of the self?

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Continuing from the previous chapter, which showed the identity of the true Self (Sankhya) along with the means of realizing it (Yoga), this chapter shows how action is enjoined as a means to knowledge; the disparagement of inaction here likewise serves ultimately to establish the necessity of action. Arjuna's question really contains two parts: why does Krishna engage him in action at all, and why specifically in such a 'terrible' (ghora) action? Jayatirtha explains that Vedic actions, including sacrifices and even war, may be either desire-prompted (kamya) or performed without desire as duties of one's station. Desire-prompted acts yield results such as attaining heaven (as indicated in 2.37 and 2.18), whereas the desireless duties proper to the renunciate order yield no worldly fruit but serve knowledge, as scripture teaches ('the tranquil, self-controlled one... sees the Self'). Since knowledge has been declared far superior to desire-prompted action, engaging Arjuna in war as though it were merely a desire-prompted act would be improper; he should instead be directed toward the renunciate's discipline. Even granting that war can 'free one from the bonds of action' as earlier stated (2.39), engaging him in it while also extolling knowledge produces exactly the confusion ('mixed statement') Arjuna complains of. Jayatirtha thus explains why Arjuna, despite Krishna's earlier teaching distinguishing the desirous doer from the seeker of knowledge, still feels troubled and raises this question.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
(Introductory verses) In eight chapters the Lord expounds to Arjuna the threefold subject of dharma, His own glory (mahatmya), knowledge, and devotion. Having first set forth Sankhya and Yoga, the third chapter determines the true nature of dharma by distinguishing renunciation from non-renunciation, concluding that acting without renouncing is superior to outright renunciation, since so long as one is bound to a body, actual renunciation of action is impossible; hence, acting under the Lord's command is the best course. Although the Lord had earlier said, 'This wisdom has been declared to you in Sankhya; now hear it with regard to Yoga' (2.39), where action for the yoga path was taught, he concluded that section with an emphasis on the renunciate mind's steadiness in the description of the resolute intellect. Uncertain of the Lord's actual conclusion, and not knowing which path is finally intended, Arjuna asks: O Krishna, if you hold that knowledge (buddhi) is superior to action, then why do you engage me in this terrible action of war?

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
(Extended overview of the whole Gita) Madhusudana Saraswati explains that chapter two, following the introduction given in chapter one, encapsulates in seed form the entire teaching of the Gita: first, desireless action as a means of purifying the mind; then, control of the senses and the renunciation of all action; then devotion to the Lord supported by inquiry into the meaning of the Upanishads; then steadfastness in true knowledge, whose fruit - through removal of the ignorance born of the three gunas - is liberation while still living (jivanmukti), and at death, liberation from embodiment (videhamukti). He maps this scheme in detail onto all eighteen chapters. Turning to this verse, he notes that in chapter two the Lord taught two disciplines - the path of knowledge grounded in Sankhya ('this wisdom has been declared to you in Sankhya') and the path of action grounded in Yoga ('now hear it with regard to Yoga... you have a right to action alone') - without explicitly stating which class of aspirant belongs to which path. Since the Lord had also declared action 'far inferior to the yoga of the intellect' (2.49) and had closed the chapter praising the steadfastness of one established in knowledge as the highest state ('this is the state of Brahman'), Arjuna reasonably concludes that knowledge is held to be superior to action. He therefore asks Krishna: if, in your view, knowledge - which pertains to the Self and requires no exertion - is superior to action, which is difficult, effortful, and comparatively inferior, why do you engage me, your own devoted follower, in this terrible, violence-laden action of war? By addressing Krishna as 'Janardana' (the one entreated by all beings for their desired ends) and 'Keshava' (Lord of all), Arjuna implies that it would be unbecoming of the Lord to mislead one who has taken complete refuge in Him.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
The Lord had first taught, in verses such as 'you have grieved for those who should not be grieved for,' the discriminative knowledge of body and Self as the means to liberation, and had then, in 'this wisdom has been declared to you in Sankhya; now hear it with regard to Yoga,' also taught action, without clearly stating which of the two - knowledge or action - is the more important. However, since the person of steady wisdom is described as free from desire, with senses mastered and ego dissolved, and since the chapter closes in praise of that very state ('this is the state of Brahman'), Arjuna concludes that the Lord regards knowledge as superior to action. He therefore asks: if knowledge, being more immediately conducive to liberation, is superior to action in your view, why then do you keep urging me - saying 'fight' and 'arise' - toward this terrible, violent action?

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
In the previous chapter two disciplines were taught, as means and as goal - the discipline of action and the discipline of knowledge - and the discipline of knowledge was declared the more excellent of the two. Having heard this, Arjuna, eager for that fruit-bearing discipline of knowledge, and considering himself entitled to it once his mind is purified, asks: O Janardana - one who chastises the enemies of the gods - it does not become you to inflict pain on me, who am no demon, by urging me into this terrible action. If, in your view, knowledge is superior even to desireless action, why then do you engage me, your own devotee, in this terrible, violence-filled action, telling me again and again to 'therefore fight'? By further addressing Krishna as 'Keshava' - one who shows compassion even to Brahma and the other gods - Arjuna implies: be that compassionate Keshava toward your own devotee too, and not merely Janardana, who afflicts only the wicked.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
(Extended philosophical introduction) Vedantadeshika first establishes the continuity between chapters two and three by showing that the knowledge of the individual self taught in chapter two is not, by itself, an independent and direct means to liberation, but rather auxiliary to the higher knowledge of, and devotion to, the Supreme Person - in keeping with Upanishadic passages such as the meditation on the 'small space within the heart' in the Chandogya Upanishad and the teaching of the Katha Valli, where knowledge of the individual self serves and culminates in knowledge of the Supreme Self (described there as the 'Supreme Light,' identified with Narayana). Having thus set the philosophical stage, he turns to Arjuna's question. Bound by his reluctance to fight, and drawn toward the path of renunciation implied in the earlier teaching on steadiness of wisdom, Arjuna asks: where has it been taught that knowledge is superior to action? And granting that it is, why should that bar him from action suited to his station, and why is this action in particular called 'terrible,' when duty-bound action ought not to be so? Vedantadeshika explains that Arjuna, understanding the statement 'action is far inferior to the yoga of the intellect' (2.49) as praising the primacy of the discipline of knowledge which is the direct means to vision of the Self, and understanding further that such vision requires the complete cessation of activity in all the senses, reasonably asks why Krishna instead engages him in war - an action that fully engages every sense and is thus, for a seeker of liberation, itself an obstacle to that vision, and 'terrible' in this specific sense, quite apart from its violence. Ruling out possible motives the Lord might have - His own fulfilment (He is already complete), a worldly result, personal grievance against those to be killed, mere expedience, or ease of accomplishment - Arjuna, assured of Krishna's special regard for him, feels justified in asking why the Lord engages him in an action that the Lord Himself seems to have called inferior to knowledge.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
(Introductory verses) Through questions touching on yoga, sankhya, the nature of Brahman, and action, Arjuna, filled with doubt and desiring to attain devotion, addresses Krishna. Since in the previous chapter the Lord had taught that the discipline of wisdom is superior to action, Arjuna, uncertain why the Lord nonetheless prescribes action for him, asks: O Janardana, destroyer of all ignorance, if you hold knowledge superior to action, why then do you engage me in this terrible action - terrible because, being liable to interruption or incompleteness, it risks becoming entirely fruitless or even a source of wrongdoing. By addressing Krishna as 'Keshava,' who grants liberation even to those entangled in base qualities, Arjuna implies: if, having made me act, you nonetheless intend to grant me liberation regardless, then such action is indeed worth performing - a thought subtly conveyed through this very form of address.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
In the previous chapter the Lord presented two forms of understanding (buddhi) - one grounded in Sankhya and one grounded in Yoga, as in 'this wisdom has been declared to you in Sankhya; now hear it with regard to Yoga.' Through the verse on the resolute intellect, he indicated that those established in Sankhya-knowledge have no fear of falling away, whereas such fear does exist for those established merely in the discipline of action; and through the statement that 'whatever purpose is served by a well is served by a great reservoir of water,' he implied that all the fruits of action are contained within the realization of Sankhya. Taking this path of tranquility - suited to his own temperament - to be the one favoured by the Lord, Arjuna asks: O Janardana, if knowledge, the realization proper to Sankhya, is superior to the desireless discipline of action, why then, though I would be content even with living on alms, do you engage me again and again - saying 'fight' - in this terrible action of slaying my own kinsmen?

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Arjuna said: O Janārdana, O Keśava, why do You want to engage me in this ghastly warfare, if You think that intelligence is better than fruitive work?

 The Supreme Personality of Godhead Śrī Kṛṣṇa has very elaborately described the constitution of the soul in the previous chapter, with a view to delivering His intimate friend Arjuna from the ocean of material grief. And the path of realization has been recommended: buddhi-yoga, or Kṛṣṇa consciousness. Sometimes Kṛṣṇa consciousness is misunderstood to be inertia, and one with such a misunderstanding often withdraws to a secluded place to become fully Kṛṣṇa conscious by chanting the holy name of Lord Kṛṣṇa. But without being trained in the philosophy of Kṛṣṇa consciousness, it is not advisable to chant the holy name of Kṛṣṇa in a secluded place, where one may acquire only cheap adoration from the innocent public. Arjuna also thought of Kṛṣṇa consciousness or buddhi-yoga, or intelligence in spiritual advancement of knowledge, as something like retirement from active life and the practice of penance and austerity at a secluded place. In other words, he wanted to skillfully avoid the fighting by using Kṛṣṇa consciousness as an excuse. But as a sincere student, he placed the matter before his master and questioned Kṛṣṇa as to his best course of action. In answer, Lord Kṛṣṇa elaborately explained karma-yoga, or work in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, in this Third Chapter.
