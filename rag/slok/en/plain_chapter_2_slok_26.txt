Life Application:
Avoid constant sorrow over life's inevitable cycles, accepting that change is a natural law of existence.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Since the eternality of the Self has already been established, this verse might seem inconsistent -- to resolve this doubt he says 'atmana iti.' Here the division is 'anityatvam,' and this refers to the view of the Buddhists or the Lokayatikas. Even after Arjuna, the listener, had heard the true nature of the Self as stated earlier, since no firm conclusion arose in him, a doubt remains as to whether he has accepted one of the two views -- hence the use of the two particles 'atha' and 'ca.' Even while granting the eternality and other qualities of the Self under discussion, the notion of being born again and again lacks any valid proof -- this is conveyed by 'loke.' If one insists on constant birth, insisting on constant death too would seem contradictory -- to resolve this doubt, 'tatha' is said. Even having voiced the other's view, there remains no scope for Arjuna's grief such as 'alas, what a great sin we are resolved to commit' -- this is conveyed by 'tathapi.' Having thus shown the visible form of Arjuna's grief, the reason for its impropriety is given by 'janmavatah' -- that which is born must perish, and that which perishes must be born; these two are inevitable and mutually pervasive -- this is the purport.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O mighty-armed one! Even if you consider this embodied Self to be constantly born and constantly dying, still you should not grieve in this way.

Explanation -- With the words 'atha ca' and 'manyase', the Lord here presents an alternative view: although the true and settled doctrine is that the embodied Self is never born or dies at any time (Gita 2.20), still, even if you accept the exact opposite -- that the embodied Self is constantly born and constantly dies -- even then you should not grieve. Because that which will be born will surely die, and that which will die will surely be born again -- no one can set aside this law. If a seed is sown in the earth, it swells and sprouts, and that same sprout gradually grows into a tree. Examined closely, did that seed remain in one form even for a moment? In the earth it first gave up its hard form and became soft, then gave up the soft form and became a sprout, then gave up the sprout-form and became a tree, and finally, when its span was over, it withered. Thus the seed never remained the same for even a moment, but kept changing every instant. If the seed had remained the same even for a moment, how could the process continuing up to the tree's withering have occurred? It gave up one form -- that was its death -- and took on another form -- that was its birth. In this way it kept being born and dying every instant. The body too is just like the seed. In a very subtle way, the seed of semen joins with the ovum. Growing gradually, it becomes a child and is born. After birth it grew, then declined, and finally died. Thus the body never remained in one form even for a moment but kept changing, that is, kept being born and dying every instant.

The Lord says that even if you consider the embodied Self, like the body, to be constantly born and dying, even this cannot be a cause for grief.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
This (verse), 'On the other hand,' etc., is uttered assuming that the Self is transient. Atha ca, on the other hand, if ( conveys the sense of assumption ); following ordinary experience, manyase, you think; enam, this One, the Self under discussion; is nityajatam, born continually, becomes born with the birth of each of the numerous bodies; va, or; nityam, constantly; mrtam, dies, along with the death of each of these (bodies); tatha api, even then, even if the Self be of that nature; tvam, you; maha-baho, O mighty-armed one; na arhasi, ought not; socitum, to grieve; evam, thus, since that which is subject to birth will die, and that which is subject to death will be born; these two are inevitable.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Objection: Having resolved every doubt and established the eternality of the Self, and having concluded that with 'tasmadevam,' why is 'atha ca' said again? In answer to this objection, even while granting the eternality of the Self, he raises the possibility of grief in another way, saying 'astu evam...' The residual doubt is: death in war is certain, and grief will arise for my kinsmen on account of birth and death -- that is my grief. This also explains the first half of the verse. Hence it is stated as 'deha-samyoga-viyogatmaka-janimrti' -- otherwise it would have simply said 'deha-samyoga-viyogau' or 'janimrti.' By 'sta eva,' it is shown that the word 'nitya' is used in the sense of emphatic certainty. This refutes the Mayavadin interpretation of those who say 'astu evam atmano nityatvam,' claiming this verse is spoken while granting the impermanence of the Self -- for that would contradict the following statement 'dhruvam janma mrtasya ca' (2.27), and would also contradict 'avyaktadini' (2.28).

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
The word 'atha' introduces the alternative view. Standing on the Lokayatika (materialist) doctrine, even if you consider the Self to be eternal yet constantly born and dying, still it is not fitting for you to grieve.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Thus the freedom from grief for the Self was stated on the basis of its changelessness. Now, over two verses, the Lord establishes the Self's freedom from grief even while granting that it is subject to change. On this matter, the Buddhists hold that the Self is of the nature of consciousness and perishes every moment; the Lokayatikas (materialists) hold that the body itself is the Self, which, though seemingly stable, undergoes modification every moment, arising and perishing -- and this is directly evident; some others hold that the Self, distinct from the body, is nevertheless born and perishes along with the body; still others hold that it arises at the start of creation like space, persists through changes of body, and perishes only at dissolution; the logicians (Naiyayikas) hold that the Self, while eternal, is still born and dies -- since 'rebirth' itself is birth, namely connection with a new body and senses, and death is likewise the severance of connection with the previous body and senses. Even granting all these views of the Self's impermanence, the Lord denies that grief is warranted -- 'atha' meaning 'on the other hand,' 'ca' meaning 'even.' If, owing to the difficulty of grasping the true nature of the Self, no firm conviction arises despite repeated hearing, and you accept some other view instead of the one stated -- even then, resting on that very doctrine of impermanence, if you consider this Self to be constantly born or constantly dying, even then, O mighty-armed one (best among men, said half in jest) -- even in accepting such a flawed doctrine, such a flawed outlook is not really possible in you (said also half in sympathy) -- thus, the kind of grief you express, such as 'alas, what a great sin we are resolved to commit,' is not something you yourself are fit to feel, because under the doctrine of momentariness, the doctrine that the body is the Self, and the doctrine of birth-and-death occurring together with the body, there is no possibility of fear of sin at all, since there is no further birth -- and it is precisely that fear of sin which causes your grief. Under the doctrine of momentariness even visible, present suffering is not possible, since there remains no one to witness the loss of kinsmen. The word 'eva' in the other doctrine allows for grief caused by visible suffering -- meaning, even though grief over visible suffering may be possible, grief over unseen (future) suffering is wholly unwarranted -- this is the meaning of the first verse.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Thus it was stated that, since there is no birth or destruction of the Self, grief is not warranted. Now, even granting that the Self is born along with the body and perishes along with the body's destruction, it is said that grief should still not be felt -- 'atha ca...' Even if you consider this Self to be born each time a body is born, and to die when that body dies -- since birth and death, being the fruits of merit and sin, belong to the Self -- even then you ought not to grieve.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Having firmly established the eternality of the Self, the Lord now removes grief even by granting the doctrine of those outside the Vedic fold -- 'atha...' The word 'atha' conveys acceptance of the alternative view. From the ordinary worldly standpoint, you consider this Self as born with the arising of each body, and as perishing with its destruction. This statement is also representative of the other views held by the nihilists. Or, the exposition of other doctrines here is not intended, since there is no purpose in it -- hence, even accepting the crude view, grief should not be felt; how much less so upon accepting the Vedic view -- this is the intended conclusion. Addressing him as 'mahabaho' (mighty-armed), he hints that, by not grieving, Arjuna should make the greatness of his arms meaningful.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Thus it was said that on accepting a Self distinct from the body, there is no cause for grief. Now, over three verses beginning with 'atha ca,' it is said that even from the nihilist view -- holding the body itself to be the Self -- there is still no cause for grief. 'Atha' conveys the sense of beginning an alternative view, or a question, or acceptance. 'Va' likewise conveys the combination of the two qualifiers. 'Nityajatam nityamrtam' means having fixed birth and destruction. This meaning will be elaborated in the next verse. An eternal entity cannot possibly be born or die, and the very nature of the acts of birth and death cannot be qualified by eternality. To dispel the confusion of taking 'nitya' separately as a qualifier of the Self, 'nityamrtam' is stated as one compound, just as 'nityajatam' is. The qualifier 'deha' (body) is derived from the force of the context. The word 'eva' carries the sense of 'ca'; stating the fruit of this emphasis, he says 'na deha...' Earlier it was said that since there is no destruction of the Self, the occasion for grief does not even arise; now, even granting the destruction of the Self conceived as the body -- since that destruction is unavoidable -- O mighty-armed one, it is not fitting for you to grieve excessively; this is the sense of the word 'evam.' Or, earlier, on accepting a Self distinct from the body, excessive grief born of fear of the next world and so on might have seemed justified; but now, like one liberated from worldly bondage, how could excessive grief be appropriate in place of your excessive attachment, O mighty-armed one -- this is the intent. By 'tvam mahabaho,' it is implied that as a hero, agitation over the death of yourself or others is not befitting you. Since an assertion requires a supporting reason, he draws out the reason -- whether located in the next verse or indicated by these two qualifiers -- with 'parinama...'

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having thus stated the doctrine of the learned, he now says that even by the doctrine of the unlearned, grief is not warranted -- 'atha ca...' From the alternative view, even if you consider this Self to be born with each body and to die when that body dies, still it is not fitting for you to grieve over it, because you are mighty-armed. The sense here is: knowledge of the birth and death of the eternal Self arises only from identification with the body. If that is so, where is the destruction of your own arm-strength and the like?

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Thus it was said that from the standpoint of ultimate truth, grief is not proper; now he says that even from the standpoint of the ordinary person, grief is not proper -- 'atha ca...' 'Nityam jatam' -- constantly born by rule -- is the Charvaka (materialist) view. 'Nityam sada jatam' -- constantly, always born -- is the momentary-cognition (Buddhist) view. 'Though eternal, born through connection with a new body and senses' is the view of the logicians (Naiyayikas) and others. The same threefold meaning applies to 'nityam va manyase mrtam' as well. In none of these three views is grief proper. By addressing him as 'mahabaho,' Arjuna is being roused to enthusiasm for the battle.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
If, however, you think that the soul [or the symptoms of life] will always be born and die forever, you still have no reason to lament, O mighty-armed.

 There is always a class of philosophers, almost akin to the Buddhists, who do not believe in the separate existence of the soul beyond the body. When Lord Kṛṣṇa spoke the Bhagavad-gītā , it appears that such philosophers existed, and they were known as the Lokāyatikas and Vaibhāṣikas. Such philosophers maintain that life symptoms take place at a certain mature condition of material combination. The modern material scientist and materialist philosophers also think similarly. According to them, the body is a combination of physical elements, and at a certain stage the life symptoms develop by interaction of the physical and chemical elements. The science of anthropology is based on this philosophy. Currently, many pseudo religions – now becoming fashionable in America – are also adhering to this philosophy, as are the nihilistic nondevotional Buddhist sects. Even if Arjuna did not believe in the existence of the soul – as in the Vaibhāṣika philosophy – there would still have been no cause for lamentation. No one laments the loss of a certain bulk of chemicals and stops discharging his prescribed duty. On the other hand, in modern science and scientific warfare, so many tons of chemicals are wasted for achieving victory over the enemy. According to the Vaibhāṣika philosophy, the so-called soul or ātmā vanishes along with the deterioration of the body. So, in any case, whether Arjuna accepted the Vedic conclusion that there is an atomic soul or he did not believe in the existence of the soul, he had no reason to lament. According to this theory, since there are so many living entities generating out of matter every moment, and so many of them are being vanquished every moment, there is no need to grieve for such incidents. If there were no rebirth for the soul, Arjuna had no reason to be afraid of being affected by sinful reactions due to his killing his grandfather and teacher. But at the same time, Kṛṣṇa sarcastically addressed Arjuna as mahā-bāhu, mighty-armed, because He, at least, did not accept the theory of the Vaibhāṣikas, which leaves aside the Vedic wisdom. As a kṣatriya, Arjuna belonged to the Vedic culture, and it behooved him to continue to follow its principles.
