Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
If a boat's helmsman dies and its sails are left open, the boat becomes entirely at the mercy of raging storms and turbulent waves; buffeted about by the fierce onslaught of the agitated waves, it wanders here and there and is destroyed before reaching its destination. In the same way, the senses of a person lacking self-control wander among sense objects and drag the mind into a storm of desires, ultimately destroying it. Therefore, if a person wishes to live a meaningful life, he must constantly strive to keep his senses under his control. With this verse, Sri Krishna concludes the topic of man's downfall that He began discussing from verse 62.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
Explaining why the undisciplined person cannot attain true contemplation, Sri Krishna says 'indriyanam...'. The word 'anuvidhiyate' means 'is made to act' -- for it will be said later that understanding follows the senses, as ordained by the Lord. The meaning is that the mind's activity even obstructs wisdom that is about to arise; and even wisdom that has already arisen is overpowered.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Raising a further verse in response to an implied question about the undisciplined person, Sri Krishna establishes with a further reason that a person of scattered mind, lacking contemplation, cannot attain the understanding that is of the nature of direct realization -- hence 'indriyanam...'. The word 'yat' (which) also carries over to govern 'mind'. 'Of the senses' refers to the ears and so on, whose objects are sound and the like; their engagement means the separate, discriminating grasp of each. The illustration is explained by the word 'in water'. Since the mind, thus engaged, produces this effect, it follows that the undisciplined person's understanding does not arise.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Among the senses that wander each toward its own objects, the one sense to which the mind becomes attached makes the mind its follower; that mind alone, like the wind carrying away a boat on water, carries away one's discrimination (buddhi).

Commentary: This human birth is meant solely for the attainment of God. One's resolve should be firmly fixed that only God-realization is to be achieved, whatever else may happen. When this resolve is firm, the importance given to sense-enjoyments drops away from the seeker's sense of self-importance, and once that importance drops away, the resolute intellect (vyavasayatmika buddhi) becomes firm. But as long as this resolute intellect is not firm, what happens to the seeker is described here.

'indriyanam hi caratam yanmano'nuvidhiyate' -- When a seeker engages in all kinds of activity in the field of action, the senses inevitably encounter their respective objects. Among these, whichever sense develops attachment to its object draws the mind along with it, making the mind its follower. The mind then begins to enjoy that object, meaning a sense of pleasure and enjoyment arises in the mind; the object colors the mind and comes to seem important there. For example, while eating, if some food tastes good, the tongue becomes attached to it; once attached, the tongue pulls the mind along too, and the mind becomes pleased and delighted by that taste.

'tadasya harati prajnam' -- Once an object gains importance in the mind, that mind alone steals away the seeker's discrimination (buddhi); that is, devotion to duty is replaced in the seeker by a desire for enjoyment. With that desire for enjoyment, the resolve 'I must attain God alone' (vyavasayatmika buddhi) no longer remains. Explaining this process takes time, but the intellect is led astray without any delay at all -- the moment a sense makes the mind its follower, the desire for enjoyment arises in the mind, and at that very moment the discrimination is destroyed.

'vayurnavamivambhasi' -- How this discrimination is carried away is explained through an illustration: just as the wind carries away a boat moving on water, so the mind carries away the intellect. Just as a person crossing a river or ocean by boat toward his destination will be driven off course if a contrary wind blows, so too the seeker, riding the boat of his resolute intellect (vyavasayatmika buddhi) to cross the ocean of the world toward God, is carried off course by the very mind that a single sense has made its follower -- that is, the mind drags him back toward worldly life. This creates in the seeker a sense of pleasure in sense objects and a sense of importance in things useful for their enjoyment.

Wind can disturb a boat in two ways -- by driving it off course, or by sinking it in the water. But a skilled helmsman turns the wind's action to his own advantage, so that the wind cannot divert the boat from its path but instead helps it reach its destination. Similarly, a mind that follows the senses disturbs the intellect in two ways -- either by suppressing the resolve for God-realization and generating a desire for enjoyment, or by engaging one in forbidden enjoyments and causing downfall. But for one whose mind and senses are under control, the mind does not disturb the intellect; rather, it helps that intellect reach God (see 2.64-65).

Connection: The previous verse explained why an undisciplined person lacks resolute intellect. The next verse describes the state of one who is disciplined (yukta).

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Since the topic is fully conveyed in a single verse, the commentator asks why it required two verses -- his answer being that meditation (dhyana) becomes possible only after hearing and reflection have already been accomplished, and this is stated to preempt the doubt that it might not be so. The word 'anuvidhana' ordinarily means 'becoming similar', which does not fit here; and since it might seem that the agent here is the individual soul (jiva), the text clarifies with 'anuvidhiyate' -- the root 'dha' with the prefix 'vi' here means 'to make' or 'to cause', and the true agent is understood to be the Lord Himself. Here the prefix 'anu' conveys the sense of 'following after', not 'characteristic of' or similar, so it is not to be read as a preposition governing the case. As to why it is said that understanding (buddhi) is affirmed here, the answer given is 'grasping power is prajna' -- but taking this literally would be inappropriate here, hence it is explained further: for one whose wisdom of the transcendent has not yet arisen, absence of discipline can accomplish nothing, since only what already exists can be carried away -- this is answered by saying it obstructs even wisdom that is about to arise. Then, as to whether absence of discipline is powerless once transcendent knowledge has already arisen, it is explained that even wisdom already arisen can be overpowered. The point is that mere hearing and reflection unaccompanied by control of the mind do not by themselves produce the settled conviction of truth and correct understanding of scripture that are necessary for meditation.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Here the reason is stated with 'indriyanam...'. Among the senses wandering among their objects, the mind, as the agent, is made to follow whichever single sense becomes dominant and is driven by it in turn; that mind then carries away one's wisdom. The illustration given for this is 'like the wind carrying away a boat on the water'.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
To explain why an undisciplined person lacks wisdom, the commentator says: among the uncontrolled senses that operate freely toward their own objects, the mind is made to follow -- is driven -- by focusing on whichever single sense it attaches to; that is, the mind becomes engaged with it. Even a single sense followed by the mind carries away, removes, the seeker's scriptural wisdom concerning the Self, because the mind becomes absorbed in that sense's object. The implication is that if even one sense can carry away wisdom in this way, it goes without saying that all the senses together certainly do so. The illustration is straightforward: the word 'on the water' indicates that the wind's power to carry away a boat exists only on water, not on dry land. In the same way, in the analogy, it is only when the mind, like water, is unsteady that a sense has the power to carry away wisdom -- not when the mind is as steady as solid ground.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
The reason why an undisciplined person lacks wisdom is given here: among the uncontrolled senses that roam freely toward their objects, whichever single sense the mind, having become subject to it, follows and moves along with -- that single sense alone carries away the wisdom of the mind or of the person, scattering it among the objects; needless to say, when many senses do so together, wisdom is carried away all the more. This is like the case of a careless helmsman, whose boat the wind drives around every which way on the ocean.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Having stated that the undisciplined person lacks wisdom, the reason is now given: among the senses acting toward their own objects, the mind that follows becomes engaged in discriminating among sense-objects, and thereby carries away the discerning person's wisdom born of discrimination between Self and non-Self -- just as the wind, blowing over the water, drives a boat off the course intended by those wishing to travel, forcing it onto a wrong path. As for the alternative reading -- that it is a single sense, focused upon and thereby driven by the mind, that carries away the wisdom of the seeker or of the mind -- that is not correct, because the statement 'the undisciplined lacks wisdom' requires that it is specifically the mind, having followed the senses, that is meant as the agent carrying away wisdom, not a single unstated sense in place of the explicitly mentioned word 'mind'; nor can 'mind' alone (independent of the senses) be meant, since it is the seeker as a whole who is the subject under discussion.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Raising the question of why sense-control would even be needed for one whose mind is already fixed in contemplation -- since control of the senses belongs only to one still in the stage of practice (sadhana), while for one already established in contemplation, being as good as accomplished, it should be unnecessary, just as for the man of realized knowledge -- the answer is given: among the senses that wander freely among worldly objects, whichever sense the mind attaches itself to and follows, that same attachment carries away that person's meditative wisdom (bhavana). The illustration given is of the wind and the boat: just as a boat on water, meant for crossing over, is capsized by a strong wind when it lacks a steady helmsman, so too is meditative wisdom overturned when the mind is not steady.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
The fault arising from the absence of that control is now explained: since, among the senses that operate toward their own respective objects -- with 'of the senses' understood as an objective genitive -- the mind, sullied by attachment and the like, is set in motion, i.e., driven, by focusing on those senses, that mind, thus following the senses, carries away this seeker's wisdom concerning the true nature of the Self, since that wisdom is dependent on the mind. The illustration is self-explanatory. Others, however, interpret it differently: that among the senses, whichever sense the mind fixes upon and follows, that particular sense carries away the wisdom of the seeker or of the mind. The commentary that says the mind, having carried away wisdom directed toward the Self, turns it toward sense-objects, is also worth considering.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
As a strong wind sweeps away a boat on the water, even one of the roaming senses on which the mind focuses can carry away a man’s intelligence.

 Unless all of the senses are engaged in the service of the Lord, even one of them engaged in sense gratification can deviate the devotee from the path of transcendental advancement. As mentioned in the life of Mahārāja Ambarīṣa, all of the senses must be engaged in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, for that is the correct technique for controlling the mind.
