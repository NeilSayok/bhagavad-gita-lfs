Life Application:
Challenge self-pity when it arises during a crisis, asking yourself whether your hesitation springs from wisdom or fear.

Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:
"The Lord said: My beloved friend! Why yield, just on the eve of battle, to this weakness which does no credit to those who call themselves Aryans, and only brings them infamy and bars against them the gates of heaven?

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The Lord was astonished to see a king who called himself an Arya reduced to such bewilderment on the battlefield. It is the nature of a true Arya -- that is, a noble person -- that he faces whatever circumstance arises in life with skill, without being shaken from his self-mastery, and turns such circumstances to his own advantage. By living in a proper manner, even the most adverse and difficult of circumstances can be transformed into joyful success. All of this depends on a person's wisdom -- on how he steers himself in the right direction through life's ups and downs. Here the Lord calls Arjuna's conduct 'anarya' (unworthy of an Arya). Noble persons remain ever alert and diligent in answering the call of life's high ideals, purity, and dignity.

Sri Krishna was astonished at seeing Arjuna in this grief-stricken state precisely because He had known him well for a long time, and this kind of grief-born delusion was wholly contrary to Arjuna's true nature. This is why He says here: 'from where has this come to you at this critical juncture...' and so on.

It is the belief of the Hindus that it is the duty of one born into a kshatriya family to fight for dharma, and that if such a hero should give up his life on the battlefield in this way, he attains heaven.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
The Blessed Lord said: O Arjuna! From where has this cowardice, unbecoming of noble persons, neither conducive to heaven nor to fame, come upon you at this critical moment?

Explanation: 'Arjuna' -- the purpose of this address is that you are one of pure, unblemished inner disposition. Hence the appearance of impurity -- cowardice -- in your nature is wholly contrary to it. How then has it come upon you?

'Kutas tva kashmalam idam vishame samupasthitam' -- the Lord, expressing astonishment, says to Arjuna: at such an occasion for battle, valour and enthusiasm ought to have arisen in you; yet at this most inopportune moment, from where has this cowardice come upon you!

Astonishment arises in two ways -- from one's own not knowing, and for the purpose of alerting another. The Lord's astonished manner of speaking here is solely to alert Arjuna, so that Arjuna's attention may turn toward his own duty. The point of saying 'kutah' (from where) is this: at root, this fault called cowardice does not belong to you (to your true self). It is an adventitious fault, one that will not remain forever. The point of saying 'samupasthitam' (has arisen) is that this cowardice has appeared not merely in your feelings and words, but has entered your actions as well. It has so thoroughly overtaken you that you have cast aside your bow and arrows and sat down in the middle of the chariot.

'Anaryajushtam' -- the sentiments that arise in wise, noble persons are always oriented toward their own true welfare. Hence, in the second half of the verse, the Lord first uses this word to say: noble persons do not accept the cowardice that has arisen in you, because your true welfare has no place whatsoever in this cowardice. Noble persons who desire their own welfare keep that welfare as their sole aim, whether in engaging in action or in withdrawing from it. Cowardice regarding their own duty never arises in them. Whatever duty falls to them according to circumstance, they perform fully and completely, with enthusiasm and diligence, for the sake of attaining their own welfare. They do not, like you, become cowardly and withdraw from battle or from any other duty. Hence, withdrawing from the duty that has come to you in the form of battle is not conducive to your welfare.

'Asvargyam' -- even setting aside the question of true welfare, if one looks at it merely from a worldly standpoint, the heavenly world is regarded as the highest in this world. But this cowardice of yours will not even grant you heaven -- that is, the fruit of withdrawing from battle out of cowardice cannot even be the attainment of heaven.

'Akirtikaram' -- even if the goal of attaining heaven is set aside, a person considered good does only that which brings him fame in the world. But this cowardice of yours will not bring you fame (glory) even in this world -- rather, it will bring you infamy (disgrace). Hence, this cowardice arising in you is wholly improper in every respect.

Here the Lord, through the sequence 'anaryajushtam, asvargyam, and akirtikaram,' has described three types of people: (1) Thoughtful persons desire only their own true welfare. Their sole aim, their sole goal, is welfare alone. (2) Meritorious persons desire, through virtuous deeds, to attain heaven. Regarding heaven alone as supreme, they keep its attainment as their sole aim. (3) Ordinary persons hold this world in the highest regard. Hence they desire fame for themselves in this world and regard that very fame as their sole aim.

By stating these three terms, the Lord cautions Arjuna: this resolve of yours not to fight will neither grant you welfare and heaven -- the goal of thoughtful and meritorious persons -- nor will it grant you fame -- the goal of ordinary persons. Hence, your resolve not to fight, born of delusion, is utterly worthless; it will bring about your downfall, lead you to the hells, and bring you infamy.

Transition: what should now be done, once cowardice has taken hold? To remove this very question, the Lord says [in the next verse]--

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
He introduces that very utterance of the Lord, in 'Sri Bhagavan uvacha...': 'Bhaga' is declared, in six-fold enumeration, to mean complete lordship, complete dharma, complete fame, complete glory, complete renunciation, and complete liberation (or the knowledge that is its means) -- this is the etymological sense. He in whom such complete lordship and the rest eternally and unobstructedly abide is 'Bhagavan.' Likewise: 'He who knows the origination and dissolution of beings, their coming and going, and both knowledge and ignorance -- he is rightly called Bhagavan.' Here too, 'of beings' connects with each term; the words 'origination' and 'dissolution' also indicate, by implication, their respective causes; 'coming' and 'going' mean the future gain and loss of fortune. Such is the meaning of the word 'Bhagavan,' and it culminates precisely in Sri Vasudeva, and is accordingly so applied: this impurity -- this turning away from one's own dharma, preceded by compassion, delusion, and the shedding of tears -- disgraceful in the eyes of the wise, has come upon you, foremost among all kshatriyas, in this fearful predicament -- from what cause has it arisen? Is it from a desire for liberation, or from a desire for heaven, or from a desire for fame? -- this threefold possibility is raised by the word 'kim' (what/whence). He then negates all three causes, through the three qualifying terms in the second half of the verse. 'Not embraced by the noble' means: not accepted by seekers of liberation -- for how could those seeking liberation through their own dharma, by way of purifying their inner disposition, yet whose passions are not fully ripened, ever abandon their own dharma? One whose passions are fully ripened and who is qualified for renunciation will be spoken of later. 'Unfit for heaven' -- since it opposes the very dharma that is the cause of heaven, it is not to be adopted out of desire for heaven. 'Productive of infamy' -- since it produces either the absence of fame or actual disgrace, it is not to be adopted out of desire for fame. Thus it is to be shunned by those desiring liberation, those desiring heaven, and those desiring fame alike. Yet you are pursuing precisely such a thing -- alas, how improper is your conduct! -- this is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
He states that very utterance. 'Sri Bhagavan uvacha...': from what cause -- 'tva' meaning 'you.' In this predicament, this bewilderment -- 'kashmalam' -- has come upon you, that is, this delusion has been attained. Since it is not embraced by the noble, it is unfit for heaven, contrary to dharma, and productive of infamy.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
In response to the expectation of what that utterance was, he says, in 'Sri Bhagavan...': from what cause has this impurity -- this turning away from battle, which is your own dharma -- come upon you, foremost of heroes, at this inopportune moment? Since it is embraced by the wicked, the unnoble, it is therefore devoid of both seen and unseen fruit, he says. By the two qualifiers 'unfit for heaven' and 'productive of infamy,' its unworthiness of heaven, being a cause of misfortune, and its producing infamy are conveyed. Is it from a desire for liberation, or from a desire for heaven, or from a desire for fame? -- this is raised by the word 'kim.' He then negates all three causes, through the three qualifying terms in the second half of the verse. Some say 'not embraced by the noble' means 'na aryaih jushtam' (not embraced by the noble); others say that although the alternative parsing 'aryair ajushtam' (unembraced by the noble) yields the same sense, it should be disregarded owing to the fault of word-order inversion. Suggesting that, by performing the battle that is your own dharma, you will not incur sin of a defiling nature, he addresses him, saying 'Arjuna.' Some say the sense is: this is unbefitting you, renowned by the name Arjuna; others say the sense is: O Arjuna, of pure disposition, this is unbefitting you.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Now begins the second chapter, whose subject is the removal of grief. Even though there is no break in Sanjaya's speech, the indication 'Sanjaya said' is given to forestall the doubt that this might be someone else's utterance, in the form of marking the start of a new chapter. He explains the three verses beginning 'Tam tatha...', in 'Evam...'. To indicate that everything up to 'vishidantam' is a recapitulation of what was stated in the previous chapter, it is said 'Evam upavishte Parthe' (Partha having thus sat down). 'Tatha' means 'in an improper place.' 'Kripa' is inner despondency; hence, being overcome even by outward grief, his eyes were filled and confused with tears, is the sense. 'Vishidantam' means having attained despondency in the manner described in the previous chapter. By the word 'Madhusudana,' his capacity to destroy the rajas and tamas that are the root of grief is suggested. 'Asthane' (out of place) carries the sense implied by the word 'vishama' (uneven, improper). 'Kashmalam' here is akin to a swoon; this is established from the earlier context, 'shoka-samvigna-manasah' (1.47). The words 'Arjuna,' 'Partha,' 'Parantapa' -- indicating his renowned lineage, valour, learning and the like -- carry, by virtue of his being Kunti's son, an implied reproach directed at you; this is the sense intended by the word 'akshipya' (having reproached). The word 'kutah' (whence) too, imputing causality to what is a mere semblance of a cause, carries an implied rebuke. 'Parantapa' means one who scorches the enemy. 'Klaibyam' here means cowardice; this is elaborated by the word 'hridaya-daurbalya' (weakness of heart). Since the qualifiers used in the previous verse also serve as grounds for the impropriety of cowardice, they are here brought into relevance in meaning, through the phrase 'tam imam vishamastham' and the rest. Those whose discernment has gone far from untruth are the 'aryas,' the wise; others are 'anaryas.' In the word 'asvargyam' (not conducive to heaven), the word 'svarga,' without qualification, denotes merely the next world in general. And since the negative prefix here, functioning to indicate opposition, opposes -- through the fruit intended by the word 'svargya' (conducive to heaven) -- the very thing the word denotes, it is rendered as 'contrary to the next world.' Since the word 'kshudra' (mean, petty) admits of no narrowing here that would restrict it to some relative pettiness, and considering that this quality is attributed to the greater Arjuna in such a state, the word 'atikshudram' (utterly mean) is used to show that a pettiness reaching its extreme limit is intended. Whether by figuratively transferring the cause's name to the effect, or by intending the fruit stated in the surrounding verses, the phrase 'hridaya-daurbalya-kritam' (produced by weakness of heart) is used, meaning: produced by unsteadiness of heart. By the word 'Parantapa,' the contextually intended meaning is conveyed, supplying the elliptical sense: 'rise up for battle.'

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
He states the very utterance of the Lord, in 'Kutas tvam...': in this predicament, this being the moment for enthusiasm in battle, not for compassion -- O Arjuna, from where has this impurity come upon you at this juncture? From where has this delusion of yours arisen? The Lord declares this impurity to be born of self-willed ignorance. He characterizes the impurity through three qualifiers: 'unbefitting the noble' -- not embraced by those in whom nobility does not exist; 'unfit for heaven' -- that from which heaven does not exist, thereby stating its opposition to dharma; 'productive of infamy' -- destructive of fame, thereby stating that, through its destruction of kshatriya-dharma, it opposes one's own family dharma.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Rousing Arjuna, the Blessed Lord said, in 'Kutah...': 'kashmalam' means weakness of spirit. 'Vishame' means in the crisis of battle. 'Embraced by the unnoble' means embraced by cowards, not by heroes such as yourself -- or, alternatively parsed, 'not embraced by the noble.' Though the alternative parsing 'not embraced by the noble' yields the same sense, it should be disregarded owing to the fault of word-order inversion. Hence it is also 'unfit for heaven' and 'productive of infamy.' O Arjuna, of pure disposition -- the sense is: this is unbefitting you.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: My dear Arjuna, how have these impurities come upon you? They are not at all befitting a man who knows the value of life. They lead not to higher planets but to infamy.

 Kṛṣṇa and the Supreme Personality of Godhead are identical. Therefore Lord Kṛṣṇa is referred to as Bhagavān throughout the Gītā. Bhagavān is the ultimate in the Absolute Truth. The Absolute Truth is realized in three phases of understanding, namely Brahman, or the impersonal all-pervasive spirit; Paramātmā, or the localized aspect of the Supreme within the heart of all living entities; and Bhagavān, or the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Lord Kṛṣṇa. In the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam (1.2.11) this conception of the Absolute Truth is explained thus: vadanti tat tattva-vidas tattvaṁ yaj jñānam advayam brahmeti paramātmeti bhagavān iti śabdyate “The Absolute Truth is realized in three phases of understanding by the knower of the Absolute Truth, and all of them are identical. Such phases of the Absolute Truth are expressed as Brahman, Paramātmā and Bhagavān.” These three divine aspects can be explained by the example of the sun, which also has three different aspects, namely the sunshine, the sun’s surface and the sun planet itself. One who studies the sunshine only is the preliminary student. One who understands the sun’s surface is further advanced. And one who can enter into the sun planet is the highest. Ordinary students who are satisfied by simply understanding the sunshine – its universal pervasiveness and the glaring effulgence of its impersonal nature – may be compared to those who can realize only the Brahman feature of the Absolute Truth. The student who has advanced still further can know the sun disc, which is compared to knowledge of the Paramātmā feature of the Absolute Truth. And the student who can enter into the heart of the sun planet is compared to those who realize the personal features of the Supreme Absolute Truth. Therefore, the bhaktas, or the transcendentalists who have realized the Bhagavān feature of the Absolute Truth, are the topmost transcendentalists, although all students who are engaged in the study of the Absolute Truth are engaged in the same subject matter. The sunshine, the sun disc and the inner affairs of the sun planet cannot be separated from one another, and yet the students of the three different phases are not in the same category. The Sanskrit word bhagavān is explained by the great authority Parāśara Muni, the father of Vyāsadeva. The Supreme Personality who possesses all riches, all strength, all fame, all beauty, all knowledge and all renunciation is called Bhagavān. There are many persons who are very rich, very powerful, very beautiful, very famous, very learned and very much detached, but no one can claim that he possesses all riches, all strength, etc., entirely. Only Kṛṣṇa can claim this because He is the Supreme Personality of Godhead. No living entity, including Brahmā, Lord Śiva or Nārāyaṇa, can possess opulences as fully as Kṛṣṇa. Therefore it is concluded in the Brahma-saṁhitā by Lord Brahmā himself that Lord Kṛṣṇa is the Supreme Personality of Godhead. No one is equal to or above Him. He is the primeval Lord, or Bhagavān, known as Govinda, and He is the supreme cause of all causes: īśvaraḥ paramaḥ kṛṣṇaḥ sac-cid-ānanda-vigrahaḥ anādir ādir govindaḥ sarva-kāraṇa-kāraṇam “There are many personalities possessing the qualities of Bhagavān, but Kṛṣṇa is the supreme because none can excel Him. He is the Supreme Person, and His body is eternal, full of knowledge and bliss. He is the primeval Lord Govinda and the cause of all causes.” ( Brahma-saṁhitā 5.1) In the Bhāgavatam also there is a list of many incarnations of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, but Kṛṣṇa is described as the original Personality of Godhead, from whom many, many incarnations and Personalities of Godhead expand: ete cāṁśa-kalāḥ puṁsaḥ kṛṣṇas tu bhagavān svayam indrāri-vyākulaṁ lokaṁ mṛḍayanti yuge yuge “All the lists of the incarnations of Godhead submitted herewith are either plenary expansions or parts of the plenary expansions of the Supreme Godhead, but Kṛṣṇa is the Supreme Personality of Godhead Himself.” ( Bhāg. 1.3.28) Therefore, Kṛṣṇa is the original Supreme Personality of Godhead, the Absolute Truth, the source of both the Supersoul and the impersonal Brahman. In the presence of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Arjuna’s lamentation for his kinsmen is certainly unbecoming, and therefore Kṛṣṇa expressed His surprise with the word kutaḥ, “wherefrom.” Such impurities were never expected from a person belonging to the civilized class of men known as Āryans. The word āryan is applicable to persons who know the value of life and have a civilization based on spiritual realization. Persons who are led by the material conception of life do not know that the aim of life is realization of the Absolute Truth, Viṣṇu, or Bhagavān, and they are captivated by the external features of the material world, and therefore they do not know what liberation is. Persons who have no knowledge of liberation from material bondage are called non-Āryans. Although Arjuna was a kṣatriya, he was deviating from his prescribed duties by declining to fight. This act of cowardice is described as befitting the non-Āryans. Such deviation from duty does not help one in the progress of spiritual life, nor does it even give one the opportunity to become famous in this world. Lord Kṛṣṇa did not approve of the so-called compassion of Arjuna for his kinsmen.
