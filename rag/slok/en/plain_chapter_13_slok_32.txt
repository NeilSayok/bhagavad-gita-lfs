Life Application:
Being beginningless and without qualities, this imperishable supreme Self does not act nor is tainted, though dwelling in body.

Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Even though the self-luminous Atman, by Its mere presence, enables the body, senses, and other equipments to function, the Atman itself always remains a non-doer. Understanding this declaration of the scriptures is difficult for beginners of Vedanta. Therefore, the Rishis of the Upanishads have taken special pains to explain to us how the one without a second, all-pervading Supreme Self is a non-doer. It has been stated earlier in the Gita that by identifying with the field (kshetra), the Atman becomes the knower of the field (kshetrajna) in the form of a living entity, who is the doer of actions and enjoyer of results. Here are some reasons given to prove the taintlessness of the Atman even though dwelling in bodies. When a judge, Shri Gopal Rao, sentences a murderer to death, the sin of that death cannot affect the judge. Shri Gopal Rao pronounces the judgment in his capacity as a judge in the court, and not in his personal capacity.

Without Beginning: A thing that has a cause can also have a beginning. To be without a beginning means to be causeless. The Supreme Truth is that from which the entire universe has arisen. Therefore, the Supreme Self, being the causeless cause, is called beginningless. For this very reason, It is also immutable and imperishable.

Without Qualities (Nirguna): Only a thing with qualities is subject to modification. We have seen that the Supreme Self, the cause of the universe, is changeless; hence It must also be without qualities.

This Supreme Self is Imperishable: Being the cause of the universe, beginningless, and without qualities, the imperishability of the Supreme Self is established.

This Supreme Self, by Its mere presence, enables the inert equipments to behave as if they are conscious, but Itself does not perform any action.

The above principle is one of the subtle concepts of Vedanta, and students of weak intellect often find it difficult to understand. Even though this is considered a difficult part of Vedantic literature, diligent contemplation can resolve all doubts and difficulties. In order to demonstrate the non-doership and qualityless nature of the Atman even in all forbidden and demoniac actions of the equipments, the Lord gives some illustrations.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:

Commentary: —
'anāditvān-nirguṇatvāt-paramātmāyam-avyayaḥ' — That which was called beginningless in the nineteenth verse of this chapter, has also been called beginningless here by the word 'anāditvāt', which means this Purusha is without a beginning (origin). Now the question arises that there Nature (Prakriti) was also called beginningless, so what is the difference between Nature and Purusha? In response to this, the Lord says — 'nirguṇatvāt', i.e., this Purusha is devoid of qualities (modes). Prakriti is indeed beginningless, but it is not devoid of qualities; rather, it is full of qualities and modifications. The three qualities — Sattva, Rajas, and Tamas — and modifications are born from it. But the Purusha is completely free from these three qualities and modifications (attribute-less and changeless). Such a Purusha is directly the imperishable Supreme Self itself, i.e., this Purusha is the changeless, supreme pure Self.
'śarīrastho’pi kaunteya na karoti na lipyate' — This Purusha, even while residing in the body, neither does anything nor is tainted by any action. The significance is that this Purusha (the self) has neither done anything in any state in the past, nor does it do anything in the present, nor can it do anything in the future; that is, this Purusha is eternally untainted by Nature, unattached, devoid of qualities, and imperishable. There is no doership or enjoyership in it at all.
The purpose of saying 'śarīrastho’pi' (even while dwelling in the body) here is that even when this Purusha, considering itself situated in the body, regards itself as the doer of action and the enjoyer of pleasure and pain, at that time also, in reality, it remains indifferent, as mere illumination. The perception of pleasure and pain occurs because of it, hence it can be called the illuminator, but it does not possess the quality of being an illuminator.
Here, the word 'api' (even) suggests that every living being (from an ant to Brahma) who considers himself situated in the body since beginningless time, is by nature eternally untainted and unattached. Its union with the body never actually occurred because the body, being an effect of Nature, always resides only in Nature, and the self, being a part of the Supreme Lord, always resides only in the Supreme Lord. The self can never be separated from the Supreme Lord. Even when assuming identification with the body, no matter how much it blends with the body, and even when considering the body as its own self, its untainted nature is never destroyed; by nature, it remains eternally untainted. Even when its untainted nature is not realized, there is no modification in its true nature. Therefore, it has never done anything by its own nature, nor does it do so, nor has it ever been tainted, nor does it get tainted.
Although the Purusha becomes a doer and enjoyer only by assuming itself to be situated in the body, yet the Lord said in the twenty-first verse that the Purusha situated in Nature becomes the enjoyer, and here He says that despite being situated in the body, the Purusha is not a doer or enjoyer. The significance of saying so is that Nature and its effect, the body, are both one and the same. Therefore, whether you say the Purusha is situated in Nature or situated in the body, it is the same thing. By having a connection with one body, it gets connected with Prakriti as a whole, i.e., with all bodies. In reality, the Purusha has no connection either with the individual (vyashti) body or with the cosmic (samashti) Nature. It is only by assuming its connection with the body that it considers itself a doer and enjoyer. In reality, it is neither a doer nor an enjoyer.
Connection — In the previous verse it was said that the Purusha neither acts nor is tainted. Now the question arises, how does it not get tainted and how does it not act? The answer to this is given in the next verse.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Anadivat, being without beginning: Adih means cause; that which has no cause is anadih. That which has a cause undergoes loss of its own characteristics. But this One, being causeless, has no parts. This being so, It does not suffer loss.
So also, nirgunatvat, being without alities: indeed, It si only something possessing alities that perishes owing to the losss of its alities. But this One, being without alities, does not perish. Hence, ayam, this; paramatma, supreme Self; is avyayah, immutable. It suffers no depletion. Therefore It is immutable. Since this is so, therefore, api, although; sarira-sthah, existing in the body-since the perception of the Self occurs in the bodies, It is said to be 'existing in the body'; even then, It na, does not; karoti, act. From the very fact that It does not act, It na, is not; lipyate, affected by the result of any action. For, one who is an agent of action becomes affected by its result. But this One is not an agent. Hence It is not affected by any result. This is the meaning.
Objection: Who is it, again, that acts in the body and becomes affected? On the one hand, if there be some embodied being other than the supreme Self who acts and becomes affected, then it has been improper to say in, 'And also understand Me to be the Knower of the field,' etc., that the Knower of the field and God are one. Again, if there be no embodied being who is different from God, then it has to be stated who is it that acts and gets affected. Or it has to be asserted that the supreme One does not exist. [If the supreme One also acts like us, then He is no God.]
Thus, since the Upanisadic philosophy as stated by the Lord is in every way difficult to understand and difficult to explain, it has therefore been abandoned by the Vaisesikas, the Sankhyas, the Jainas and the Buddhists.
Reply: As to that, the following refutation has been stated by the Lord Himself in, 'But it is Nature that acts' (5.14). Indeed, Nature, which is nothing but ignorance, acts and becomes affected. In this way empirical dealing becomes possible; but in reality it does not occur in the one supreme Self.
It has been accordingly shown by the Lord in various places that there is no duty to be performed by those who adhere to this philosophy of discriminating knowledge of the supreme Reality, who are steadfast in Knowledge, who have spurned actions arising out of ignorance, and who are mendicants belonging to the highest Order of monks.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
By using the restatement 'avyayah' (imperishable), only the absence of taint is ordained. To refute any contrary understanding, the commentator says, 'na cha' (nor) etc. By this, it is stated that being imperishable (avyayatva) is also to be established since it is not already established. The view that this is an adjective of the Jiva (individual soul) to justify the negation of taint because taint is possible in the Jiva, is refuted. For, even for Vishnu, the status of 'being in the body' has been stated. To show that being beginningless and qualityless are the causes of imperishability, the pervasion (vyapti) is stated: 'sadi' (having a beginning) etc. The construction is: that which is subject to decay is generally beginningless and consists of qualities. To avoid fallacy (vyabhichara) in the case of antecedent non-existence (pragabhava), the word 'prayah' (generally) is used. Or, with the determination that what has a beginning is indeed composed of qualities, the construction is taken as it is. By saying 'He does not act because He is without qualities', it might seem that there is a complete absence of actions and attributes in Ishvara; hence he says 'na karoti' (does not act) etc. He recalls what was said earlier: 'na' (not) etc. The rest of the explanation is that His actionless nature is to be understood like 'na prajnam' etc. Although imperishability is also to be established here, yet it can be known from common experience; thus, only the absence of taint is appropriate, as in 'yatha sarvagatam' (just as the all-pervading) etc.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
The commentator explains that the Purusha (self) naturally has no connection with Prakriti (material nature) by saying 'anaditvat' (being without beginning) etc. The Supreme Self is the indwelling Purusha; or, by being of the nature of the Imperishable (Akshara), being beginningless and devoid of qualities, this Jivatma (individual soul) Purusha is immutable. Even though separated by the Lord's will, it remains a non-doer and untainted. It appears to be otherwise only due to superimposition (adhyasa), and not in reality — this is the meaning.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Dispelling the doubt that although the Self is naturally a non-doer, it might acquire conditional doership due to its connection with the body, the Lord elaborates on the statement 'He who sees the Self as a non-doer, he truly sees' by saying 'anaditvat' (being without beginning) etc. This directly perceived Supreme Self, which is non-different from the Supreme Lord, the innermost Self, is 'avyayah' — it does not decay (vyeti), hence it is imperishable; meaning it is free from all modifications. Here, decay is of two kinds: either by the origin of the substratum itself, or, even if the substratum is unborn, by the origin and change of its attributes. Of these, the first kind is refuted by 'anaditvat'. 'Adi' means the state of prior non-existence. That does not exist for the eternally existent Self; therefore, due to the absence of a cause, it has no birth. Indeed, birth is not possible for that which is beginningless. In the absence of birth, the subsequent modifications of existence (such as growth, decay, death) are also not possible. Thus, it does not decay in its own nature. The second kind of decay is refuted by 'nirgunatvat', meaning it is attribute-less. For, with respect to the substratum, no attribute comes or goes, because of the identity between the attribute and the substratum. This Self is attribute-less, so it does not decay through attributes either, as supported by the Shruti: 'Verily, this Self is imperishable, of indestructible nature'. Since it is free from the six modifications of existence — birth, existence, growth, change, decay, and destruction — even though residing in the body through a relationship of superimposition (adhyasa), this Self does not act when the body acts. Just as the sun reflected in water, though the water moves, does not move at all, similarly. Since It does not perform any action at all, therefore It is not tainted by any fruit of action; for he who performs an action is tainted by its fruit, but not this Self, because It is a non-doer. This is because desire, hatred, pleasure, pain, etc., are declared to be the attributes of the field (kshetra), and actions are said to be performed by Nature (Prakriti), denoting them as effects of Maya. Therefore, the cessation of the eligibility for all actions for the knowers of the ultimate truth has been explained earlier. By this declaration of the attribute-less nature of the Self, the internal difference (svagata-bheda) is also refuted. By the phrase 'actions are performed by Prakriti alone', the homogeneous difference (sajatiya-bheda) is refuted; by the phrase 'when he perceives the separate existence of beings', the heterogeneous difference (vijatiya-bheda) is refuted; and by the phrase 'being beginningless and without qualities', the internal difference (svagata-bheda) is refuted. Thus, it is established that the non-dual Brahman alone is the Self.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Still, in the state of worldly existence, since the connection of the Supreme Lord with the body and actions makes pleasure, pain, and other inequalities unavoidable, how is equal vision possible? To this, the Lord replies with 'anaditvat' (being without beginning) etc. Indeed, only that which is born undergoes decay and destruction. And that object which has qualities undergoes decay when its qualities are destroyed. But this Supreme Self is beginningless and without qualities, hence It is immutable, meaning changeless. Therefore, even though residing in the body, It does not act, nor is It tainted by the fruits of action.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
An objection: Since the one Self is equally situated everywhere, it should acquire doership etc. associated with the body etc., just as the pure water of the Ganges acquires impurity when associated with impure bodies of creatures. To this, the Lord answers: 'anaditvat' (being without beginning) etc. This Supreme Self is immutable, free from decay, i.e., changeless (kutastha). Here, decay is of three kinds: by its own nature, through its parts, or through its qualities. To state that decay by its own nature is not possible for Supreme Brahman, the word 'paramatma' is used. To show the impossibility of the second kind (decay through parts), the cause is stated: 'anaditvat'. 'Adi' means cause; that which has no cause is beginningless (anadi). Objects like pots, having a beginning, are composed of parts, and thus their decay is seen. But for the Self, being beginningless and therefore partless, decay through parts is not possible. The third kind of decay is refuted by 'nirgunatvat' (being without qualities). Similarly, a thing with qualities decays due to the decay of its qualities, but this Self, being without qualities, does not decay. Thus, decay through qualities is not possible for It. Since this is so, even though residing in the body — since the perception of the Self occurs in the bodies, It is said to be 'residing in the body'. Even though situated in the body due to all-pervasiveness and being the self of all, It does not act either by Itself or through the body, because It is changeless (kutastha) and the body is superimposed on It. Because of the absence of doership, It is not tainted by the fruits of action. The intent of addressing Arjuna as 'Kaunteya' (son of Kunti) is to show: 'Even for you who reside in the body born of Kunti, there is no doership or taint.' Objection: If so, who is it that resides in the body, acts, and gets tainted? Is he different from the aforementioned actionless and untainted Self, or is he non-different? The first view is not correct, because then statements declaring the oneness of the Knower of the Field and the Lord, such as 'Thou art That' and 'Understand Me to be the Knower of the field', would become invalid. On the second view, if there is no doership etc. in the Supreme Self, then the substratum of the perceived doership must be stated. Thus, it seems this is in every way difficult to explain. If so, we reply: No. For the Lord Himself has explained in 'But it is Nature that acts' (5.14) that it is only Nature (Avidya/ignorance) which acts and gets tainted, and that doership etc. during the empirical state are illusory (caused by ignorance). And thus, this illusory doership in Brahman, which is non-different from the inner Self, is not real. This Upanishadic philosophy of ultimate truth as stated by the Lord, being difficult to understand, was abandoned by the Vaisesikas, the Sankhyas, etc., due to their confusion-born doubts mentioned earlier. But for those who are steadfast in this knowledge, liberation is attained by the cessation of the doership imagined by ignorance, and not for those who adhere to the systems imagined by Sankhyas etc., which are like silver in mother-of-pearl.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Through the verse 'anaditvat' (being without beginning) etc., the Lord firmly establishes the previously mentioned differences of the self, such as eternality, non-eternality (differentiation from the body), and changelessness, along with their reasons. Explaining the intent of the relative term 'ayam paramatma' (this supreme self), the commentator says 'extracting from the body' etc. Objection: Since the contraction of knowledge as a form of decay (vyaya) and the doership in the form of prompting the body etc. are established by direct perception etc., how can it be called immutable (avyaya) etc.? To this, the Lord answers 'defined by nature'. By 'anaditvat' which is the cause of immutability, the absence of origin is intended, which is clarified by 'due to not being originated'. For, only that which is originated is seen to undergo decay. To prevent the denial of general qualities like knowledge by 'nirgunatvat', the commentator says 'being devoid of Sattva and other Gunas'. Thus, according to the power of the two reasons, the appropriate connection with the two objects to be established is shown. The doubt of taint and its negation is proper only in a possible context. Since the body, declared by scriptures as 'consisting of seven humors, three impurities, two sources, and sustained by four kinds of food', has a natural tendency to taint, just as mud taints a cloth, the commentator explains the implication of 'even though dwelling in the body' by 'with the attributes of the body (It is not tainted)'.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
An objection: Just as the individual soul (Jiva), being a part of Brahman, gets tainted by actions due to its connection with the body, which leads to ignorance, and the destruction of that ignorance occurs through connection with the prompting Supreme Self; similarly, if the Supreme Self gets tainted by connection with the Jiva, then how can equal vision be possible? To this doubt, the Lord replies with 'anaditvat' (being without beginning) etc. Indeed, only that which has an origin undergoes destruction due to connection with another. But the Supreme Self is beginningless, It does not have an origin like the ignorance-born Jiva-state; therefore, to show the absence of Its connection with Prakriti, Its status as a witness was described earlier. Thus, only that which is connected with the qualities undergoes destruction when they are destroyed; but He is without qualities (nirguna). Therefore, this Supreme Self is immutable (avyaya), i.e., free from destruction caused by connection with another etc. Hence, even though dwelling in the body, It does not perform actions, and therefore It is not tainted.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Objection: Granted that doership is not accepted for the Self in its all-pervading form, but it must be accepted in its form limited by the body etc.; otherwise it would contradict experience. To remove this doubt, the Lord says: 'anaditvat' (being without beginning) etc. This Self, which is eternally directly perceived by all living beings, is the supreme one, different from the non-supreme selves like the body etc., and beyond the five sheaths (koshas); hence It is the Supreme Self (Paramatma). It is 'avyayah' — it does not decay, i.e., it is not limited by space, time, and object; hence It is immutable. The cause of immutability is 'anaditvat'. Indeed, that which has a beginning, like space (ether) etc., decays, but this Self does not decay because It is beginningless. Objection: Since that which is beginningless is necessarily infinite, let there be no limitation of the Self by time. And since that which is limited in space must necessarily perish, which is incompatible with being beginningless, let there also be no limitation of Brahman in space. If you say it is like an atom, we say: No. For a substance which has different parts limited by space and directions, the atom-ness in the form of partlessness is not established. For, the part of an atom limited by the eastern direction cannot be separated from the western part, as it contradicts experience. Because of the absence of limitation by space, let there be no limitation caused by the existence of homogeneous or heterogeneous objects. Yet, for the Supreme Being, who is endowed with wonderful powers, skilled in creating the wonderful universe, and possessed of qualities like lordship over all and omniscience, internal difference (svagata-bheda) is inevitable. One must accept Its doership of the universe in Its form limited by Its power, Maya, and Its doership of rituals like Agnihotra when limited by the body. To remove this doubt, the Lord says: 'nirgunatvat' (being without qualities). For, that which has qualities, like space (ether) etc., upon obtaining a limiting adjunct (upadhi) like conjunction or disjunction, manifests its quality, sound; but it cannot manifest touch, which is absent in it, by any adjunct whatsoever. Similarly, the Self is completely devoid of qualities; hence, even when it obtains limitation, it is not capable of manifesting qualities like doership. The Lord states the conclusion: 'even though residing in the body' etc. The meaning of this is clear.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Those with the vision of eternity can see that the imperishable soul is transcendental, eternal, and beyond the modes of nature. Despite contact with the material body, O Arjuna, the soul neither does anything nor is entangled.

 A living entity appears to be born because of the birth of the material body, but actually the living entity is eternal; he is not born, and in spite of his being situated in a material body, he is transcendental and eternal. Thus he cannot be destroyed. By nature he is full of bliss. He does not engage himself in any material activities; therefore the activities performed due to his contact with material bodies do not entangle him.
