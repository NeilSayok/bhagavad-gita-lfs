Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The word Kshatriya here does not refer merely to a caste fixed by birth, but points to certain distinct tendencies of the mind. A person of Kshatriya temperament is one in whom strength and enthusiasm surge so powerfully that he can protect the weak and the poor while guarding the nation against the enemies of its culture. According to Hindu ethics, one endowed with such qualities of leadership should himself never become a destroyer or aggressor against culture; yet cowardly non-resistance to unrighteousness is equally foreign to Hindu tradition. Whenever such a righteous opportunity arises, it is the duty of Kshatriyas to seize it as a golden chance and protect the nation. Such righteous wars are like an open door to the attainment of heaven. It is worth noting here how Lord Krishna, while presenting His arguments, descends from the highest Vedantic truth to the level of the materialist, and even further, examining the situation from the standpoint of an ordinary worldly person. Through these varied viewpoints He proves to Arjuna that it is right for him to fight. Fighting is certainly your duty, and if you now flee from it...

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
An objection is raised: since war involves the killing of many beings, including one's own teachers, and thus conflicts with the scriptures on non-violence, it cannot be a duty. This is answered by pointing out that, just as violence in sacrifices such as the Agnishomiya is prescribed for a Kshatriya, so too is fighting; a specific scriptural injunction overrides a general one. A further doubt arises: since those who engage in battle appear to gain no happiness either here or hereafter, would it not be better to cease from it? This is answered by the word 'yadrcchaya' (of its own accord): those who perform sacrifices attain heaven only after a long time, whereas Kshatriyas who fight without turning away enjoy the fruits of heaven at once; hence war is indeed your duty. The purport is that, since fighting - their own natural duty - secures the future happiness of Kshatriyas both here and hereafter, they should rise to it, casting aside grief and delusion.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
A war that has come of its own accord is itself an open door to heaven. O son of Pritha, those Kshatriyas who obtain such a war are truly fortunate. During the game of dice, Duryodhana had laid down the condition that if the Pandavas lost, they would have to spend twelve years in exile in the forest and a thirteenth year incognito, after which their kingdom would be restored; but if discovered during the year of hiding, they would have to repeat the twelve years of exile. Having lost, the Pandavas duly completed the exile and the year of hiding, yet when they later asked for their kingdom, Duryodhana declared he would not give even as much land as the point of a needle without a fight. Despite repeated offers of peace from the Pandavas' side, Duryodhana refused reconciliation. Therefore the Lord tells Arjuna that this war has come to you unsought; for a Kshatriya who dies fighting valiantly in such a righteous war that has come of its own accord, the gate of heaven stands open. Such Kshatriyas, to whom this righteous war has come, are truly happy - meaning that the happiness found in the performance of one's own duty is not the happiness found in enjoying worldly pleasures, for even animals and birds enjoy worldly pleasure. Hence those who get the opportunity to perform their duty should be considered truly fortunate.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Why, again, does that battle become a duty? This is being answered (as follows) [A specific rule is more authoritative than a general rule. Non-violence is a general rule enjoined by the scriptures, but the duty of fighting is a specific rule for a Ksatriya.]: Partha, O son of Partha; are not those Ksatiryas sukhinah, happy [Happy in this world as also in the other.] who labhante, come across; a yuddham, battle; idrsam, of this kind; upapannam, which presents itself; yadrcchaya, unsought for; and which is an apavrtam, open; svarga-dvaram, gate to heaven? [Rites and duties like sacrifices etc. yield their results after the lapse of some time. But the Ksatriyas go to heaven immediatley after dying in battle, because, unlike the minds of others, their minds remaind fully engaged in their immediate duty.]

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
This is now established through the word 'yadrcchaya'. The 'happy' ones are the fortunate, for whom such a war becomes a gateway to heaven, in accordance with the statement of the Bhagavatam (6.10.33): 'Two kinds of death are here regarded as auspicious and hard to attain.'

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
An objection may be raised: even if war is a duty, is it proper to wage it against revered elders like Bhishma and Drona, since that seems utterly reprehensible? This is answered by the word 'yadrcchaya' - unsought, without any effort of your own, this war involving such heroic elders has come about, offering fame and a kingdom as its visible fruit if victorious, or heaven if defeated. Therefore Kshatriyas who obtain such a war, whichever the outcome, are truly blessed. The phrase 'the gate of heaven thrown open' shows this war leads to heaven without delay or obstruction, unlike sacrifices such as the Jyotishtoma, which yield their fruit only after a long time and depend on further conditions. This also removes the fear of sin such as attaches to acts like the Shyena sacrifice, since killing an enemy in war is not the killing forbidden by scripture, and Manu declares that kings who fight to the best of their strength without turning back attain heaven. Nor is there any sin in slaying teachers or Brahmins like Bhishma and Drona here, since they have become assailants (atatayin); Manu rules that an assailant advancing to kill, even if he be one's teacher, a child, an elder, a Brahmin, or a great scholar, may rightly be slain without hesitation, and no sin of killing accrues to the one who slays an assailant. Thus Arjuna's earlier objection, 'how can we be happy after killing our own kinsmen,' stands refuted.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Moreover, when so great a good has presented itself unsought, why should there be any wavering? This is explained through the word 'yadrcchaya'. Only the fortunate obtain, unsought and unasked, such a war as this, for it is nothing but an unobstructed gateway to heaven. Or else, the meaning is that only those who obtain such a war are truly the happy ones. By this, Arjuna's earlier objection - 'how can we be happy after killing our own kinsmen?' - stands refuted.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Although war is a Kshatriya's own duty which he must ordinarily strive to bring about, in your case it has come about through good fortune, without any effort on your part; hence it is explained through the word 'yadrcchaya' that it must certainly be undertaken. Having come unsought, and granting heaven at once since it throws open the very gate of heaven, only those Kshatriyas who obtain such a war are truly happy, sharing in the joys of a kingdom, heaven and the like. By addressing Arjuna as 'Partha', Krishna spurs him toward an effort worthy of his own valor.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Once again, under the guise of praising war - even though it involves the killing of living beings - the Lord uproots Arjuna's misconception that it is unrighteous. This is conveyed through the word 'yadrcchaya'. To dispel the notion that this war is a chance accident without real cause, it is explained that it comes 'unsought' now only because it is the fruit ripening from Arjuna's own past great merit. The term signifies the highest, unmixed happiness, free from heat, cold, or any trace of sorrow - happiness sought and attained, of which heaven is the abode; and by their very nature, righteous acts are themselves the means to such unsurpassed happiness, any apparent difference arising only from obstacles like attachment to results. The word 'apavrtam' (open) conveys that it is unobstructed. 'Sukhinah' (happy) here means 'meritorious', for happiness alone could not be the cause of obtaining such a war; rather, the word points to the means of happiness, or else denotes fitness for happiness, which is itself meritoriousness.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Therefore, only the truly fortunate obtain such a war, as shown by the word 'yadrcchaya' - come about by the Lord's own will. 'Apavrtam' means the door of heaven thrown wide open. Such a war is obtained by those Kshatriyas who are happy, that is, fortunate. Their good fortune in gaining such a war lies in its being in accord with the Lord's will and coming through the Lord's own presence.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Further, the word 'yadrcchaya' means that this war has presented itself even though unsought; the gate of heaven stands open, thrown wide; those Kshatriyas who obtain such a war are indeed happy and blessed - this is the intended connection.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
O Pārtha, happy are the kṣatriyas to whom such fighting opportunities come unsought, opening for them the doors of the heavenly planets.

 As supreme teacher of the world, Lord Kṛṣṇa condemns the attitude of Arjuna, who said, “I do not find any good in this fighting. It will cause perpetual habitation in hell.” Such statements by Arjuna were due to ignorance only. He wanted to become nonviolent in the discharge of his specific duty. For a kṣatriya to be on the battlefield and to become nonviolent is the philosophy of fools. In the Parāśara-smṛti, or religious codes made by Parāśara, the great sage and father of Vyāsadeva, it is stated: kṣatriyo hi prajā rakṣan śastra-pāṇiḥ pradaṇḍayan nirjitya para-sainyādi kṣitiṁ dharmeṇa pālayet “The kṣatriya’s duty is to protect the citizens from all kinds of difficulties, and for that reason he has to apply violence in suitable cases for law and order. Therefore he has to conquer the soldiers of inimical kings, and thus, with religious principles, he should rule over the world.” Considering all aspects, Arjuna had no reason to refrain from fighting. If he should conquer his enemies, he would enjoy the kingdom; and if he should die in the battle, he would be elevated to the heavenly planets, whose doors were wide open to him. Fighting would be for his benefit in either case.
