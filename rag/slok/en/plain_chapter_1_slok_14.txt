Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
It is a very ordinary fact that Lord Krishna and Arjuna answered the Kauravas' conch-blast by blowing their own conches. But the beauty and generosity with which Sanjaya describes this clearly indicates where his sympathies lay. He says: seated in a magnificent chariot yoked with white horses, Madhava and Arjuna blew their divine conches. This description suggests that Sanjaya's mind still clung to some hope -- that perhaps, seeing the contrast between the two sides' conch-blasts, Dhritarashtra might even now restrain his sons from war.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
After that, seated in a great chariot yoked with white horses, Lakshmi's Lord, Sri Krishna, and Pandu's son Arjuna, blew their divine conches very loudly.

Explanation: 'tatah shvetairhayairyukte' -- the Gandharva Chitraratha had given Arjuna a hundred divine horses. These horses had the special quality that however many were slain in battle, their number always remained a full hundred, never diminishing; they could travel to earth, heaven, and every other realm. Of these hundred horses, four beautiful, well-trained white ones were yoked to Arjuna's chariot.

'Mahati syandane sthitau' -- Agni had suffered indigestion from eating the ghee offered in sacrifices, and wished to cure it by consuming (burning) the unique herbs of the Khandava forest. But since the gods protected that forest, Agni could not succeed -- whenever he set it ablaze, Indra would put it out with rain. Finally, with Arjuna's help, Agni burned the entire forest and cured his indigestion, and, pleased, gave Arjuna this magnificent chariot. It could hold as many weapons as nine bullock-carts could carry. It was gold-plated and radiant, its wheels immensely strong and large, its banner flashing like lightning and streaming a full yojana (four kos) in length -- yet despite this length it carried no weight, never got stuck anywhere, and never caught on trees or the like. Hanuman resided upon that banner.

'Sthitau' -- the point is that with the Lord Sri Krishna Himself and His beloved devotee Arjuna seated upon that beautiful, radiant chariot, its glory and splendor increased manifold.

'Madhavah pandavashchaiva' -- 'Ma' means Lakshmi, and 'dhava' means husband; hence 'Madhava' means Lakshmi's Lord. Here 'Pandava' refers to Arjuna, since Arjuna is chief among all the Pandavas -- 'Panduvanam dhananjayah' (Gita 10.37). Arjuna was an incarnation of Nara, and Sri Krishna of Narayana; at the start of every parva of the Mahabharata, homage is paid to Nara (Arjuna) and Narayana (Sri Krishna): 'Narayanam namaskritya naram chaiva narottamam.' From this viewpoint, Sri Krishna and Arjuna were the two foremost in the Pandava army. Sanjaya himself says at the end of the Gita: 'Wherever Yogeshvara Sri Krishna and Gandiva-bow-bearing Arjuna are, there will be prosperity, victory, glory, and unshakeable policy' (18.78). 'Divyau shankhau pradadhmatuh' -- the conches in the hands of Sri Krishna and Arjuna were radiant and supernatural; they blew them very loudly.

One might ask: on the Kaurava side the chief commander is Grandsire Bhishma, so it is fitting that he blew the first conch; but on the Pandava side, with commander Dhrishtadyumna present, why did Lord Krishna, merely acting as charioteer, blow the first conch? The resolution: whether the Lord takes the role of charioteer or great warrior, His supremacy can never be diminished. Whatever position He occupies, He always remains the greatest -- for He is Achyuta, the one who never falls. In the Pandava army, the Lord Sri Krishna was indeed foremost, and He directed everyone. Even in His childhood, Nanda, Upananda and others obeyed His word; that is why, at the child Krishna's bidding, they abandoned the traditional worship of Indra and began worshipping Govardhana instead. The point is that the Lord, in whatever state, in whatever place, and wherever He may be, always remains foremost. That is why the Lord blew the first conch in the Pandava army.

One who is inherently small is regarded as great only because of a high position; so one who considers himself great merely because of a high post is, in truth, small himself. But one who is inherently great makes any place he occupies great by his very presence. So, since the Lord took up the position of charioteer here, that very position of charioteer itself became exalted.

Transition: Sanjaya now, over the next four verses, elaborates on the previous verse by describing the conch-blowing of the other warriors.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Dhrtarastra said - Sanjaya said  Duryodhana, after viewing the forces of Pandavas protected by Bhima, and his own forces protected by Bhisma conveyed his views thus to Drona, his teacher, about the adeacy of Bhima's forces for conering the Kaurava forces and the inadeacy of his own forces for victory against the Pandava forces. He was grief-stricken within.

Observing his (Duryodhana's) despondecny, Bhisma, in order to cheer him, roared like a lion, and then blowing his conch, made his side sound their conchs and kettle-drums, which made an uproar as a sign of victory. Then, having heard that great tumult, Arjuna and Sri Krsna the Lord of all lords, who was acting as the charioteer of Arjuna, sitting in their great chariot which was powerful enough to coner the three worlds; blew their divine conchs Srimad Pancajanya and Devadatta. Then, both Yudhisthira and Bhima blew their respective conchs separately. That tumult rent asunder the hearts of your sons, led by Duryodhana. The sons of Dhrtarastra then thought, 'Our cause is almost lost now itself.' So said Sanjaya to Dhrtarastra who was longing for their victory.

Sanjaya said to Dhrtarastra:  Then, seeing the Kauravas, who were ready for battle, Arjuna, who had Hanuman, noted for his exploit of burning Lanka, as the emblem on his flag on his chariot, directed his charioteer Sri Krsna, the Supreme Lord-who is overcome by parental love for those who take shelter in Him who is the treasure-house of knowledge, power, lordship, energy, potency and splendour, whose sportive delight brings about the origin, sustentation and dissolution of the entire cosmos at His will, who is the Lord of the senses, who controls in all ways the senses inner and outer of all, superior and inferior - by saying, 'Station my chariot in an appropriate place in order that I may see exactly my enemies who are eager for battle.'

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Although all the other warriors too were seated in chariots, the verse 'tatah shvetairhayairyukte' etc. specifically describes their chariot to show its unique excellence: for it stood in a chariot given by Agni, unassailable by anyone -- meaning, one that could not be conquered by any means at all. (The verse then names, further on, the conches: Panchajanya, Devadatta, Paundra, Anantavijaya, Sughosha, and Manipushpaka -- the point being that whereas each conch in the enemy's army is famous by its own name, in your own army not a single conch is famous by name, illustrating the enemy's superior distinction.) The word 'Hrishikesha' (Lord of the senses) is used to convey that, as the inner controller of all senses, He is the (true) helper of the Pandavas. The word 'Dhananjaya' (winner of wealth) conveys that Arjuna, having conquered all kings in his digvijaya campaign and brought back wealth, is utterly unconquerable. Bhimasena, whose deeds include the slaying of Hidimba etc., is called 'Vrikodara' (wolf-bellied) for his immense strength from eating vast quantities of food. 'Kuntiputra' (Yudhishthira) -- gained by Kunti through great austerity in worship of Dharma, himself the foremost king by having performed the Rajasuya sacrifice, and ever steady in claiming victory in battle -- his epithet conveys that he, not his opponents, will remain steadfast. It is understood that Nakula blew Sughosha and Sahadeva blew Manipushpaka. 'Parameshvasa' (great archer) refers to the king of Kashi, a mighty bowman. Satyaki, unconquered in great battles such as the abduction of the Parijata and the arrow-duel, is such a one. O Dhritarashtra, lord of the earth, listen calmly -- this is the sense conveyed by the address. The rest is easily understood.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
That sound became a source of valor for the enemy, not fear -- with this understanding, he describes their activity of sounding conches: after the tumultuous sound, Madhava, Lakshmi's Lord, and the son of Pandu, Arjuna, seated in a great and excellent chariot given by Agni, yoked with white horses of immense strength and prowess -- not trembling with fear -- blew their supernatural, divine conches, filling them fully. The word 'Madhava,' by its very sound, indicates that You (Duryodhana) will be deprived of your kingdom's fortune by him; and 'Pandava' indicates that the kingdom will belong to him alone.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Venkatanatha explains the passage from 'tatah shvetaih...' up to '...dhananjayah': the word 'tatah' (then) refers to what is being explained -- 'having heard that uproar' is its implied sense. The epithet 'Sarveshvareshvarah Parthasarathih' (the Lord of all lords, Partha's charioteer) conveys that the Supreme Person, greater than the greatest, out of loving devotion (vatsalya) for His devotee, condescended even below the status of an ordinary human, taking up the humble role of charioteer. Sanjaya's use of the word 'Madhava' here, to foreshadow the Pandavas' coming victory, refers precisely to His being Lakshmi's Lord -- that is, His supremacy over all lords. 'Seated in the chariot' -- stated in this general way -- is further distinguished by specifying the division of roles as charioteer and warrior. The words 'shvetaih' etc., having established the chariot's greatness, are followed by 'trailokya' (the three worlds) to clarify that the greatness meant is not merely one of size, but of cosmic significance. 'Making the three worlds tremble' means that even their mere sitting in that chariot was cause enough to shake all three worlds. This is confirmed by the exceptional force of the conch-blast described here ('divyau shankhau pradadhmatuh' etc.) and fulfilled later by the statement 'it rent both heaven and earth' (1.19).

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Thus, after hearing the conch-blast signaling battle-enthusiasm, battle-enthusiasm arose in the Pandava army too -- so it is said, in the next five verses. After the Kaurava side's action, seated in a chariot yoked with white horses -- auspicious-signifying horses (the epithet 'white' is used here both to affirm the Lord's charioteer-role and, since the Lord's own horses are of variegated color, to clarify this is not His own personal chariot but signifies readiness for battle) -- in a great chariot given by Agni, fit to carry the Lord, comparable to Garuda, called Nandighosha, Sri Krishna and Arjuna blew their divine conches. The word 'Madhava' hints that they will soon attain fortune (Lakshmi). The word 'Pandava' declares their righteousness. That the Lord's conch-blast, like everyone's, was also meant to crush pride -- Arjuna's included -- is conveyed by the words 'cha' and 'eva.'

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
On the other side, both Lord Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna, stationed on a great chariot drawn by white horses, sounded their transcendental conchshells.

 In contrast with the conchshell blown by Bhīṣmadeva, the conchshells in the hands of Kṛṣṇa and Arjuna are described as transcendental. The sounding of the transcendental conchshells indicated that there was no hope of victory for the other side because Kṛṣṇa was on the side of the Pāṇḍavas. Jayas tu pāṇḍu-putrāṇāṁ yeṣāṁ pakṣe janārdanaḥ. Victory is always with persons like the sons of Pāṇḍu because Lord Kṛṣṇa is associated with them. And whenever and wherever the Lord is present, the goddess of fortune is also there because the goddess of fortune never lives alone without her husband. Therefore, victory and fortune were awaiting Arjuna, as indicated by the transcendental sound produced by the conchshell of Viṣṇu, or Lord Kṛṣṇa. Besides that, the chariot on which both the friends were seated had been donated by Agni (the fire-god) to Arjuna, and this indicated that this chariot was capable of conquering all sides, wherever it was drawn over the three worlds.
