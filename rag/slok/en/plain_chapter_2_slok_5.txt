Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:
Rather would I content myself with a beggar's crust that kill these teachers of mine, these precious noble souls! To slay these masters who are my benefactors would be to stain the sweetness of life's pleasures with their blood.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Arjuna again presents an argument that seems exceedingly noble but is in fact devoid of substance, because, not understanding himself, he has failed to understand his own problem as well. Here he has called his teachers -- Bhishma and Drona -- 'mahanubhava,' meaning the ideal men of their age. They are the very embodiment of whatever is exalted and excellent in our culture, having made numerous sacrifices for the eternal dharma with a vast and generous heart. To destroy such exalted representative men of a whole age merely for the sake of personal power and the desire for a throne does not seem in any way proper. It is not merely that particular age that would suffer -- future generations too would be impoverished by the priceless severing of these great lives.

Arjuna says that rather than entertain the thought of destroying the finest flowers in the garden of culture, it would be far more proper for the Pandavas to live on the food of alms. Even the kingdom won by slaying these teachers could not truly be enjoyed by him, for it would forever be steeped in the bitter memory and precious blood of these men -- a memory impossible to forget.

Once we make a flawed assessment of a situation, our intellect becomes veiled by emotion, and we too begin to behave in life just as Arjuna does. A clear indication of this is found in the detailed description Vyasa gives of this very episode.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Having taken up the notion that even in a righteous war the slaying of teachers and the like yields only mere livelihood as its fruit, and having attributed sin to it, he speaks, in 'Gurun...': not having slain the teachers Bhishma, Drona and the rest, and the brothers and others present here, who are 'mahanubhava' -- of great glory, endowed with learning and study -- it would be more truly praiseworthy, more fitting, to eat, to partake of, alms -- a collection of begged food, the eating of alms, though forbidden for kings and the like -- here, in this world, in the field of ordinary conduct. For the enjoyment of a kingdom is never something to be sought through the slaying of one's teachers. Moreover, having slain the teachers, I would only be enjoying objects of desire and wealth -- I would not be experiencing liberation; the enjoyment is here alone, not in heaven. He qualifies 'objects of desire and wealth' with the word 'bhogan' (enjoyments): enjoyments, so called because they are enjoyed, stained with blood, smeared with gore, utterly contemptible. Hence, abandoning such enjoyments obtainable only through the slaying of one's teachers, eating alms alone is proper -- this is the sense.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Rather than slaying these noble teachers, I consider it better to eat even the food of alms in this world. For, having slain my teachers, what I would enjoy here would be nothing but pleasures stained with their blood and driven chiefly by the desire for wealth!

Explanation: From this verse it appears that the words spoken by the Lord in verses 2-3 are now beginning to have their effect within Arjuna. This is producing in Arjuna's mind the thought: 'If slaying elders like Bhishma and Drona were truly against dharma, would the Lord, knowing this, still be commanding me without hesitation to fight? Somewhere, my own understanding itself must be at fault!' Hence Arjuna no longer speaks with the same agitation as in the previous verse, but rather with some hesitancy.

'Gurun ahatva ... bhaikshyam api iha loke' -- Arjuna first sets forth his own position: if I do not fight against these worshipful elders, Bhishma, Drona and the rest, Duryodhana alone will not fight against me either. Thus, with no battle occurring, I shall not obtain the kingdom, and shall have to suffer as a result. My livelihood too will become difficult. I may even be forced to resort to the practice of begging alms, forbidden though it is for a Kshatriya, merely to sustain my life. Yet, compared to slaying my teachers, I consider even taking up that painful begging to be preferable.

'Iha loke' -- the meaning is: though begging for food will bring me dishonor and scorn in this world, and people will censure me, still, compared to slaying my teachers, begging is preferable.

'Api' conveys: for me, slaying my teachers is forbidden, and begging too is forbidden; but of these two, slaying my teachers appears to me the more forbidden.

'Hatva arthakamams tu ... rudhirapradigdhan' -- Arjuna now turns his attention to the Lord's own words and says: if I fight as You command, then, as the result of slaying my teachers in battle, I would enjoy only those pleasures which are stained with their blood and in which the desire for wealth is paramount. I would gain nothing but pleasures. Would obtaining such pleasures bring liberation? Would it bring peace? Certainly not!

Transition: the Lord's words carry such a distinctive power that they are gradually making their impression felt within Arjuna, so that Arjuna is growing increasingly doubtful of his own resolve not to fight. Having reached such a state, Arjuna now says--

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Arjuna said: Again Partha, agitated by affection, compassion, and fear of both righteousness and unrighteousness, not knowing what the Lord had spoken for his own highest good, said this: How shall I slay Bhishma, Drona and the rest, my greatly esteemed teachers? How much more so should I, having slain them, then enjoy those very pleasures which they themselves once enjoyed, having sprinkled those pleasures with their blood, and having seated myself upon their very seats, partake of them!

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
Continuing His rebuke through terms such as 'impotence' and the rest, and showing that this is 'a false conviction of righteousness in what is actually unrighteous,' and so on, He introduces Arjuna's reply, in 'Katham...': through 'How shall I, in battle, fight against Bhishma and Drona' and the rest, and through 'should I then enjoy those pleasures,' Arjuna, in his opening argument (purvapaksha), signals as objectionable both a fixation on the particular nature of the act and a fixation on the particular nature of its fruit. Through 'we do not know this' he likewise states a fixation on the particular nature of the act. Action undertaken without any such fixation is simply not possible. Nor does one engage in battle intending defeat. Yet even victory here would be nothing but calamity for us. Hence he says: without slaying our elders, it would be better even to live by begging alms. And this itself cannot be resolved -- whether we should desire victory or desire defeat -- since even in victory there is the destruction of our own kinsmen.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Hence he says that the slaying of teachers and the like is opposed to both worldly custom and Vedic injunction, in 'Gurun...': not slaying the teachers, the greatly glorious ones, and eating alms, food obtained by begging, like a renunciant, is better in this world. That I should enjoy those pleasures stained with their blood -- this is spoken in a tone of scornful rhetorical questioning. The sense is: this is simply not proper.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
One might object: the worthiness of worship of Bhishma and Drona rests solely on their status as teachers -- as would likewise apply to Kripa and others; and it is not now proper to accept their status as teachers, since the smriti states: 'Even of one's own teacher, if he is arrogant and does not know what should and should not be done, if he has taken to a wrong path, his abandonment is enjoined.' Hence, since these men, arrogant with the pride of battle, having wrongfully seized a kingdom and betrayed their own disciples, are utterly devoid of discrimination between right and wrong action and are established on a wrong path, their very slaying would be the true good -- anticipating this objection, he says: 'Not having slain the teachers' -- the next world surely exists regardless; but in this very world, for those kings and others whose kingdom has been seized by them, even to eat forbidden alms is more truly praiseworthy, more fitting, than to gain even a kingdom through their slaying. Thus, taking up the notion that even in a righteous war there is only mere livelihood as fruit, and attributing sin to it, he speaks.

But lest it be thought that their arrogance and the rest negate their status as teachers altogether, he anticipates this and says 'mahanubhavan' (of great glory): 'mahan anubhavah' -- those whose power rests upon the foundation of learning, study, austerity, and right conduct. Thus even time and desire itself have been subdued by them; being possessed of such abundant merit, they are not tainted by petty sins such as arrogance. Or, as a single word, 'himahan' -- one who destroys cold, i.e. the sun or fire -- 'mahanubhavan' would mean those whose power is like that of the sun or fire. Thus, due to their exceeding brilliance, they are not touched by faults such as arrogance -- for it is said: 'Transgression of dharma is seen even among the mighty, and boldness too; the mighty are not tainted by fault, just as fire, which consumes all, is not.'

One might further object: since they entered battle out of greed for wealth, having thus sold themselves, whence comes their aforementioned greatness? For Bhishma himself said to Yudhishthira: 'Man is the slave of wealth, but wealth is the slave of none; this is true, O great king -- I am bound by wealth to the Kauravas.' Anticipating this, he says: even if greedy for wealth, they remain, relative to me, my teachers still -- hence the repeated use of the word 'gurun' (teachers). The word 'tu' too carries the sense of 'even.' Having slain even such teachers, I would enjoy only pleasures -- I would not attain liberation. 'Bhogah' (enjoyments) means objects of enjoyment. And those enjoyments would be here alone, not in the next world. And even here they would be, as it were, stained with blood -- that is, utterly contemptible, pervaded by infamy. The sense is: if it is thus even in this world, how much more should the suffering of the next world be described! Or alternatively: having slain the teachers, I would enjoy only enjoyments consisting of wealth and desire -- not dharma or liberation; this is another way of explaining the word 'arthakaman' as a qualifier of 'enjoyments.'

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
One might object: then you would have no means even for the sustenance of your own body -- anticipating this, he says, in 'Gurun...': not slaying the teachers, Drona and the rest -- since the slaying of teachers is opposed to the next world, not committing that slaying -- to eat even the food of alms in this world is better, more fitting. In the opposite case, not only would there be suffering in the next world, but I would experience the suffering of hell in this very life -- he says this, in 'Hatva...': having slain the teachers, here alone I would enjoy, would eat, enjoyments consisting of wealth and desire, thoroughly stained, smeared, with blood. Or alternatively, 'arthakaman' (desirous of wealth) qualifies the teachers themselves: being agitated by the thirst for wealth, they would not desist from battle; hence their slaying would certainly follow. And thus it was said by Bhishma to Yudhishthira: 'Man is the slave of wealth, but wealth is the slave of none; this is true, O great king -- I am bound by wealth to the Kauravas.'

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
One might object: if so, then, lacking enjoyment through failure to gain the kingdom, begging would become necessary -- anticipating this objection, he averts it by embracing it, in 'Gurun...': not slaying, not doing violence to, the teachers Bhishma, Drona and the rest, the greatly glorious ones, eating even alms -- food obtained by begging, forbidden though it is for a Kshatriya -- in this world, is better, more praiseworthy. For the eating of alms, undertaken for the sake of avoiding violence to one's teachers, does not produce demerit. Having stated the virtue of avoiding hell through not slaying teachers -- qualified by 'mahanubhavan,' meaning those whose power is exceedingly renowned -- and thereby the absence of infamy, he now states the fault in slaying them, in 'Hatva...': the word 'mahanubhavan' applies here as well. Having slain the greatly glorious teachers, I would enjoy enjoyments consisting of wealth and desire here alone, not in the next world -- and here too, stained with blood, that is, utterly contemptible, pervaded by infamy, is the sense. 'Arthakaman' qualifies the teachers: being thus agitated by the thirst for wealth, they would not desist from battle; hence their slaying would certainly follow. And thus it was said by Bhishma: 'Man is the slave of wealth, but wealth is the slave of none; this is true, O great king -- I am bound by wealth to the Kauravas' -- so say others. But some, anticipating the objection that, per the smriti on abandoning an arrogant teacher who has taken a wrong path, since these men, arrogant with the pride of battle, having wrongfully seized a kingdom and betrayed their disciples, are devoid of discrimination and established on a wrong path, their slaying would be the true good, say: 'mahan' means power founded upon learning and study; hence, being possessed of such abundant merit, they are not tainted by petty sins. Or, as a single word, 'himahan' (destroyer of cold, i.e. sun or fire) -- 'mahanubhavan' meaning those of solar or fiery power; thus, due to exceeding brilliance, they are not touched by faults -- so it is described, since it is said the mighty are not tainted by fault just as fire is not by what it consumes. Whether, according to the smriti cited by the objector, the abandonment enjoined for one tainted with arrogance and the like, without slaying, truly negates their claim to being praiseworthy in this way, or not, is to be considered by the learned. Further, as to the objection that, having sold themselves in entering battle from greed for wealth, whence their aforementioned greatness -- as Bhishma said to Yudhishthira, 'Man is the slave of wealth' and so on -- this too, the latter half beginning 'Hatva,' is drawn by some as the answer; whether, even there, the faults of arrogance and the like rooted in this are adequately addressed by the description of their exceedingly renowned greatness and brilliance, and whether such an objection could arise again, is likewise to be considered.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Now, since the beginning of battle enjoined by the Lord is not recognized as truly beneficial -- being the cause, through its proper sequence, of the highest good -- Arjuna's speech, which is a rejection of this, arises; and such ignorance is rooted in confusion caused by misplaced affection and the like. Explaining this, he introduces the reply, in 'Punarapi...': since the subject matter is the same as before, it is said 'again he spoke thus.' In the verse beginning 'Katham...', the word 'cha' (and) indicates an unstated further inclusion, or, through the word 'adi' (and so on), indicates that what is taken up is merely representative of what is not explicitly stated. The word 'gurun' (teachers, elders) used in the following verse is drawn back and applied here, since it conveys the sense of 'worthy of high regard' intended by the word 'pujarhau' (worthy of worship). 'Bahumantavyan' (greatly esteemed) means 'persons of great distinction,' either drawn from the following verse's 'mahanubhavan,' or else they are inherently greatly esteemed in themselves -- by virtue of being a grandfather, a teacher of the science of the bow, and the like, they are exceedingly esteemed, is the sense. Ceasing even the worship of those worthy of worship with flowers and the like would itself be a bold act; to slay them would be an act of extreme boldness. One ought to fight, out of devotion to one's teachers, against those who oppose them -- not against the teachers themselves. This is the sense behind 'how shall I fight against my teachers with arrows?' By the word 'aham' (I), his renowned lineage and the like are implied. That 'I shall fight against them with arrows' carries the sense of fighting through to the point of slaying is clarified by the following verse, where it is stated 'I shall slay.' By the two words 'Madhusudana' and 'Arisudana' it is suggested: are You not also the slayer of Sandipani and others [your own teachers]? The infinitive 'chartum' (to wander, to beg) here carries only the general sense of the action, not a sense tied to any specific verb requiring an object. Although some means of livelihood must indeed be resorted to, still, compared to the pleasures obtained through the slaying of one's teachers, even resorting to begging alms -- a duty proper to another class -- would in this world be preferable, more truly praiseworthy. This is because the suffering in the next world resulting from the slaying of teachers of such great influence is exceedingly great, is the sense. To dispel any illusion of a meaning contrary to the context, and owing to the connection with the word 'katham' (how) used in the previous verse, as well as the extreme cruelty involved, and the contrast indicated by the word 'tu' (but), the word 'kathantaram' (how much more so) is used. This is an optative form used in the sense of censure, per the grammatical rule governing such usage. Here, to prevent confusion arising from a compound construed as a dvandva and the like, the phrase 'bhogeshv atimatra-prasaktan' is explained through both components of the compound taken separately. The analysis is: 'those whose desire is fixed upon objects of enjoyment' -- following the rule that an exocentric bahuvrihi compound ending in a word denoting birth and the like is not to be avoided. Alternatively: 'artha-kamah' means 'those who desire wealth'; were they free from such desire, then the seizing of their enjoyments too might be tolerable -- but this is rather like snatching food from the hungry, is the sense. To convey an even greater cruelty than mere slaying, the meaning is established through words such as 'bhoga' (enjoyment) and 'rudhira' (blood). The word 'tu' (but) indicates the distinctive point conveyed through 'taih' (by them) and the rest. The word 'ihaiva' (in this very place) shows the intended extremity of cruelty, as indicated in 'teshu' (among them) and the rest. The enjoyments obtained through the slaying of one's teachers become, as it were, difficult to partake of, on their own account, since they perpetually recall the blood-stained memory of the teacher -- hence even worldly happiness is absent; this is the intent behind the word 'blood-stained.' For sprinkling itself, when consumed by oneself, becomes the cause of another's eating as well -- here both of these are reversed, is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
It is better to go about begging alms than to slay the teachers; and no enjoyment of a kingdom could come through their slaying -- he says this, in 'Gurun...': not slaying the teachers, Bhishma, Drona and the rest, even to eat, to partake of, the food of alms, in this world, is preferable, is truly good -- since they are 'mahanubhava,' bringers of the presence of the great Lord Himself. That happiness in the next world results from enjoyment in this world is conveyed by the very phrase 'in this world.' That the slaying of these would result in suffering not merely in the next world but suffering equal to hell in this very world too, he states, in 'Hatva...': having slain the teachers, who are wealth-desiring, who are focused on wealth, I would enjoy here alone enjoyments stained with blood, smeared with gore.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
One might object: the slaying even of teachers, if they have taken up arms for battle, would be the true good -- anticipating this, he says, in 'Gurun...': though what you say is indeed commendable, still, not slaying the greatly glorious teachers, to eat alms alone, is better, is even more truly praiseworthy. If this be so, then, abandoning the teachers, one should simply slay the truly wicked, Duryodhana and the rest -- anticipating this, he says, in 'Arthakaman...': the teachers, being desirous of wealth, will certainly render assistance to Duryodhana; hence their slaying too would necessarily follow, is the sense. The word 'tu' serves to introduce this alternative position. 'Here alone,' not in the next world. 'Bhunjiya' (I would enjoy) is an optative used in a rhetorical question. Whether, in this rhetorical question -- not slaying the teachers, is alms preferable, or, having slain them, is the attainment of enjoyment preferable -- he himself states the fault in the latter alternative, in 'Rudhirapradigdhan' (stained with blood).

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
It would be better to live in this world by begging than to live at the cost of the lives of great souls who are my teachers. Even though desiring worldly gain, they are superiors. If they are killed, everything we enjoy will be tainted with blood.

 According to scriptural codes, a teacher who engages in an abominable action and has lost his sense of discrimination is fit to be abandoned. Bhishma and Drona were obliged to take the side of Duryodhana because of his financial assistance, although they should not have accepted such a position simply on financial considerations. Under the circumstances, they have lost the respectability of teachers. But Arjuna thinks that nevertheless they remain his superiors, and therefore to enjoy material profits after killing them would mean to enjoy spoils tainted with blood.
