Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
It is quite clear that whatever cannot be destroyed by nature's destructive forces or by man-made instruments and weapons must indeed be eternal.

In the second line of this verse, several qualifiers of the Self are given -- these are not employed at random or without purpose, out of haste. Each word is chosen and used as part of a chain of reasoning. That indestructible principle described in the first line must be eternal. Whatever is eternal must certainly also be all-pervading (sarvagata).

This small word 'sarvagata' carries a vast and profound meaning. Nothing whatsoever can remain that is not pervaded by the all-pervading principle. If the eternal Self is all-pervading, then it can have no particular form or shape either, since shape belongs only to a limited object, outside whose boundary some other, different object exists. Limbs like hands and feet have shape because space exists around and beyond them. Hence the unlimited, all-pervading Self has no shape.

Thus it naturally follows that an eternal, all-pervading substance must also be stable and immovable. Motion is impossible for it. Movement belongs only to that which resides at some particular place and time, and can therefore be relocated. Since the Self is absent from no place or time, the question of its moving simply does not arise. I cannot move about within myself.

Here the joint use of both 'stable' and 'immovable' might seem redundant, since they appear somewhat synonymous. But 'stable' refers to fixedness at the root, as a tree is fixed in one place even while growing upward. Here, even while remaining unmoving, upward motion too is denied. The infinite Self is stable and immovable, that is, it has no kind of motion whatsoever.

That which is ancient, primeval, is called eternal (sanatana). This word 'sanatana' has two meanings -- one literal, the other implied. Its simple literal meaning is that the Self is not something newly made; it is ancient. According to its implied meaning, the sense is that the Self is not bounded or limited by time and place. Whoever, in whatever place, at whatever time, attains perfection through Self-realization -- that realization will be one and the same, not different.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
A point well known and unopposed in common usage should be accepted here too. Just as, though Indra's fault of adultery with Ahalya was itself a grave transgression, no great taint attached to him -- this scripture speaks only of his greatness, since another scriptural text also says, 'not even much of his hair diminishes' (Kaushitaki Upanishad 3.1). Likewise it is said, 'he who thus knows Me, the Purushottama, without delusion' (Gita 15.19). The Naradiya Purana too speaks of the infinite divisibility even of a fraction of Vishnu's glory, and states that Shri, Shesha, Brahma, and Shankara do not equal even one such fraction in glory. Similarly, the supremacy of the Mahabharata among all scriptures is established in other texts as well -- 'who else but the lotus-eyed one (Vishnu) could be the author of the Mahabharata' (Vishnu Purana 3.4.5). By this true statement -- 'there has been none equal to Narayana, nor will there be' -- I establish everything; he from whose grace Brahma arose and from whose wrath Rudra arose, none is equal to him -- it is on such general occasions that Vishnu's supreme greatness is spoken of.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
He states the reason for the absence of fitness for the actions of cutting and the like produced by the elements beginning with earth, in 'yatah...' He connects the first half as the reason for the second half, in 'yasmat...' He indicates the mutual relation of reason and consequence among eternality and the rest, in 'nityatvat...' One should not object that, since atoms too are eternal yet not all-pervading, this would be an exception undermining the inference regarding the Self, since atoms themselves, being unproven, do not constitute a genuine exception. Nor is it proper that the Self, though all-pervading, should possess the capacity for modification, since even space, though held to be all-pervading, is not found to possess such capacity -- this is said, in 'sthiratvat...' Since, even granting the Self is eternal of itself, destruction from some cause might still be conceivable, and hence its being 'ancient' (chirantana) might seem unestablished -- to this doubt he answers, in 'na karanat...'

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
This embodied one cannot be cut, cannot be burned, cannot be wetted, and cannot be dried either. Because it remains eternally, is fully present in all things, is of unmoving, stable nature, and is beginningless.

Explanation -- [In this verse it is stated why weapons and the like do not produce any modification in this embodied one.]

'Achchhedyo'yam' -- weapons cannot cut this embodied one. This does not mean that there is no weapon available, or that the wielder of the weapon is unskilled; rather, the very act of cutting cannot enter into the embodied one at all -- it is simply not fit to be cut.

Apart from weapons, this embodied one cannot be cut even by mantra, curse, and the like. As is heard, Shakalya's head fell off, cut by the curse of Yajnavalkya, for failing to answer his question (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad). Thus the body can indeed be cut by mantras, by mere words, but the embodied one is utterly uncuttable in every way.

'Adahyo'yam' -- this embodied one is unburnable, since it has no capacity whatsoever to burn. Besides fire, this embodied one cannot be burned even by mantra or curse. As it is heard that a hunter, cursed by Damayanti, burned to ashes without any fire. Thus fire, whether through curses or mantras, can burn only that which is fit to burn; the act of burning cannot enter into this embodied one at all.

'Akledyah' -- this embodied one cannot be made wet, that is, it has no capacity whatsoever to become wet. It cannot be moistened by water, nor by mantra, curse, medicine, or the like. It is heard that singing the Malkauns raga can even make stone moist, and that a moonstone becomes moist on seeing the moon; but this embodied one is not a thing that can be made moist by any raga or the like.

'Ashoshyah' -- this embodied one is undryable. It is not a thing that can be dried up by wind, since the very act of drying cannot enter into it at all. Neither wind, nor mantra, curse, medicine, or the like, can dry up this embodied one. Just as sage Agastya once dried up the ocean, yet no one, by any power whatsoever, can dry up this embodied one.

'Eva cha' -- since Arjuna was grieving over the possibility of destruction, the Lord, having called the embodied one uncuttable, unburnable, unwettable, and undryable, now, with the words 'eva cha,' emphasizes with special force that this embodied one is simply and utterly so -- no action of any kind whatsoever can enter into it. Hence this embodied one is not fit to be grieved for at all.

'Nityah' -- this embodied one endures eternally and continuously. It is not that it did not exist at some time and will not exist at some other time; rather, it remains eternally and continuously, exactly as it is, at all times.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Weapons, fire, water and air are incapable of cleaving, burning, wetting and drying the self; for the nature of the self is to pervade all elements; it is present everywhere, for it is subtler than all the elements, and hence not capable of being pervaded by them; and cleaving, burning, wetting and drying are actions that can occur only by pervading a substance. Therefore the self is eternal. It is stable, immovable and primeval. The meaning is that it is unchanging, unshakable and ancient.

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
'Na enam...' Nothing whatsoever -- weapons and the like -- can be the cause of its destruction. Since the Self is of the nature of pure consciousness alone, without support, without parts, and independent, it cannot possibly be destroyed by any sequence involving the acquisition of a different nature, the destruction of a substrate, or the division of parts. Nor is its passing into another body anything new -- even while connected with a body, it continually passes into another body and becomes connected with it, is the sense, since the body itself does not remain stable even for a single moment. Knowing this to be so, it is not proper for you to grieve for this Self.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Since this is so, therefore this Self cannot be cut, cannot be burned, cannot be moistened, and cannot be dried. Since the other elements, which are the causes of destruction of one another, are not capable of destroying this Self, therefore it is eternal. Being eternal, it is all-pervading. Being all-pervading, it is stationary, that is, fixed like a stump. Being fixed, this Self is unmoving. Therefore it is changeless, that is, it is not produced from any cause, as a new thing.

It should not be argued that these verses are repetitive, since the eternality and changelessness of the Self have already been stated in a single verse, 'never is this One born, and never does it die,' etc. (2.20). Whatever has been said there about the Self does not go beyond the meaning of this verse; something is repeated in the very same words, something in idea alone. But since the object, the Self, is so hard to grasp, Lord Vasudeva raises the topic again and again, and explains that very object in other words, so that, somehow, the unmanifest Self may come within the comprehension of the intellect of these transmigrating persons, and bring about the cessation of their cycle of birth and death.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
One might object that 'achchhedyo'yam...' too is merely a repetition of 'nainam chindanti...' To this he answers: in the previous verse, by the use of the present tense, only present cutting and the like were denied, leaving open the doubt that destruction by weapons might yet occur in the future. To dispel this very doubt, this verse states that the Self has no fitness whatsoever for cutting and the rest -- that is, by the sense of fitness (arha) conveyed by the krtya-suffix, it is uncuttable and so forth for all time. Thus the two verses differ: the first conveyed unfitness through denial in the present tense, while this one directly states the very absence of fitness. Hence there is no repetition.

The words 'nityah sarvagatah' and the rest, which might seem inapplicable to the individual soul, are explained by dispelling this very doubt: does the non-observation of present cutting and the like establish the Self's unfitness for cutting definitively, or merely as a possibility? It is definitive, since, were the Self fit to be cut, its cutting would certainly have been observed in a context such as war; its non-observation establishes its unfitness. Likewise, by the qualifiers eternality, all-pervasiveness, stationariness, immovability, and antiquity, the four kinds of causal action -- origination, attainment, modification, and refinement -- are shown, in turn, to be impossible in the Self. One should not object that atoms too, though eternal, are not all-pervading, and hence this would be an exception, since atomism itself is unproven. Thus the Self is established as free of every kind of modification, eternal, all-pervading, stable, and beginningless.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
With the half-verse 'achchhedyah...' he draws the conclusion. To exclude the property of being an effect, 'nityah' (eternal) is said. 'Sarvagatah' means all-pervading. From the standpoint of oneness with Brahman, the individual soul too is all-pervading -- not in its own essential form, but through its quality of consciousness, since it is a mode (dharma-bhuta) of the Lord, as will be stated later. 'Sthanuh' means stability. 'Achalah' means inactivity. If it be said that space too is similarly well known to be such, and hence space itself is the Self -- this is not so, he says, with 'sanatanah' (beginningless): for the origination of space is heard of in the scripture, 'from this very Self, space arose' (Taittiriya Upanishad 2.1); hence space is not the Self.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
On the absence of the capacity of weapons and the like to destroy it, he states the reason for the Self's unfitness for destruction thereby: since it is uncuttable, weapons do not cut it; since it is unburnable, fire does not burn it; since it is unwettable, water does not moisten it; since it is undryable, wind does not dry it -- the construction should follow this order. The word 'eva' (indeed), connected with each in turn, serves to fix each attribute definitively. 'Cha' (and) is used in the sense of aggregation or reason. The reason for unfitness for cutting and the rest is stated in the second half: it is eternal, and, being free of beginning and end, is not producible. If it were not all-pervading, it would be non-eternal -- by the maxim 'the extent of division corresponds to the extent of modification' -- as other schools hold regarding atoms and the like, which is not accepted here. But it is all-pervading, omnipresent, and hence eternal indeed. By this, its being an object to be attained is also refuted. If it were subject to modification, it would not be all-pervading; but it is stationary, unmodified, and hence all-pervading indeed. By this, its being subject to modification is also refuted. If it were mobile, capable of action, it would be subject to modification, like a pot; but it is immovable, hence not subject to modification. By this, its being subject to purificatory refinement is also refuted. Modification consists in abandoning a prior state to attain another; even where the state remains one, mere motion alone constitutes a distinct kind of change. For this very reason, it is ancient, ever uniform.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
He states the reason for this, in 'achchhedyah...,' spanning two and a half words. Being without parts, it is uncuttable and unwettable. Being formless, it is unburnable; being devoid of fluidity, it is undryable -- this is the sense. Hence it is not fit for cutting and the rest. For this very reason it is eternal, indestructible, all-pervading, stationary (of unchanging nature, free of any change of form), immovable (never abandoning its prior form), and ancient (beginningless).

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
He states the resulting conclusion: 'achchhedyah...' The word 'eva' (indeed) applies throughout. Since the elements, though causes of destruction of one another, are not capable of destroying this Self, it is eternal -- no overextension results here, since there is no proof of eternality for atoms in the way opponents claim. For this very reason it is all-pervading; hence, like all-pervading space, it is 'sthanu,' that is, stationary. For this very reason it is immovable; for this very reason this Self is ancient, primeval, not produced from any cause whatsoever.

By these four qualifiers -- eternal and the rest -- the four possible kinds of causal outcome (being producible, being attainable, being subject to modification, and being subject to refinement) are refuted in turn, and the relation of reason and consequence is also established by the commentarial statement 'not produced from any cause whatsoever' -- hence there is no contradiction here.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Avoiding the redundancy with what was said before, by a difference of purpose -- namely, firmness and ease of comprehension -- he brings down these two verses beginning 'na enam...' In this verse and a half, the incapacity of the instrument and the unfitness of the object, though stated separately for the sake of clarity, are, being mutually inclusive, not taken up separately elsewhere in the commentary. In 'achchhedyah' and the rest, the suffix carries the sense of fitness, showing that in 'nainam chindanti' and the rest, it is precisely this fitness on the part of weapons and the like that is denied. The word 'sarvagata' (all-pervading) is explained as a restatement of the reason already given. On the doubt as to how the atomic Self can be all-pervading, he answers that the word 'sarvagata' here does not mean the all-pervasiveness of the individual soul, which would contradict the many scriptural texts, but rather its special capacity to enter into all things.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
These -- weapons and the like -- though possessed of the property of cutting and the rest, do not act so without My will, and act so only by My will in the case of a body that must be given up; hence you too should act, knowing this Self to be possessed of the properties of uncuttability and the rest -- having said this, he states these properties: 'achchhedyah...' It is possessed of the properties of uncuttability and the rest -- this is the sense. He states the reason for its possessing the properties of uncuttability and the rest: eternal, indestructible; all-pervading, ubiquitous; stationary, of unchanging nature; immovable, ever uniform; ancient, beginningless.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
To the doubt as to why weapons and the like do not produce cutting and the rest in it, he answers that it is simply not fit for cutting and the rest at all -- this is the sense, in 'achchhedyo'yam...' Here, for uncuttability and the rest, the same reasons already stated -- non-grossness and the like -- should be understood. Having thus stated, through uncuttability and the rest, the negative qualities free of grossness, he now states the positive qualities too, in 'nitya...' Since all these qualifiers are meant to point to a single, undivided, uniform reality, eternality and the rest refute producibility and the like. Since it is eternal, it is not producible, like a pot. Since it is all-pervading, it is not attainable, like a village. Since it is stationary, that is, of unchanging nature, never abandoning its prior form, it is not subject to modification, like milk and the like. 'Immovable' -- just as a mirror, though of itself never departing from its own clarity, may seem to depart from that clarity owing to a covering of dirt, so too something stationary might, through contact with something else, come to acquire restlessness. But this is immovable, and hence not so. Thus it is stated that four kinds of causal outcome -- origination, attainment, transformation, and refinement -- are not possible in it. The reason for this is 'sanatana' (ancient) -- the indeclinable 'sana' means continuity, indicating freedom from limitation by place, time, or substance.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
This individual soul is unbreakable and insoluble, and can be neither burned nor dried. He is everlasting, present everywhere, unchangeable, immovable and eternally the same.

 All these qualifications of the atomic soul definitely prove that the individual soul is eternally the atomic particle of the spirit whole, and he remains the same atom eternally, without change. The theory of monism is very difficult to apply in this case, because the individual soul is never expected to become one homogeneously. After liberation from material contamination, the atomic soul may prefer to remain as a spiritual spark in the effulgent rays of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, but the intelligent souls enter into the spiritual planets to associate with the Personality of Godhead. The word sarva-gata (“all-pervading”) is significant because there is no doubt that living entities are all over God’s creation. They live on the land, in the water, in the air, within the earth and even within fire. The belief that they are sterilized in fire is not acceptable, because it is clearly stated here that the soul cannot be burned by fire. Therefore, there is no doubt that there are living entities also in the sun planet with suitable bodies to live there. If the sun globe is uninhabited, then the word sarva-gata – “living everywhere” – becomes meaningless.
