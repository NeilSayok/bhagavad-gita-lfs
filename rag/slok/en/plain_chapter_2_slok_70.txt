Life Application:
Let impressions and desires pass through your awareness like rivers entering the ocean without disturbing your inner calm.

Commentary by Swami Tejomayananda:
Just as the waters of many rivers enter the ocean -- which is full on every side yet remains firmly unmoved -- without disturbing it in the least, so too the objects of desire enter the person in whom they arise, without producing any disturbance in him. Such a person attains peace, not the one who desires the objects of enjoyment.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
It is a well-known fact that although crores of gallons of water pour in from every direction through countless rivers, the ocean's shoreline is never breached. In the same way, countless sense-impressions constantly reach the mind of the man of wisdom through his sense organs, yet they can never produce the slightest disturbance or agitation in his inner being. One who remains amid sense-objects, performing all worldly activity through the senses, and yet never swerves from his own true nature -- he alone is the man of knowledge, the sage. The Lord states plainly that only such a person attains real peace and bliss, and, as if unsatisfied with saying this much, He adds further that one who desires objects of enjoyment never attains peace. This teaching stands in complete contrast to the modern materialist outlook, which holds that having more desires drives material progress and that satisfying an ever-greater number of desires can make a person happy. Industrialized, mass-production-based societies are built on continually multiplying human desires; the result is that the ordinary person today has millions of times more desires than his forebears had a century ago. Big businesses and industries use the achievements of modern science to keep generating new desires and to fulfil them, and whenever a person's desires get fulfilled to some degree, he is told he is now happier than before. The great thinkers of ancient India, by contrast, discovered through self-experience, careful observation and study that happiness gained from the fulfilment of desires can never be complete. This can be expressed as: the measure of happiness equals the number of desires fulfilled divided by the number of desires held in the mind. Even modern secular materialists accept this truth in principle, but their practical approach differs greatly from that of the sages -- today, everywhere, people try to attain happiness simply by fulfilling more and more desires. The ancient sages too lived within human society and, through their philosophy, aimed to make society happier; they recognised that without reducing the sheer number of one's desires, merely fulfilling an ever-growing quantity of them brings neither true joy nor any real increase in happiness. But today we follow a path quite different from that of the sages, and this is why real joy is nowhere to be seen in society. This very Upanishadic principle finds full expression here in the Gita, and has been praised unreservedly by India's poets and sages down the centuries. A person who desires many objects of enjoyment never attains peace. It is only our flawed evaluation of the outer world that gives sense-objects the power to make us miserable; in themselves they can cause us no harm at all. The wise person, established in his own blissful nature, remains entirely unmoved by all such objects. Having begun the description of the marks of the man of steady wisdom with his self-contentment and freedom from desire, the Lord now elaborates further on that very theme in this verse.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
This describes the manner in which sense-objects are experienced. One who, though filled with objects of desire, remains unmoved -- neither swelling with elation over their presence, nor exerting effort to obtain them, nor withering away in their absence -- is like the ocean, which neither swells excessively nor dries up on account of rivers entering or not entering it, and which makes no effort either way. Such a person attains liberation.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
An objection is raised: since even one who has not formally renounced but possesses knowledge could obtain the fruit of that knowledge, namely liberation, why is renunciation prescribed as necessary for the wise? In reply it is shown that liberation belongs properly to the true renunciate of knowledge -- one endowed with discrimination and dispassion, who has risen above all worldly cravings and attained direct realization through hearing and reflection -- and not to one still overcome by thirst for sense-objects; and this is illustrated by the analogy of the ocean, reaffirming what was already stated in the verse on being freed from attachment and aversion. Though rivers pour their waters into the ocean from every side, one might wonder whether its state thereby increases or decreases; but it is explained that the ocean's essential watery form never truly grows or diminishes, so its condition remains ever the same, described as 'unmoved.' Again, one might ask whether, if particular waters not proper to it enter the ocean, this would make it subject to change and hence without a fixed footing; this is answered by saying it remains 'grounded in its own self.' Likewise, one might suppose that particular desires for objects, though absent from the unchanging wise man when the objects are absent, might produce disturbance in him when the objects come near; this is clarified by explaining how they 'enter' him -- namely from every side, yet without effect. And it is said, in line with the scriptural teaching that only the desireless one who has turned away from sense-objects attains liberation and never the one driven by craving for them, that 'he attains peace.'

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
During the rainy season the waters of rivers and streams swell greatly, and many rivers flood; yet when that water, coming from every side, pours into the ocean, the ocean does not rise -- it remains exactly within its own limit. And in the hot season, when the rivers run low, the ocean does not fall either. Whether more river water comes in, or less, or none at all, and whatever water is drawn off by the submarine fire or evaporated by the sun, the ocean is unaffected either way -- it neither rises nor falls. It has no real dependence on the rivers; it remains eternally full exactly as it is, and never abandons its own boundary. In just the same way, all the objects of enjoyment in this world come to, and present themselves before, the self-controlled man who has realized the Supreme Reality, yet they can produce no disturbance of pleasure or pain in what is called his body and mind. Hence he attains supreme peace -- a peace that comes from the Truth of the Self, not from objects of enjoyment (cf. Gita 2.46). The simile of the ocean and the rivers given here does not, however, apply perfectly to the man of steady wisdom, because ocean-water and river-water are of the same kind -- the water filling the ocean is of the same nature as the water flowing in from the rivers. But the difference between the man of steady wisdom and worldly objects of enjoyment is so vast that no simile, not even that of day and night or heaven and the netherworld, could fully capture it: for the reality in which the man of steady wisdom is established is conscious, eternal, real, unlimited, infinite -- while worldly objects are inert, transient, unreal, limited and finite. There is a further difference: river water actually reaches into the ocean itself, but these worldly objects never actually reach the reality in which the man of steady wisdom abides; they reach only as far as what is called his body and mind. The ocean simile, therefore, is given only to describe the condition of his so-called body and mind, not his true nature, for which no simile exists at all. Those whose minds are fixed on desire for objects, who give importance only to objects, whose gaze is turned only toward objects -- however many worldly enjoyments they may obtain, they can never be satisfied; their craving, their inner burning and torment cannot be extinguished. How, then, could they ever attain peace? For the conscious Self can never be satisfied through inert, material objects.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
The river waters enter into the sea which is full by itself and is thus the same, i.e., unchanging in shape. The sea exhibits no special increase or decrease, whether the waters or rivers enter it or not. Even so do all objects of desire, i.e., objects of sense perception like sound etc., enter into a self-controlled one, i.e., they produce only sensorial impressions but no reaction from him. Such a person will attain peace. The meaning is that he alone attains to peace, who by reason of the contentment coming from the vision of the self, feels no disturbance when objects of sense like sound, etc., come within the ken of the senses or when they do not come. This is not the case with one who runs after desires. Whoever is agitated by sound and other objects, never attains to peace.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
This verse gives the answer to the whole question that was earlier asked, beginning with the word 'apuryamanam.' It should be understood that no deliberate effort is involved, since the wise man is not dependent on such effort. One might ask: even someone inwardly disengaged from the world could still, in some sense, move about outwardly like anyone else -- so how could actual sense-experience, which is plainly observed to occur, arise in him without the ordinary mental engagement that experience normally requires? This is explained precisely to remove that doubt. The brief statement 'thus all desires enter him' is now elaborated: here 'desire' means the objects of desire themselves, as confirmed by the later verse (2.71), which speaks of 'abandoning desires' -- a usage that would be inconsistent otherwise. The explanation of 'being unmoved' is that the wise man is neither elated by the presence of objects nor otherwise disturbed by them; this sense is drawn directly from the very use of the ocean simile. The phrase 'he attains peace' is offered in praise of the man of knowledge; and though some have taken this to mean that liberation belongs to the knower alone, that claim is not correct here, since the point has already been fully established elsewhere.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Furthermore: just as the ocean does not go out toward anyone, but rather all waters flow toward it of their own accord, so too all desires enter that person alone, and having thus had his desires fulfilled, he attains peace. Even though outwardly stirred, he remains, like the ocean, firmly unmoved -- his true nature undisturbed at its core even by the waves of desire raised by external conditions. This is precisely the point of the comparison, brought out through the accompanying qualifying words of the verse.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
That the man of steady wisdom attains complete freedom from all disturbance is now confirmed through this illustration: just as the ocean is filled by all the rivers -- born of rain and other sources -- pouring into it from every side, and yet remains 'unmoved,' that is, never oversteps its own limit (expressing thereby the height of its depth and grandeur), so too, in that very same unchanging manner, all the objects of desire -- sound and the rest -- craved by ignorant people, inevitably enter the man of steady wisdom through the force of his past actions, and yet they are wholly unable to disturb his mind. Such a person, like a vast ocean, attains, through the strength of his knowledge, a peace that consists in freedom from all disturbance caused by worldly or scriptural activity, and also in the cessation of the residual effects of ignorance carried over by past momentum. The one devoted to desire, however, who by nature craves objects, does not attain this peace that has just been described; instead, he remains forever submerged in the vast ocean of grief through the constant disturbance of worldly and scriptural activity. This shows that renunciation through knowledge bears fruit only in the man of realization, and that liberation-while-living, consisting precisely in this freedom from all disturbance -- remaining unaffected even while experiencing objects that come by destiny -- belongs to him alone.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
An objection is raised: if the sage has no regard at all for sense-objects, how then does he experience them? This verse answers that objection. Just as the ocean, though continually filled by many rivers, remains unmoved and never exceeds its own bounds, receiving ever more water yet remaining unchanged, so too the objects of desire, drawn in by the momentum of the sage's past actions, enter the sage whose vision is turned inward, without disturbing him in any way through their enjoyment. Such a person attains peace, that is, liberation -- and not the one who by nature craves the enjoyment of objects.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Only such a person, and not one full of desire, attains liberation -- this is now confirmed by illustration. The ocean, though filled from every side by waters carrying all manner of things, remains 'unmoved,' that is, it never transgresses its own bounds, expressing thereby its unsurpassed depth and grandeur. In the very same way, all the objects that ordinary people crave enter the man of steady wisdom -- whose unshaken foundation rests upon the unmoving Brahman -- yet they simply dissolve within the Self and never bring him under their sway; such a person attains peace, the state called liberation, while the one who by nature craves objects never attains it. An objection is then raised: does not the scriptural teaching on 'abandoning desires' and restraining the senses imply that desires exist as entities separate from the Self, and would this not undermine non-dualism? This is answered through the same ocean illustration: just as the various waters entering the ocean -- some bearing thorns, some flowers -- are, in the very act of entering, indistinguishable from the ocean itself and dissolve within it, so too all desires, arising from the very Self, enter the man who, like the ocean, remains unmoved -- neither increasing nor decreasing -- and he attains peace, while the one who craves objects by nature does not. The point is that the earlier teaching about relinquishing desires does not establish that desires possess any independent reality apart from the Self; it merely speaks in terms of their apparent separateness as commonly assumed, and this understanding is consistent throughout the text.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
An objection is raised: since the devotee has no regard at all for worldly objects, how can any fulfilment come to him? This is answered: just as the ocean, though filled by many rivers, remains unmoved and free from any change such as swelling, so too, whoever understands that all his desires -- even those stirred through the delight of divine love -- ultimately enter into the Lord for the Lord's own sake, attains peace, the supreme bliss that is the cessation of all desire. This is confirmed in the Shrimad Bhagavatam (10.32.13), which states that even the aspirations voiced in the Vedas find their culmination there. The person devoted to worldly desire and sense-enjoyment, however, does not attain this. Alternatively: he into whom all desires enter in the manner just described -- one who longs for the Lord even without having seen Him, or who acts for the Lord's sake alone -- attains peace, supreme bliss; not the one who desires objects for his own sake.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
An objection is raised: does not the verse elsewhere speaking of 'relinquishing desires' and of withdrawing the senses from their objects establish that desires have an existence separate from the Self -- and would this not contradict the scriptural declaration that here there exists nothing manifold at all? For the phrase 'here there is nothing manifold' need only deny the independent existence of things apart from their underlying ground, not their existence altogether -- just as saying 'there is no pot on this patch of ground' does not deny that a pot exists elsewhere. On this reasoning desires would seem to possess a separate reality, undermining non-dualism. This objection is answered through the ocean simile: the ocean, whether being filled by waters entering it or diminished by waters leaving it, remains ever 'unmoved,' unaffected by increase or decrease, because its true nature never actually grows or shrinks. In just the same way, all desires -- which arise from the Self and dissolve back into the Self -- enter the man in whom they appear to increase or diminish, and yet leave him unmoved, since he is by nature free of increase and decrease; and only such a person attains peace, the ultimate cessation of sorrow called liberation -- not the one who desires objects. The point is that everything, having its origin and dissolution in the one changeless Self, is affirmed throughout scripture and tradition to arise from and dissolve into that Self alone; hence the mention of relinquishing desires and restraining the senses does not prove that they possess any ultimate reality apart from the Self, but merely follows the popular assumption of their apparent separateness. Thus the great Upanishadic declarations -- 'all this is the Self,' 'all this is Brahman,' 'all this is indeed Brahman' -- remain in full harmony, and the ocean simile for desire is entirely fitting, since an effect never possesses any existence independent of its cause, as expressed in the teaching that transformation is merely a verbal handle, a name only.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
A person who is not disturbed by the incessant flow of desires – that enter like rivers into the ocean, which is ever being filled but is always still – can alone achieve peace, and not the man who strives to satisfy such desires.

 Although the vast ocean is always filled with water, it is always, especially during the rainy season, being filled with much more water. But the ocean remains the same – steady; it is not agitated, nor does it cross beyond the limit of its brink. That is also true of a person fixed in Kṛṣṇa consciousness. As long as one has the material body, the demands of the body for sense gratification will continue. The devotee, however, is not disturbed by such desires, because of his fullness. A Kṛṣṇa conscious man is not in need of anything, because the Lord fulfills all his material necessities. Therefore he is like the ocean – always full in himself. Desires may come to him like the waters of the rivers that flow into the ocean, but he is steady in his activities, and he is not even slightly disturbed by desires for sense gratification. That is the proof of a Kṛṣṇa conscious man – one who has lost all inclinations for material sense gratification, although the desires are present. Because he remains satisfied in the transcendental loving service of the Lord, he can remain steady, like the ocean, and therefore enjoy full peace. Others, however, who want to fulfill desires even up to the limit of liberation, what to speak of material success, never attain peace. The fruitive workers, the salvationists, and also the yogīs who are after mystic powers are all unhappy because of unfulfilled desires. But the person in Kṛṣṇa consciousness is happy in the service of the Lord, and he has no desires to be fulfilled. In fact, he does not even desire liberation from the so-called material bondage. The devotees of Kṛṣṇa have no material desires, and therefore they are in perfect peace.
