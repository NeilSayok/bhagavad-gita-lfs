Life Application:
Step forward to perform your personal duties without hesitation when circumstances call for moral courage.

Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:
Thou must look at thy duty. Nothing can be more welcome to a soldier than a righteous war. Therefore to waver in this resolve is unworthy, O Arjuna!

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
A Kshatriya's role is to lead and safeguard society and nation; being a Kshatriya, it is Arjuna's duty to protect society from the danger of unrighteousness that has befallen it. His duty is to answer society's call, stand firm on the battlefield without wavering, and fight the enemy to preserve the nation's culture. For Kshatriyas there can be no greater privilege than the golden chance to display their valour in a righteous war. Here it is the unrighteous who struck first; hence it is not proper for Arjuna to withdraw from the fight. The Udyoga Parva of the Mahabharata states plainly that the sin of killing an innocent person is as great as the sin of failing to destroy one who deserves destruction. Having thus established the rightness of the war, the Lord goes on to give further reasons.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Introducing a further point while continuing the theme: the preceding verse spoke from the standpoint of the ultimate truth of the Self, where grief and delusion cannot arise at all. Not only on that basis, but also considering your own duty (svadharma), you should not waver — this is the connection intended. Anticipating the doubt that one might instead pursue whatever means of good is not opposed to duty, the verse specifies that this is the natural duty born of one's own caste, namely the Kshatriya's. It then shows what stands opposed to such fitness for wavering, addressing the further doubt that natural duty may lack scriptural sanction, and concludes that a Kshatriya devoted to dharma and the protection of subjects should not desist from war for the sake of anything supposedly more meritorious.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Even considering your own duty (the duty of a Kshatriya), you should not waver, that is, you should not turn away from your prescribed action; for there is no other welfare-bringing act for a Kshatriya greater than a righteous war. The self is itself a part of the Supreme; when it identifies with a body, whatever role it takes to be its own becomes its 'own duty' (svadharma). One who considers himself a Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya or Shudra has the duty proper to that station; a teacher or a servant has the duty of a teacher or servant; a father or a son has the duty appropriate to that relation. Here the Kshatriya's duty is termed dharma, and his special duty is never to turn away from battle. Since Arjuna is a Kshatriya, fighting is his own duty; hence the Lord says that even looked at from the standpoint of svadharma, fighting according to Kshatriya-dharma is Arjuna's duty, and he must never turn away from it. There is no other welfare-bringing act for a Kshatriya greater than a righteous war — performing one's own Kshatriya duty is itself the highest task (Gita 18.43); likewise, for a Brahmin, Vaishya or Shudra, there is no true good apart from performing their own respective duties. Earlier, in verse seven, Arjuna had asked to be told definitively what is truly good for him; in reply, the Lord says that true good comes only from following one's own dharma — abandoning it, from any point of view, can never be beneficial. Therefore Arjuna should not turn away from his duty in the form of this war.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Further, even though there is killing of life in this war which has begun, it is not fit for you to waver, considering your own duty, as in the Agnisomiya and other sacrifices involving slaughter. To a Ksatriya, there is no greater good than a righteous war, begun for a just cause. It will be declared in the Gita:  'Valour, non-defeat (by the enemies), fortitude, adroitness and also not fleeing from battle, generosity, lordliness - these are the duties of the Ksatriya born of his very nature.' (18.43).

In Agnisomiya etc., no injury is caused to the animal to be immolated; for, according to the Vedic Text, the victim, a he-goat, after abandoning an inferior body, will attain heaven etc., with a beautiful body. The Text pertaining to immolation declares:  'O animal, by this (immolation) you will never die, you are not destroyed. You will pass through happy paths to the realm of the gods, where the virtuous only reach and not the sinful. May the god Savitr give you a proper place.' (Yaj. 4.6.9.46). Likewise the attainment of more beautiful bodies by those who die here in this war has been declared in the Gita, 'As a man casts off worn-out garments and takes others that are new ৷৷.' (2.22). Hence, just as lancing and such other operations of a surgeon are for curing a patient, the immolation of the sacrificial animal in the Agnisomiya etc., is only for its good.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Having thus, through discrimination of the three limiting factors — the gross body, the subtle body, and the ignorance that is their cause — explained the true nature of the Self in order to dispel the illusion, common to all beings, that this unreal transmigratory world and its duties are real, the Lord now turns to dispel Arjuna's own particular delusion, born of excessive compassion, that this war, being full of violence, must be unrighteous. He teaches that even though war involves violence, when performed as one's own duty it does not become adharma. Not merely from regard to the ultimate truth of the Self, but also considering your own Kshatriya duty — which here means not turning away from battle, as examined through the scriptures — you ought not waver or turn back out of the mistaken notion that this duty is unrighteous. All of Arjuna's earlier hesitation about the sinfulness of war and about killing elders like Bhishma arose only from not properly examining the scriptures on duty. For there exists no other means of good for a Kshatriya superior to a righteous war that does not deviate from proper conduct; war alone, through the conquest of the earth, sustains the Kshatriya's duties of protecting subjects and serving Brahmins and others. This is affirmed by the sage Parashara and by Manu, who declare that a king summoned to protect his people should never retreat from battle while holding fast to his Kshatriya duty, since not fleeing from battle, protecting subjects, and serving Brahmins are the highest good for a king. It is thus well established that a righteous war is the most excellent duty for a Kshatriya, and this the Lord here rightly declares without fault.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
What Arjuna had said — that his body trembles and his hair stands on end — is thereby shown to be improper, since the Self can never be destroyed and killing these people need not disturb him. Moreover, considering your own duty, you should not waver — this is the connection intended. As for Arjuna's statement, 'I see no good in killing my kinsmen in battle,' it is here answered: there is no good greater than a righteous war — 'righteous' meaning proceeding according to what is right and just.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Thus it has been said that, from the standpoint of the truth of the Self, grief and delusion cannot arise. Not only from that standpoint, but also considering one's own duty, one should not waver — this is conveyed by 'considering your own duty.' Some hold that this addresses only the delusion common to all beings and separately removes Arjuna's own particular delusion, but that view is to be set aside on the strength of what was explained earlier. Having examined the scriptures, understand that the righteous war is the Kshatriya's own duty; overwhelmed by grief and delusion, you should not turn away from your own duty. Since war, through the conquest of the earth, serves the purpose of sacrifice and the protection of Brahmins and other subjects, and does not deviate from dharma, there is for a Kshatriya no other means to good comparable to it. Hence, even for one who seeks the good of liberation, there is no better means to the fruits of sacrifice and the protection of subjects than first securing victory through the performance of one's own duty of war.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Beginning with the word 'svadharma,' and continuing through verse 2.34, the Lord takes up and dispels doubts about what is righteous and unrighteous in this matter. The word 'api' (also) signals a further, distinct reason beyond what was said before. 'Svadharmam' refers to this very war that has begun. Anticipating the objection that such war, involving the killing of living beings, might still be thought irreligious like an animal sacrifice such as the Agnishomiya, the text reminds us that a specific scriptural injunction governing such righteous violence is not overridden by the general prohibition against harming beings. Such acts are, properly understood, not injury at all: the Vedic mantras declare that the sacrificial animal, after giving up an inferior body, attains a more beautiful one in heaven, and it has likewise been said that those slain here in righteous war attain, through death, more excellent bodies — just as the soul casts off worn-out garments and takes new ones (2.22). Hence, just as a surgeon's cutting is for the patient's own good, the killing that occurs in sacrifices such as the Agnishomiya is, in truth, for the good of the being immolated. So too, there is no other means of good for a Kshatriya greater than a righteous war undertaken for the protection of subjects and the sustenance of dharma — this alone is proclaimed the most excellent duty for a Kshatriya.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having thus taught, through knowledge of the true nature of the Self, that grief is not to be entertained, the Lord now says that Arjuna should not grieve over abandoning his own duty either. Considering his Kshatriya duty, he should not waver, for this alone is the highest duty for Kshatriyas; hence, there is no good for a Kshatriya apart from a righteous war — it is through this alone that Kshatriyas attain the higher worlds.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Having removed Arjuna's delusion of mistaking the non-Self, the body, for the Self, the Lord now removes his delusion of regarding his own duty, this war, as unrighteous, beginning with the words 'considering your own duty.' War is the Kshatriya's own duty; even considering it, Arjuna should not waver, for there is no greater good for a Kshatriya than a righteous war that does not deviate from dharma.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Considering your specific duty as a kṣatriya, you should know that there is no better engagement for you than fighting on religious principles; and so there is no need for hesitation.

 Out of the four orders of social administration, the second order, for the matter of good administration, is called kṣatriya. Kṣat means hurt. One who gives protection from harm is called kṣatriya ( trāyate – to give protection). The kṣatriyas are trained for killing in the forest. A kṣatriya would go into the forest and challenge a tiger face to face and fight with the tiger with his sword. When the tiger was killed, it would be offered the royal order of cremation. This system has been followed even up to the present day by the kṣatriya kings of Jaipur state. The kṣatriyas are specially trained for challenging and killing because religious violence is sometimes a necessary factor. Therefore, kṣatriyas are never meant for accepting directly the order of sannyāsa, or renunciation. Nonviolence in politics may be a diplomacy, but it is never a factor or principle. In the religious law books it is stated: āhaveṣu mitho ’nyonyaṁ jighāṁsanto mahī-kṣitaḥ yuddhamānāḥ paraṁ śaktyā svargaṁ yānty aparāṅ-mukhāḥ yajñeṣu paśavo brahman hanyante satataṁ dvijaiḥ saṁskṛtāḥ kila mantraiś ca te ’pi svargam avāpnuvan “In the battlefield, a king or kṣatriya, while fighting another king envious of him, is eligible for achieving the heavenly planets after death, as the brāhmaṇas also attain the heavenly planets by sacrificing animals in the sacrificial fire.” Therefore, killing on the battlefield on religious principles and killing animals in the sacrificial fire are not at all considered to be acts of violence, because everyone is benefited by the religious principles involved. The animal sacrificed gets a human life immediately without undergoing the gradual evolutionary process from one form to another, and the kṣatriyas killed on the battlefield also attain the heavenly planets, as do the brāhmaṇas who attain them by offering sacrifice. There are two kinds of sva-dharmas, specific duties. As long as one is not liberated, one has to perform the duties of his particular body in accordance with religious principles in order to achieve liberation. When one is liberated, one’s sva-dharma – specific duty – becomes spiritual and is not in the material bodily concept. In the bodily conception of life there are specific duties for the brāhmaṇas and kṣatriyas respectively, and such duties are unavoidable. Sva-dharma is ordained by the Lord, and this will be clarified in the Fourth Chapter. On the bodily plane sva-dharma is called varṇāśrama-dharma, or man’s steppingstone for spiritual understanding. Human civilization begins from the stage of varṇāśrama-dharma, or specific duties in terms of the specific modes of nature of the body obtained. Discharging one’s specific duty in any field of action in accordance with the orders of higher authorities serves to elevate one to a higher status of life.
