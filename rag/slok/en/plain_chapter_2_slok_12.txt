Life Application:
Remember that your fundamental essence is eternal, reducing your fear of change, loss, or physical mortality.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Here the Lord clearly declares that the Self, wearing a body, has set out on a great pilgrimage, taking on various bodies for a time in the course of this journey, identifying with them and thereby gaining particular experiences. That Krishna, Arjuna and the other kings came to be in those particular bodies was no chance occurrence. They did not come from nothingness, nor will they become nothingness after death. Through authentic philosophical reflection, a person can understand the continuous chain of events across past, present and future. The Self, remaining ever itself, appears to take on many bodies and experience the circumstances it thereby obtains.

This is the celebrated doctrine of rebirth in Hindu philosophy. The greatest opponents of this doctrine appear not to have properly studied even their own scriptures. Christ Himself accepted this doctrine, if not directly then indirectly, when He told His disciples that John was Elijah. The learned Christian father Origen stated plainly that every person receives this body as the fruit of the merits of a former birth.

There is no great thinker who has not accepted the doctrine of past births, whether directly or indirectly. The Buddha regularly referred to his own past lives. Both Virgil and Ovid accepted this doctrine as self-evident. Josephus said that in his time there was ample belief in rebirth among the Jews. Solomon, in the Book of Wisdom, says that to be born with sound limbs in a healthy body is the fruit of virtuous deeds performed in a former life. And who does not know the saying of the Prophet Muhammad of Islam, who said, 'I died from a stone and became a plant, died from a plant and became an animal, died from an animal and became man — why then should I fear death? When did dying ever diminish me? Dying from man, I shall become an angel.'

In a later era, the German philosophers Goethe, Fichte, Schelling and Lessing too accepted this doctrine. Twentieth-century philosophers like Hume, Spencer and Max Muller regarded it as beyond dispute. Famous Western poets too, wandering the clear skies of imagination, sensed this very doctrine through inner inspiration — among them chiefly Browning, Rossetti, Tennyson, Wordsworth and others.

The doctrine of rebirth is no mere fancy of philosophers. The day is not far when, under the weight and influence of the facts being rapidly gathered in the fast-developing field of psychology, Western nations will be forced to rewrite their scriptures. Those who wish to understand the reality of life without prejudice or pressure are troubled by the visible inequalities of the world. All these cannot simply be dismissed as mere coincidence. If we accept reason, we must accept the existence of the individual soul as distinct from the body. Mozart offers a striking example: at four years of age he composed orchestral pieces, at five performed before audiences, and at seven composed a musical drama. Looking at the life of India's Shankaracharya and others, we find how profound was their knowledge even in childhood. Without accepting the doctrine of rebirth, such astonishing occurrences would have to be tossed into the wastebin as mere coincidence.

Many incidents proving rebirth are observed, yet very few of them are properly documented as evidence. As I have said, the modern world's research into this great self-evident law of life still remains to be done. A person of immature understanding may at first doubt this doctrine. When the Lord said that none of them would perish, Arjuna, being an ordinary person of the world, could not properly grasp this statement. He looked at Sri Krishna with a questioning expression, seeking further clarification.

Why should there be no grief for these? Because, in their essential nature, they are all eternal. How? — the Lord says—

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Having indicated that eternality is the reason for not-to-be-grievability, he now takes up, through a question, the task of examining it, in 'Kutah...': he raises the objection that eternality itself is unproven for want of any evidence, in 'Katham...': he resolves this, in 'Na tv eva...': the Self does not undergo birth, since it has never been in a state of prior non-existence — like the horn of a man (a stock example of the impossible). Further, he states another inference: the Self is eternal, since, though existent, it is unborn — like a pot is not, by way of contrast, is the sense in 'na chaiva...' Some hold that the Lord here, in these four verses beginning 'na tu...', is answering an inquiry into the Self's true nature — this is mistaken, since no special reason is given here to distinguish this teaching from the general exposition of the Self's true nature found everywhere else in the text; hence he takes up, in 'na tv iti...', the threefold task proper to commentary — word-division, statement of word-meanings, and syntactic construction. One might object: since it is well known that the body undergoes origination and destruction, and the Self resides in the body, would not the two inferences given above (regarding the Self's birthlessness and deathlessness) then be invalidated as contradicting common observation? He answers: this apparent origination and destruction, observed in past instances such as pots, belongs properly to that which is dependent on adjuncts, since 'that which depends on the moving and unmoving is thus' is the accepted maxim — hence, for the Self, which is without such adjuncts in its true nature, birth and the rest simply do not apply. Though this argument establishes the Lord's own birthlessness as Ishvara, how does it apply to me, Arjuna, might be asked; he answers, in 'tatha...': likewise for you. But how, then, is the very birth of Bhishma and the rest to be denied? — he answers, in 'tatha cha neme...': likewise. He then elaborates the second inference, explaining the latter half of the verse, in 'tatha...': one might further object: though the Self undergoes no birth or destruction correlating with that of the body, does it not undergo birth and destruction correlating with the great cosmic dissolution and re-creation, per the scriptural simile of sparks from a fire? Anticipating this, he resolves it by the maxim 'the Self is not subject to scripture [in this regard, being self-evident]', in 'trishv api...': and lest it be thought that, since souls are inferred to be distinct from one another (as shown by the plural usage), they must therefore be subject to modification and change, he clarifies, in 'dehe...', that the plural refers only to the plurality of bodies, not to any real plurality or mutability in the Self itself.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
It is not that at some time I was not, nor that you were not, nor that these kings were not; nor is it that hereafter I, you and these kings — all of us — shall cease to be.

Explanation: In this world there are only two things — the embodied Self (the real) and the body (the unreal). Both are equally not to be grieved for: grief cannot properly attach to the embodied Self, since it never ceases to exist, nor to the body, since it never truly remains. The word 'ashochyan' (not to be grieved for) used in the previous verse is now explained through the eternality of the embodied Self and the transience of the body.

'Na tv evaham...janadhipah' — from the standpoint of ordinary perception, before I took incarnation I was not manifest before everyone in this particular form (as Krishna); before your birth, you were not manifest in this form (as Arjuna); and before these kings were born, they too were not manifest in this royal form. But it is not the case that I, you and these kings did not exist at all before manifesting in these particular forms. Here, rather than simply saying 'we existed before,' the Lord says 'it is not that we did not exist before' — a double negative that more firmly establishes 'we certainly did exist before.' The sense is: the eternal principle is always eternal; it never had any non-existence at all. The word 'jatu' (at any time) conveys that in past, present or future, and in any place, condition, state, event or object whatsoever, the eternal principle can never, even in the slightest degree, be absent.

Here, by using the word 'I,' the Lord makes a remarkable statement. Later, in the fourth chapter (4.5), the Lord tells Arjuna that both of them have had many births, but He knows them while Arjuna does not — there, the Lord reveals His divine lordship and distinguishes Himself from ordinary beings. But here, the Lord asserts His oneness with all beings. The distinction is: there (4.5) the Lord's intent is to reveal His own greatness and uniqueness, while here His intent is to convey, from the philosophical standpoint, knowledge of the eternal principle common to all.

'Na chaiva...vayam atah param' — in the future these bodily states will not remain, and one day these bodies too will cease to exist, but even in that state it is not that we shall all cease to be — that is, we shall certainly continue to exist. For the eternal principle never had non-existence, nor will it ever have any.

Having thus spoken of past and future, the Lord does not explicitly mention the present. This is because, from the standpoint of the bodies, our present existence is directly evident and beyond doubt — hence there was no need to say 'we exist now.' But viewed philosophically, we are all present right now, and these bodies are changing every moment — so we ought to experience, even now in the present, our separateness from the body. The point is: just as we have no non-existence in the past or future, so too we should recognize we have no non-existence even in the present.

Just as every being, even before waking from sleep, carries the sense 'we exist now,' and upon waking too carries the sense 'we exist now,' this shows that even in the state of sleep we remained exactly as we were — only the external means of knowing were absent, not our own existence. In the same way, our bodies — mine, yours, and these kings' — did not exist before and will not remain afterward, and even now they are moving moment by moment toward destruction; yet our own existence existed before, will remain after, and is exactly what it is right now.

Our existence is a principle beyond time, since we are the very knower of time itself — that is, all three times, past, future and present, come within our knowing. It is precisely to explain this principle beyond time that the Lord speaks this verse.

A special point: saying 'it is not that I, you and these kings did not exist before, nor that we shall not remain hereafter' conveys that even when these bodies did not exist, we all existed, and even when these bodies will no longer exist, we shall still exist — that is, all these bodies are perishable, while we are all imperishable. That these bodies did not exist before and will not remain hereafter proves the impermanence of bodies; and that we all existed before and shall remain hereafter proves the eternality of everyone's true nature. From these two facts a single principle is established: that which exists at the beginning and the end also exists in between; and that which does not exist at the beginning and end does not exist in between either.

One might ask: how can something not exist in between if it does not exist at the beginning and end, since it does appear visible to us? The answer is this: from the very standpoint — that is, through the very mind, intellect and senses — by which the visible object is experienced, that visible object, together with the mind, intellect and senses themselves, is changing every single moment; not one of them remains stable even for an instant. And yet, when the Self identifies with the visible object, it becomes the 'seer,' the observer. But when the very instruments of seeing (mind, intellect, senses) and the visible object (their contents) are themselves not stable even for a moment, how can the seer be established as stable? The point is: the very designation 'seer' arises only through the relationship with the seen and the act of seeing; without that relationship, there would be no designation 'seer' at all — rather, only the eternal principle that is its very ground remains. That eternal principle we may call the ground of the origination, continuance and dissolution of us all, and the illuminator of every appearance. But even these names, 'ground' and 'illuminator,' arise only through relationship with what is grounded and what is illuminated. Even without the grounded and the illuminated, its existence remains exactly as it is. How can grief arise in one whose vision is fixed on that very real principle? It cannot. It is from this very standpoint that I, you and these kings are, in our essential nature, not to be grieved for.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
I, the Lord of all, in the beginningless time prior to the present, was not non-existent — rather I certainly was. Likewise you and these individual souls, whose lord I am, were not non-existent — rather you certainly were. And likewise I and all of you, in the time following the present, shall not cease to be — rather we shall certainly continue to be. Just as I, the Supreme Self, the Lord of all, am eternal beyond doubt, so too you, the individual souls, should be understood to be equally eternal. Thus it appears that the Lord Himself here declares that the distinction between Himself and the individual souls, and among the souls themselves, is ultimately real — since, at the very moment of teaching this ultimate eternal truth to one deluded by ignorance for the sake of dispelling that delusion, He specifically distinguishes 'I,' 'you,' 'these,' and 'all of us.' On the theory that such distinction is merely a product of limiting adjuncts and therefore not ultimately real, such a distinction would make no sense at precisely the moment of imparting ultimate truth. Scripture too confirms that the distinction taught by the Lord is essential and natural, not adventitious: 'the eternal among the eternals, the conscious among the many conscious beings, the one who ordains the desires of all' (Shvetashvatara 6.13) — meaning: that one eternal, conscious being among the many eternal, conscious beings, who ordains their desires. On the rival theory that this apparent distinction is merely a product of ignorance, and that upon direct realization of the Supreme Person's true nature as undifferentiated, eternal, pure consciousness such teaching and instruction would themselves become impossible (since ignorance and its effects would have ceased), this position is untenable at length: for if it be said that this teaching, though sublated in ultimate knowledge, nonetheless persists as a mere convention, like a burnt cloth retaining its shape — this does not hold, since a sublated cognition, like the illusory perception of water in a mirage, does not, even while persisting as an appearance, motivate someone toward action such as fetching water; here too, the awareness of difference, once sublated by non-dual knowledge, being ascertained as referring to something unreal, could not motivate any teaching or activity. Nor can it be said that the Lord's own persisting sense of difference here is like the case of a sublated-yet-persisting cognition, since He is described elsewhere as omniscient, all-knowing, and this would contradict such statements as 'I know the past, the present, and all future beings, O Arjuna, but no one knows Me' (7.26). Moreover, if the Supreme Person and the entire succession of teachers down to the present truly possess the ascertainment of a non-dual Self, upon what basis, and to whom, could the notion of duality — including the very act of instruction — still be taught, since the ignorance and its effects, the very cause of such dualistic teaching, would already be destroyed by that very knowledge? Whereas in ordinary illusions like the perception of a double moon, the persisting appearance is explained by an undestroyed causal defect (such as an eye disorder) distinct from the object of the correcting cognition — but here, since the awareness of difference has both its object and its cause rooted in what is, on the non-dual theory, itself unreal and hence destroyed by the very knowledge of ultimate truth, there is no possible basis for its persistence in any sense. Hence, if the Lord and the guru-tradition truly possess non-dual realization, no dualistic teaching could arise at all; and if such dualistic teaching does arise, then, by that very fact, the necessary ignorance underlying it must still be present, and hence such 'knowers' remain themselves unenlightened, rendering the entire theory self-defeating. Thus the many refined arguments proposed on the non-dualist side are refuted, and the distinction taught by the Lord between Himself and the individual souls, and among the souls, stands established as ultimately real.

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
'Why?' — for what reason do the wise not grieve for the departed as for the living, since destruction is imminent for both? — the answer here is: because of their eternality. Whence this eternality? — this is what he now explains. The construction supplies, after 'na tv evaham,' the word 'parah' (beyond). Since the Lord's teaching, occasioned by Arjuna, is truly given for the benefit of all the world, the plain construction applies to those who do not know the Lord Krishna's lordship and eternality; but for those who, though knowing this, still do not know the individual soul's eternality, a different construction applies, as he explains next: since the Lord's own eternality is not the immediate topic, it is stated by way of illustration, in order to establish the desired conclusion of the soul's eternality. Hence 'Ishvara' (the Lord), not 'Krishna,' is said. One might ask: how is the Lord's eternality itself established, given that, though the first half of the verse forms three separable clauses admitting some construal, the second half forms a single indivisible sentence — how then can it serve as an illustration? He answers: through the Vedanta texts (the Upanishads); since to state an already-established truth as though it were still to be proven would itself be a flaw, the word 'yatha' (as) and a division of the sentence must be supplied. One might further ask: if the Vedanta texts already establish the Lord's eternality, why would people, knowing this from the very same texts, still fail to know the soul's eternality stated right alongside it? The answer: from a text such as 'the eternal among the eternals' (Katha 5.13, Shvetashvatara 6.13), which conveys, by way of comparison, both the souls' eternality and the Lord's supreme eternality, the dull-witted grasp only that the Lord alone is truly eternal and that the term is applied to souls merely figuratively — reasoning that eternality means the absence of destruction, and that absence admits of no degrees, so that only the Lord, strictly speaking, is eternal. Against such people, the soul's eternality is established by inference, using the Lord as the illustrative example: 'never was there a time when I was not' establishes beginninglessness through this example; 'nor you' and the rest establish that this same property belongs to the soul as well; 'nor shall we cease to be' establishes the corresponding property in both the example and the case at hand. Here, though the Lord teaches that souls are distinct from each other and from Him, the use of the plural is with reference to their bodies, not their true nature as Self — this being the intended resolution [per Shankaracharya], to be given later.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
In answer to the question 'why,' he explains: those alone are truly grievable who, having been born, will die; but for the Self, origination itself is impossible — hence, by this doctrine of the Self, refuting any notion of the Self's origination, he declares its not-to-be-grievability, in 'Na tv eva aham...': just as in the statement 'I, you the truthful, and these residents of Dvaraka,' the Lord here denies any coming-into-being of the Self across all three times, exactly as He denies it of the body. Never at any time was I, the Supreme Self, born, nor did I not exist, nor did you not come into being or exist; these kings standing here too did not come into being — rather, all are ever eternal, pure, conscious Selves alone, and hence not to be grieved for. By the scriptural statement 'the eternal among the eternals, the conscious among the conscious ones' (Katha 5.13, Shvetashvatara 6.13), the notion of the Self's origination as any kind of real modification is thereby refuted.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Through nineteen verses beginning 'Na tv eva...', the statement 'you have grieved for those not to be grieved for' is elaborated; through the eight verses beginning 'having regard even to your own dharma...' (2.31), the statement 'you speak the words of the wise' is elaborated — since these two delusions require separate treatment. Here, the eternality of the Self as distinct from the gross body is established. The word 'tu' indicates the Self's distinctness from the body and the rest. Just as I never, at any time in the past, was not — that is, I certainly always was — so too you were, and these kings were. By this, the Self's freedom from prior non-existence is shown. Likewise, all of us — I, you, and these kings — shall not, hereafter, cease to be — that is, we shall certainly continue to be. By this, the Self's freedom from subsequent destruction is shown. Hence, being existent in all three times, the Self's eternality, and thereby its distinctness from the non-eternal body, is established, is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
He states the reason for not-to-be-grievability, in 'Na tv eva...': just as I, the Supreme Lord, was never, at any time, not — even amid the appearance and disappearance of my own playful embodied forms — that is, I certainly always was, since I am beginningless. Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
He states the reason for their not-to-be-grievability arising from their eternality, in 'Na...': it is not that at any time I was not — rather I certainly was. Likewise, it is not that you were not — rather you certainly were. Likewise, these kings were not non-existent — rather they certainly were. And it is not that we shall not continue to be — rather we certainly shall, hereafter, that is, in the time to come, even after this destruction of the body. In all three times, we are eternal in our essential nature as the Self, is the sense. The inclusion of oneself within the collective enumeration conveys the intent of the Self's unity, while the plural is used with reference to the plurality of individual souls, following the plurality of bodies — not with a view toward any real plurality in the Self in the ultimate sense. Accordingly, the Sharirakabhashya states: 'difference is caused only by adjuncts, imagined through false knowledge, not real in the ultimate sense' — this should be understood.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Having roused in Arjuna the desire to understand the true nature of what is to be attained (the Self), the means to attain it, and what must be abandoned, the Lord, in order to make him fit for the pursuit of the otherworldly goal, first teaches — as the necessary object of knowledge distinct from the body, and the very thing to be desired for its own sake — the Self itself, so as to awaken in him the desire for its attainment and, through that, the desire for the means to attain it. The word 'shrinu' ('hear') is used here, not merely to reproach Arjuna, but to secure his full attention. The Lord first states the point most readily grasped regarding the eternality of both the individual soul and the Supreme Self, in 'aham' — 'I': the term 'Ishvara' (Lord) here, denoting one who rules over all, expresses the Lord's role as ruler of all. He is designated in both grammatical genders elsewhere, expressing His control over all — for were there no ultimate difference between the Lord and the individual souls, there would be a contradiction in nature between them (one all-ruling, one suffering); and were there no ultimate difference among the souls themselves, the entire scheme of bound and liberated souls, disciples and teachers, and so forth, would make no sense.

It is the Lord Himself, not some ordinary charioteer-figure, who here declares the truth of Himself and of the individual souls who are, as it were, His own body — as scripture says, 'you alone truly know yourself, whoever you are' and 'whether one knows this or not' (Rig-Veda). The teaching of one deluded by ignorance must not itself become the further cause of delusion — hence, in response to the objection 'why does the Lord not teach through an avatara such as Buddha, misleading the wicked, as needed for other purposes,' it is said that this teaching is 'for the removal of that very delusion' — the Gita-Upanishad exists precisely for the removal of delusion, as even Shankara himself acknowledges in his own commentary.

He refutes Shankara's view that the plural usage here refers only to bodily distinctions, not to any real distinction among Selves: this text, 'na tv eva aham...', is not merely a mantra recited for removing confusion, such that we might construe its distinctions as referring to something other than what is stated; rather, it is a direct teaching of ultimate truth. 'I' refers to the first person, the inward Self; 'you' to another conscious being addressed directly; 'these' to many conscious beings not so addressed; 'all' to the many individuals gathered under one common designation; 'we' to the endless individuals classed together with oneself as of the same nature. The very use of these distinct designations shows that no real negation of 'I,' 'you,' or the rest as the true referents is intended.

Against the alternative Bhaskara-school view — that this distinction, while resting on real (not illusory) limiting adjuncts, is nevertheless not ultimately real of the Self itself — the same general refutation applies: just as the distinction of a single moon reflected in various media (jewel, sword, river, ocean), though the media are real, is not an ultimate distinction in the moon; and just as space, though associated with real adjuncts like pots, is not itself really divided by them beyond a merely relational distinction — so too Brahman, being partless, beyond division, and completely full everywhere, cannot be really distinguished by even real adjuncts such as the inner faculties. Hence, at the very moment of imparting ultimate truth, a teacher who truly wished to benefit the hearer would not teach the very opposite (namely, real distinction) if it were false. Both rival views also stand refuted by conflict with scripture; the Lord's own scriptural citation ('nityo nityanam...') is offered as authoritative in its own right, requiring no further proof, exactly like the Vedas themselves — and, construed properly (as in the parallel usage 'the purest among the pure,' Mahabharata 13.149.10), the text conclusively establishes, through grammatical coordination of the terms for eternality, plurality, and consciousness, that the souls' plurality and consciousness, like their eternality, are unconditioned and real — thereby refuting not only the Buddhists (who deny the Self's eternality), but also Shankara and those like him (who hold that difference is rooted merely in ignorance), the Vaisheshikas and others (who hold consciousness to be a mere adventitious quality subject to origination and cessation), and various other rival schools.

He then, by a further sweeping argument, shows that the entire Advaita position collapses at its very root, since teaching itself becomes impossible on that theory: does the Lord teach something He Himself already knows, or something unknown even to Him? If known, is it known by direct realization or merely by having heard of it? In either case, has the ignorance underlying it and its effects (the perception of difference) already ceased for Him, or not? If it has ceased through direct realization, then the individual soul's (i.e. the Lord's, viewed as a bound soul) very perspective would itself be non-ultimate, undermining the possibility of any true teaching at all.

He examines and rejects, one by one, the various technical evasions offered by the opposing school: that the persisting sense of difference, though sublated by ultimate knowledge, might survive like a burnt cloth retaining its shape without any further binding function — this fails, since a sublated cognition (like the illusory water of a mirage) never motivates real activity, whereas the Lord's teaching plainly does; that this survival might be attributed to unremoved subtle impressions (vasanas) — this too fails for the same reason, and further because, unlike the case of an optical illusion (double moon) where an undestroyed physical defect explains the persistence, here the very cause of the perception of difference (ignorance) is, by the opponent's own theory, already destroyed by the liberating knowledge, leaving no possible basis for its continuance; that the Lord's or the guru's knowledge might itself be merely provisional or heard-of rather than fully realized — this too fails, contradicting the many scriptural statements of the Lord's omniscience, such as 'I know the past, present and future, but none knows Me' (7.26) and 'His diverse powers are described as inherent, comprising both knowledge and action' (Shvetashvatara 6.8). He concludes that the entire Advaita framework, being caught between mutually destructive alternatives — either no true teaching is possible if the teacher is already liberated and non-dually aware, or the teacher himself remains bound and ignorant if such distinct teaching persists — collapses under its own weight, is riddled with self-contradiction, conflicts with scripture, and renders the very commencement of the scriptural teaching purposeless. He closes by affirming, from the accepted meaning of the text, that it is the one Lord who, eternally and everywhere, presiding over the karma-based worship offered by each of the many eternal conscious beings, dispenses to each what is fitting.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having stated devotion-lessness as the reason for not-to-be-grievability, he now states a further reason for the same, in 'Na tu...': I, exactly as you shall see Me, was never at any time not — rather I have always, eternally, been just as I am. By this, His own eternality is declared. One might ask: given your eternality, how are these not-to-be-grieved-for? He answers: nor were you, nor were these kings not — rather, since all is but a manifestation of My own divine play, all is eternal indeed. By this, even the death of the demonic is shown to be eternal in this same sense. Hence these are but illusion (maya), and thus not to be grieved for. One might further object: if I should die in this battle, then separation from your feet, or the consequence of unrighteous conduct, would result — hence I grieve. He answers, in 'Na chaiva...': hereafter, that is, after the present moment, it is not that all of us shall cease to be — rather we shall certainly continue to be. Thus, since all is eternal, all is not to be grieved for, and you ought not grieve, is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
One might object: is it not the case that whatever else is distinct from the body also perishes when the body perishes, just as the sheath-maker perishes with the destruction of the sheath? To this he says, in 'Na tv eva aham...': you, I, and all these are beginningless and endless, is the sense. It is not that at any time I was not — rather I certainly was. Likewise, it is not that you were not — rather you certainly were. 'These kings' — a representative example standing for the whole class of living beings — it is not that they were not — rather they certainly were, is the construction. Being beginningless, they are also endless, he says, in 'Na cha...': it is not that we shall not continue to be — rather we shall all certainly continue to be. One might object: if the body is not the Self, how then is the Self afflicted by the body's affliction? We answer: just as when a spirit possesses another's body, the true owner of that body is not afflicted by its suffering, since he has, at that time, no identification with it, whereas the possessing spirit is afflicted, since it does have such identification — this is well known in common experience. Moreover, life itself cannot be accounted for apart from past karma, on pain of the fault of either losing what is earned or incurring what is not earned; even in trees and the like, past karma must be inferred, since their means of livelihood, like ours, presuppose prior action, exactly as our own livelihood presupposes prior acts such as cooking. Moreover, just as diversity of effect is observed to follow from diversity of action, as in the varied shapes of a pot, dish, water-vessel and the like from the same clay, so too the diversity of pleasure and pain and the like here must be inferred from the diversity of past karma. Likewise, the newborn calf's instinctive movement toward the mother's teat, and every creature's inborn fear of death, arise from impressions born of experience in a former life, exactly as breathing does — hence past karma indeed exists. Moreover, this is well established in the tantric tradition: just as Devadatta suffers pain from a thorn piercing his own body, so too, when an enemy pierces with a thorn an effigy made in Devadatta's likeness, Devadatta suffers pain — here, the cause of the pain is neither any internal humoral imbalance nor any external piercing, but solely his past karma. Thus, by the analogy of seed and sprout, through the beginningless succession of karma and the impressions born of it, worldly existence is without beginning; hence there is no destruction of the Self through the destruction of the body, and thus Bhishma and the rest are not to be grieved for. In the previous verse, the Self's distinctness from the body was stated through the qualifier 'gatasun,' the bodies; here, however, the eternality of the Self as characterized by its subtle-body accompaniment is established from the empirical standpoint — this is the distinction between the two verses.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Never was there a time when I did not exist, nor you, nor all these kings; nor in the future shall any of us cease to be.

 In the Vedas – in the Kaṭha Upaniṣad as well as in the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad – it is said that the Supreme Personality of Godhead is the maintainer of innumerable living entities, in terms of their different situations according to individual work and reaction of work. That Supreme Personality of Godhead is also, by His plenary portions, alive in the heart of every living entity. Only saintly persons who can see, within and without, the same Supreme Lord can actually attain to perfect and eternal peace. nityo nityānāṁ cetanaś cetanānām eko bahūnāṁ yo vidadhāti kāmān tam ātma-sthaṁ ye ’nupaśyanti dhīrās teṣāṁ śāntiḥ śāśvatī netareṣām ( Kaṭha Upaniṣad 2.2.13) The same Vedic truth given to Arjuna is given to all persons in the world who pose themselves as very learned but factually have but a poor fund of knowledge. The Lord says clearly that He Himself, Arjuna and all the kings who are assembled on the battlefield are eternally individual beings and that the Lord is eternally the maintainer of the individual living entities both in their conditioned and in their liberated situations. The Supreme Personality of Godhead is the supreme individual person, and Arjuna, the Lord’s eternal associate, and all the kings assembled there are individual eternal persons. It is not that they did not exist as individuals in the past, and it is not that they will not remain eternal persons. Their individuality existed in the past, and their individuality will continue in the future without interruption. Therefore, there is no cause for lamentation for anyone. The Māyāvādī theory that after liberation the individual soul, separated by the covering of māyā, or illusion, will merge into the impersonal Brahman and lose its individual existence is not supported herein by Lord Kṛṣṇa, the supreme authority. Nor is the theory that we only think of individuality in the conditioned state supported herein. Kṛṣṇa clearly says herein that in the future also the individuality of the Lord and others, as it is confirmed in the Upaniṣads, will continue eternally. This statement of Kṛṣṇa’s is authoritative because Kṛṣṇa cannot be subject to illusion. If individuality were not a fact, then Kṛṣṇa would not have stressed it so much – even for the future. The Māyāvādī may argue that the individuality spoken of by Kṛṣṇa is not spiritual, but material. Even accepting the argument that the individuality is material, then how can one distinguish Kṛṣṇa’s individuality? Kṛṣṇa affirms His individuality in the past and confirms His individuality in the future also. He has confirmed His individuality in many ways, and impersonal Brahman has been declared to be subordinate to Him. Kṛṣṇa has maintained spiritual individuality all along; if He is accepted as an ordinary conditioned soul in individual consciousness, then His Bhagavad-gītā has no value as authoritative scripture. A common man with all the four defects of human frailty is unable to teach that which is worth hearing. The Gītā is above such literature. No mundane book compares with the Bhagavad-gītā . When one accepts Kṛṣṇa as an ordinary man, the Gītā loses all importance. The Māyāvādī argues that the plurality mentioned in this verse is conventional and that it refers to the body. But previous to this verse such a bodily conception is already condemned. After condemning the bodily conception of the living entities, how was it possible for Kṛṣṇa to place a conventional proposition on the body again? Therefore, individuality is maintained on spiritual grounds and is thus confirmed by great ācāryas like Śrī Rāmānuja and others. It is clearly mentioned in many places in the Gītā that this spiritual individuality is understood by those who are devotees of the Lord. Those who are envious of Kṛṣṇa as the Supreme Personality of Godhead have no bona fide access to the great literature. The nondevotee’s approach to the teachings of the Gītā is something like that of a bee licking on a bottle of honey. One cannot have a taste of honey unless one opens the bottle. Similarly, the mysticism of the Bhagavad-gītā can be understood only by devotees, and no one else can taste it, as it is stated in the Fourth Chapter of the book. Nor can the Gītā be touched by persons who envy the very existence of the Lord. Therefore, the Māyāvādī explanation of the Gītā is a most misleading presentation of the whole truth. Lord Caitanya has forbidden us to read commentations made by the Māyāvādīs and warns that one who takes to such an understanding of the Māyāvādī philosophy loses all power to understand the real mystery of the Gītā. If individuality refers to the empirical universe, then there is no need of teaching by the Lord. The plurality of the individual soul and the Lord is an eternal fact, and it is confirmed by the Vedas as above mentioned.
