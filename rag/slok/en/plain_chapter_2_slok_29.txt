Life Application:
Approach self-inquiry with deep wonder and humility, knowing that spiritual truth is experienced rather than merely debated.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The ultimate Truth is described as infinite, all-knowing, and of the nature of bliss, whereas our experience of ourselves is that we are limited, ignorant, and sorrowful. Thus our direct experience is entirely at odds with our true nature as the Self. The gap between the ultimate nature and direct experience appears as vast as that between cold and heat, light and darkness. Why is it that we cannot directly experience our own pure Self? In the state of ignorance, when we wish to know the Truth, we imagine It to be some distant goal, to be attained only at some future time. But if we trust the Lord's teaching, we come to know that we are never far from that Truth at all, since It is our very own nature. A mortal being is as far from immortality as a dreamer is from the waking man. One who is aware of the glory of his own true nature is God, and God forgetful of the glory of his own nature is the deluded soul. At first it is difficult even to grasp the idea that there exists a Self beyond body, mind, and intellect; and when one practices the discipline of Self-realization and comes to recognize one's own blissful nature, one is struck with wonder on experiencing that infinite bliss beyond the senses. When wonder arises in the mind, it has the power to leave a person, for a moment, unable to think of anything else, so that in that instant one becomes one with that very feeling. As an experiment, startle someone suddenly and observe the expression on his face -- mouth open, eyes staring blankly, every nerve taut, the person standing rooted to the spot like a statue, utterly bewildered for that moment. Such, too, is the bliss of Self-realization, when the Self rests and delights in the Self alone, by the Self. And it is for this reason that the great sages indicated this experience by the word 'wonder' -- when the ego-bound individual self dissolves and only the pure, infinite nature remains. The ignorant man thinks 'I am the body in which the Self dwells,' but the wise man knows 'I am the Self that has taken on a body.' Those seekers who properly hear this teaching are then encouraged to reflect upon it, and thereafter are instructed to meditate until true Self-realization is attained. This verse can inspire even the ignorant to attain this rare and supreme knowledge through hearing, reflection, and meditation. The nature of the Self cannot be known as an object; hence it is said here that no one, having heard of It, truly knows It. In the next verse, concluding this topic, the Lord says

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
Since the union and separation of the body are fixed by rule, and since the Self, being of the same nature as the Lord, is utterly indestructible, grief is not warranted -- to conclude this topic, the Lord again displays His divine capacity, with 'ascaryavat...' This means: due to rarity. That is a wonder in the world. Even though rare, since the Self shares the Lord's own nature and is subtle, a seer of the Self does exist.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Having shown the reproach directed at Arjuna, since the Self under discussion is difficult to know, reproach is not truly possible against him -- holding this view, he says 'durvijneya...' Thus, since the delusion caused by ignorance of the Self is common to all, there is no room for a special reproach -- this is said with 'kim tvam eva...' An objection is raised that the Self's being difficult to know is unestablished, since It is knowable through the notion of 'I' -- with 'katham...' Even though the notion of 'I' regarding the particular self is evident, since there is no such notion regarding the pure Self alone, there is indeed difficulty in knowing It -- showing this, the verse is introduced with 'aha...' By the first quarter, 'ascaryavat,' the rarity of seeing the Self is shown, indicating the rarity of the seer; by the second quarter, the rarity of knowledge of that subject is stated, indicating the same about the teacher; by the third quarter, the rarity of hearing about It indicates the scarcity of the hearer; and even where hearing, seeing, and speaking of It occur, the fourth quarter conveys that direct realization of that subject is attainable only with the utmost effort -- this is the division of meaning. He establishes the Self's difficulty of comprehension through the rarity of seeing It and so on, with 'ascaryavat...' Or, he states Its difficulty of comprehension by stating the rarity of the seer, speaker, hearer, and one who directly realizes the Self, with 'athava...' In both explanations, he states the resulting meaning with 'atah...'

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Someone sees this embodied Self as a wonder, and likewise someone else describes It as a wonder, and yet another hears of It as a wonder; and even having heard of It, no one knows It -- that is, this embodied Self is difficult to comprehend.

Explanation -- 'ascaryavat pasyati kascid enam' -- someone knows this Self as a wonder. The point is: unlike other things, which are seen, heard, read about, and known in the ordinary way, this Self is not known thus. Other things are known as objects ('this'), that is, they are the content of knowing; but this Self is not an object of the senses, mind, or intellect. It is known only by oneself, through oneself. Knowledge gained through oneself is not like worldly knowledge; rather, it is altogether extraordinary.

The word 'pasyati' has two meanings: seeing with the eyes, and knowing oneself through oneself. Here it refers to knowing oneself through oneself (cf. Gita 2.55, 6.20, etc.). Where seeing (knowing) occurs through instruments like the eyes, there is a threefold structure: the seer, the seen, and the seeing. All worldly seeing-knowing occurs through this triad. But in the knowledge of oneself, this triad does not exist -- that is, self-knowledge is independent of any instrument. Self-knowledge occurs only through oneself; the knowledge 'I am' requires no proof or instrument at all. This 'being-ness' cannot be seen objectively, as a visible thing; it is known only to oneself. This knowledge is not born of the senses or the intellect. Hence knowing oneself is like a wonder.

Just as a lamp needs no other light to be seen, since it is self-luminous, so too this embodied Self (one's true nature) is self-luminous and needs no other light to be known; it knows itself by itself alone.

There are three bodies -- gross, subtle, and causal. Examining all three, the gross body is not one's true nature since it constantly changes and is known as an object; the subtle body likewise changes and is known, so it too is not one's true nature; the causal body is of the nature of Prakriti, while the embodied Self is beyond Prakriti -- so the causal body too is not one's true nature. When this embodied Self, having set aside Prakriti, becomes established in its own nature, it knows itself by itself. This knowing is utterly unlike knowing worldly objects, and is therefore called 'seeing as a wonder.'

Here the Lord says that one who realizes oneself is rare ('kascit'), and later, in chapter 7 verse 3, He says the same thing -- that some rare person knows Him in truth ('kascin mam vetti tattvatah'). These words suggest that knowing this imperishable Truth is extremely difficult and rare.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:

The term 'ca' (and) implies that even amongst the seers, the speakers and hearers, one with authentic percepetion, authentic speech and authentic hearing, is a rarity.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
'This Self under discussion is inscrutable. Why should I blame you alone regarding a thing that is a source of delusion to all!' How is this Self inscrutable? [It may be argued that the Self is the object of egoism. The answer is: Although the individualized Self is the object of egoism, the absolute Self is not.] This is being answered in, 'Someone visualizes It as a wonder,' etc.
Kascit, someone; pasyati, visualizes; enam, It, the Self; ascaryavat, as a wonder, as though It were a wonder  a wonder is something not seen before, something strange, something seen all on a sudden; what is comparable to that is ascarya-vat; ca, and; tatha, similarly; eva, indeed; kascit, someone; anyah, else; vadati, talks of It as a wonder. And someone else srnoti, hears of It as a wonder. And someone, indeed, na, does not; veda, realize It; api, even; srutva, after hearing, seeing and speaking about It.
Or, (the meaning is) he who sees the Self is like a wonder. He who speaks of It and the who hears of It is indeed rare among many thousands. Therefore, the idea is that the Self is difficult to understand.
Now, in the course of concluding the topic under discussion, [viz the needlessness of sorrow and delusion,from the point of view of the nature of things.] He says, 'O descendant of Bharata, this embodied Self', etc.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
The verse 'ascaryavat' does not appear to have any connection with the current topic, and the following verse too seems to be a mere repetition -- to this, he replies with 'dehayoge...' This compound is of the dvandva-ekavadbhava type. 'Sarvatha,' meaning by every means, including the absence of destruction of the reflected image, is stated in order to conclude the topic through the following verse. This is said to conclude the discussion by resolving the doubt as to how, even while sharing the Lord's own nature, indestructibility is possible. The word 'punah' (again) is used because this was already stated earlier with 'aprameyasya' and so on. Thus, whoever sees It is 'ascaryavat' -- having said this, the fourth quarter provides its justification, which is explained with 'durlabhatvena...' Rarity alone is the reason for the use of the word 'ascarya' (wonder) -- this is the meaning. An objection arises: since everyone is a seer of the Self, how can a seer of the Self be rare, so as to be called a wonder? -- to this, he replies with 'durlabho'pi...' The seer of the Self is rare precisely because of sharing the Lord's own nature and the like -- this is the point.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
This Self is unmanifest, and hence It is like a wonder -- this is said with 'ascaryavat...' One sees It as though It were beyond reasoning, and so on.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Objection: Even learned people grieve; then why do you reproach me alone, again and again? In answer, he states the Self's difficulty of comprehension. This embodied Self appears like a wonder -- that is, due to nescience, It seems to possess many mutually contradictory attributes: though existent, It appears non-existent; though of the nature of self-luminous consciousness, It appears inert; though of the nature of dense bliss, It appears sorrowful; though changeless, It appears subject to change; though eternal, It appears transient; though ever-luminous, It appears non-luminous; though non-different from Brahman, It appears different; though liberated, It appears bound; though without a second, It appears to have a second -- thus It appears in a wondrous variety of contradictory forms. Through the instruction of scripture and teacher, by the negation of the entire nescience-born duality, the nature of the Supreme Self reflected in the modification of the inner faculty born of the great Vedantic sayings and resulting from all accumulated merit is directly realized, through the ripening of samadhi, only by some rare soul equipped with tranquility, self-control, and the other means, in his final body -- not by everyone. Likewise, whoever sees It, his very seeing is itself a wonder -- because the vision of the Self, though unreal in itself, reveals the Real; though born of nescience, it destroys nescience. He who sees It is himself like a wonder.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Then why do even learned people grieve in this world? Precisely because of ignorance of the Self -- with this intent, he states the Self's difficulty of comprehension, with 'ascaryavat...' Someone, perceiving this Self through the instruction of scripture and teacher, sees It as a wonder. Since the all-pervading Self, of the nature of eternal knowledge and bliss, is extraordinary, It is seen with astonishment, as though impossible like a magic trick, being overwhelmed by a sense of improbability. Likewise, another speaks of It as a wonder and hears of It as a wonder. Yet another, overwhelmed by a contrary conviction, does not know It even after hearing. The word 'ca' should also be understood to mean that one does not truly know It even after speaking of It.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Since the pure Self is difficult to comprehend, and since this delusion is common to all, it is not proper to reproach you alone -- this is said with 'ascaryavat...' 'Ascarya' means something wondrous, never seen before, appearing suddenly; that which resembles it is 'ascaryavat.' Someone sees this Self as a wonder. Likewise another speaks of It as a wonder. And yet another hears of It as a wonder. Having heard, spoken, and even seen It, someone, owing to some obstruction, does not directly know It. Or, he who sees this Self is himself comparable to a wonder; likewise he who speaks of It and he who hears of It is rare, one among many hundreds of thousands. And he who, having spoken and heard of It, gains indirect knowledge of It by also seeing It, is rarer still; but he who directly realizes It is rarer yet. Hence the Self is exceedingly difficult to comprehend -- this is the intent.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Thus, by concluding the argument of provisionally granting the opponent's position, he praises his own previously stated doctrine, showing that in a world sullied by the manifold evils rooted in the delusion of identifying the Self with the body, those qualified for this teaching are rare -- with 'evam...' This verse, 'ascaryavat' and so on, distinguished by a few words, is drawn straight from the Upanishads. The very distinctiveness stated earlier with 'avyakto'yam' and so on is now given as the reason for its being a wonder. To dispel the confusion of taking the word 'ascaryavat' as qualifying the verb 'pasyati' and the rest, it is said 'ascaryavad avasthitam' -- meaning that it is precisely the distinctiveness of the Self that is intended to be conveyed here. The word 'kascit' conveys a sense of selection, of rarity. 'Jantusu' is used to show the parity of unenlightened men with beasts. As stated elsewhere in the Mahabharata, it is only through the ripening of great austerity that the inclination toward knowledge arises. Since saying 'srutva api na veda' might raise the objection that this would render the very undertaking of scriptural study pointless, it is clarified as 'yathavad avasthitam tattvatah' -- meaning fully and authoritatively as It truly is, without any superimposition. The rarity intended here concerns only the knower of the ultimate Truth.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
If this is so, why do even learned people grieve out of fear of hell arising from sinful action? To this objection, he replies with 'ascaryavat...' Someone sees It as a wonder -- that is, even while knowing through scriptural understanding that It is eternally unmanifest and so on, upon seeing phenomena such as birth, he views It with wonder, as though it were an illusion wrought by Maya. These phenomena of birth and so forth in beings occur only by My will, for My own play -- hence it is called 'ascaryavat.' Likewise, deluded by My Maya, someone speaks of It as a wonder -- that is, instructs others about It. And another hearer, himself devoid of knowledge, hears of It as a wonder. Even having heard, and even having said 'this is indeed so,' no one truly knows It. Among these three, indeed, no one truly knows It -- hence they too grieve out of ignorance, this is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Objection: The phenomenal world of space and the rest, established by every means of valid knowledge and as firm as an adamantine cage, is asserted to be utterly unreal, born of ignorance like the rope mistaken for a snake -- how can this be? Or, how can the Self's agency in rites such as sacrifice, and its agency in hearing and the rest, required by the karma and jnana sections of scripture, be denied? To this, he says 'ascaryavat...' Some person who has known the truth of the Self sees the aggregate of beings described in the previous verse just as one sees a dream, an illusion, a magic trick -- that is, as a wonder. Likewise, another speaks of It as a wonder -- that is, though It cannot be described either as existent or non-existent, he expounds It as indescribable, in a manner unfamiliar to ordinary discourse. Just as this phenomenal world is like the rope-snake -- if it were real, it should not be contradicted by the scriptural statement 'here there is no diversity whatsoever,' and if it were unreal, it should not appear at all; hence it is indescribable. That the very ground of all worldly transactions should be shown to be unreal is itself utterly wondrous -- this is the meaning. Likewise, another hears of this phenomenal world as a wonder. That the phenomenal world -- these worlds, these gods, these Vedas, all that there is -- which is directly perceived as other than the Self, is also heard of as non-different from the Self, is itself utterly wondrous.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Some look on the soul as amazing, some describe him as amazing, and some hear of him as amazing, while others, even after hearing about him, cannot understand him at all.

 Since Gītopaniṣad is largely based on the principles of the Upaniṣads, it is not surprising to also find this passage in the Kaṭha Upaniṣad (1.2.7): śravaṇayāpi bahubhir yo na labhyaḥ śṛṇvanto ’pi bahavo yaṁ na vidyuḥ āścaryo vaktā kuśalo ’sya labdhā āścaryo ’sya jñātā kuśalānuśiṣṭaḥ The fact that the atomic soul is within the body of a gigantic animal, in the body of a gigantic banyan tree, and also in the microbic germs, millions and billions of which occupy only an inch of space, is certainly very amazing. Men with a poor fund of knowledge and men who are not austere cannot understand the wonders of the individual atomic spark of spirit, even though it is explained by the greatest authority of knowledge, who imparted lessons even to Brahmā, the first living being in the universe. Owing to a gross material conception of things, most men in this age cannot imagine how such a small particle can become both so great and so small. So men look at the soul proper as wonderful either by constitution or by description. Illusioned by the material energy, people are so engrossed in subject matters for sense gratification that they have very little time to understand the question of self-understanding, even though it is a fact that without this self-understanding all activities result in ultimate defeat in the struggle for existence. Perhaps they have no idea that one must think of the soul, and thus make a solution to the material miseries. Some people who are inclined to hear about the soul may be attending lectures, in good association, but sometimes, owing to ignorance, they are misguided by acceptance of the Supersoul and the atomic soul as one without distinction of magnitude. It is very difficult to find a man who perfectly understands the position of the Supersoul, the atomic soul, their respective functions and relationships and all other major and minor details. And it is still more difficult to find a man who has actually derived full benefit from knowledge of the soul, and who is able to describe the position of the soul in different aspects. But if, somehow or other, one is able to understand the subject matter of the soul, then one’s life is successful. The easiest process for understanding the subject matter of self, however, is to accept the statements of the Bhagavad-gītā spoken by the greatest authority, Lord Kṛṣṇa, without being deviated by other theories. But it also requires a great deal of penance and sacrifice, either in this life or in the previous ones, before one is able to accept Kṛṣṇa as the Supreme Personality of Godhead. Kṛṣṇa can, however, be known as such by the causeless mercy of the pure devotee and by no other way.
