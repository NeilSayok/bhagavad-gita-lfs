Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Arjuna, already bewildered, lacked -- as an ordinary man -- the subtle intellect needed to follow the Lord's subtle reasoning and determine with certainty whether the path of action or the path of knowledge was the easier way to the highest good. He therefore humbly requests the Lord to settle the matter and command him on the one path by which he may attain that supreme good. Arjuna had no doubt that life is not meant merely for earning, hoarding, and spending wealth; he knew his life was meant for a higher cultural and spiritual goal, for which material progress is only a means and not the end in itself. He merely wanted to know how best to use the situation at hand to fulfil life's true purpose and shape his future. In keeping with this question, the Lord now answers.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
This is a sub-commentary elaborating Sri Shankaracharya's bhashya on the verse. It takes up the view of some earlier commentators (the Vrittikaras) that a combination of Vedic and worldly (smarta) rites together constitutes the means to the highest good for householders, while for others the smarta rites alone suffice, and notes that the Lord's own words, and Arjuna's question, rule this out. It argues that if the Lord truly intended a combination (samuccaya) of knowledge and action, His reproach 'you appear to confuse my understanding' would make no sense, since one who teaches such a combination would have no reason to be charged with contradiction, and the question would not proceed as one demanding a single path. It further reasons that if the Lord's speech genuinely seems mixed to Arjuna, this cannot mean the Lord actually intends to bewilder him -- so the word 'iva' (as it were) is used, since a compassionate teacher's words are not really confusing, only apparently so to a limited listener. It resolves that if action and knowledge belong to persons of different qualification and cannot be practised simultaneously by one person, then the Lord is being asked to determine, according to Arjuna's own capacity, disposition and situation, which single one of the two -- knowledge or action -- is fit for him, so that by pursuing it he may attain the highest good. It concludes that had the Lord intended knowledge merely as a subordinate part of a life devoted to action, Arjuna would not have had occasion to ask for just one of the two, since the Lord never said He would teach only one and not both -- so the very form of Arjuna's question shows he understood the two paths as mutually exclusive alternives for a single person, not as a combination to be undertaken together.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Arjuna says: O Janardana (one who fulfils everyone's wish) -- since You consider knowledge superior to action, why then, O Keshava, do You engage me in this terrible action? With Your seemingly conflicting words You appear to bewilder my understanding; therefore, having determined it for certain, tell me that one thing by which I may attain the highest good.

Explanation: By addressing Krishna as 'Janardana', Arjuna implies that since the Lord fulfils the prayers of all, He will surely fulfil his prayer too. There is a common human weakness of wanting one's own view confirmed even while asking a question; yielding to whatever the teacher instructs, whether pleasant or unpleasant, is true courage, while everything else is a form of weakness. Because of this weakness a person finds it hard to bear what is unfavourable, and then dresses up that weakness as virtue. Here too, under the guise of the virtue of non-violence, Arjuna's abandonment of his duty has crept in, and so he is now rating knowledge above action, and asks: if action is inferior to knowledge in Your view, why then engage me in this dreadful action of war?

Earlier (2.39), the Lord had spoken of equanimity of intellect (samabuddhi) using the word 'buddhi', but Arjuna took it to mean 'knowledge' (jnana). So he says: You told me that this understanding was explained from the standpoint of Sankhya, and that I should now hear it with reference to Yoga -- that endowed with this understanding I would cast off the bondage of action; yet bondage is destroyed only through knowledge. You also said that action is far inferior to 'buddhi-yoga', that is, to knowledge (2.49). If, in Your view, knowledge is superior to action, then I should not be engaged even in scripturally enjoined good actions like sacrifice, charity and austerity, but only in knowledge -- yet, on the contrary, You are engaging me in a most cruel action like war, in which I must kill people the whole day. Why?

Earlier Arjuna, filled with the ardour to fight, had asked Krishna to place his chariot between the two armies so he could see who had come to oppose him; but when Krishna, standing the chariot before Bhishma, Drona and the assembled kings, said 'Behold these Kurus', Arjuna's attachment to his kinsmen awoke, and his inclination turned away from action and war towards knowledge, since knowledge does not require one to perform terrible acts like war. Hence Arjuna asks why he is being engaged in such dreadful action.

Here the word 'buddhi' is understood as 'knowledge' rather than 'equanimity', because if it meant equanimity the charge of 'mixed words' could not be sustained -- since in the second chapter (2.48) the Lord had already told Arjuna to act while established in yoga (equanimity). The charge of mixed, contradictory teaching is meaningful only if Arjuna understood the Lord to be presenting two distinct things; and later the Lord does in fact describe two distinct paths -- the path of knowledge for men of knowledge and the path of action-yoga for yogis -- which supports reading 'buddhi' here as 'knowledge'.

On 'vyamishreneva vakyena buddhim mohayasiva me': Arjuna's sense is -- sometimes You say 'perform actions' (2.48), and sometimes You say 'take refuge in understanding' (2.49); these seemingly mixed words are bewildering my intellect, so that I cannot clearly see whether I should act or take refuge in knowledge. The double use of the word 'iva' (as it were) shows Arjuna's underlying faith in the Lord -- he does not really believe the Lord is confusing him, only that, not having understood the Lord's words correctly, they appear mixed to him; for if the Lord Himself were confusing Arjuna's understanding, who then could remove his delusion?

On 'tadekam vada nishchitya yena shreyo'ham apnuyam': Arjuna asks the Lord to determine, once and for all, whether his good lies in action or in knowledge, and to state that one thing clearly for his welfare -- repeating the same request he had made earlier (2.7), 'tell me for certain that which is good for me'.

In the following three verses (3, 4 and 5) the Lord answers Arjuna's charge of 'mixed words'.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
If it be objected that the Lord Himself had already declared action superior, as in 'there is nothing more auspicious for a kshatriya than righteous war' (2.31), the answer is: even so, Your teaching is mixed, not one-pointed, and gives rise to doubt, since at one place You praise action and elsewhere You praise renunciation of action. Hence Arjuna asks the Lord to determine and state the one path -- by this he means 'You confuse my understanding, as it were; so having determined it, tell me that one thing for certain.'

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
An objection is raised: since the Lord deceives no one, and Arjuna is especially dear to Him, why does Arjuna see any sign of deception in the Lord's words? The answer given is that the Lord's speech is not truly mixed or contradictory; rather, because Arjuna, being of dull understanding, does not clearly grasp the Lord's intention -- namely, whether the teaching of both knowledge and action is meant for one and the same person or for persons of different qualification -- His words merely appear mixed to him, and thus the Lord seems, as it were, to bewilder his understanding, though out of supreme compassion the Lord does not truly do so; the confusion arises from Arjuna's own limitation, which is why the word 'iva' (as it were) is used. Since it is impossible for contradictory disciplines to be combined for a person of a single qualification, and since (having ruled out combination) an either-or choice is also inapplicable without knowing whether the qualifications differ, Arjuna asks the Lord to determine and state clearly which single one -- knowledge or action -- is his true qualification, so that by practising whichever one is prescribed and pursued, he may attain the highest good, liberation. Thus Arjuna's question is fundamentally about the difference of qualification (adhikara-bheda) between the path of knowledge and the path of action, not a request to choose arbitrarily between two applicable options. In response, the Lord explains that in this world there are two dispositions (nishtha), corresponding to the purity or impurity of the inner faculty (antahkarana) in two classes of people: for the Sankhyas -- those of purified mind who have taken renunciation from the stage of celibate discipleship and are firmly established in Vedantic realization -- the disposition is the path of knowledge (jnana-yoga); for those whose minds are not yet purified and who remain qualified for action (yogis), the disposition is the path of action (karma-yoga), which serves to purify the mind and thereby prepares them, in due course, to rise to the platform of knowledge. These two are not to be combined or treated as alternatives for the very same person at the very same stage; rather, they represent one underlying discipline expressed in two forms according to the stage of inner purity -- karma-yoga as the means to purify the mind, and jnana-yoga, culminating in renunciation of all action, as the fruit for those whose minds are already purified.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Anticipating the objection that the Lord had already declared action to be superior (as in 'there is nothing more auspicious for a kshatriya than righteous war'), Arjuna says: since at one place You praise action and at another You praise knowledge, Your words appear mixed and doubt-raising, swinging my mind between the two, as if bewildering it. Since You are supremely compassionate, You cannot truly be confusing me -- this apparent confusion is only due to my own limited understanding, hence the word 'as it were' (iva) is used. So please determine which of the two is truly good for me and tell me that one thing for certain -- or, alternatively, determine and tell me the one means by which, when practised, I may attain the highest good, liberation.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Anticipating the objection -- 'since it was You who already established from Your own words that action is superior for me, why do You ask again now?' -- Arjuna explains what he means by 'mixed words': at one place You praised action (as in the verse on being endowed with that understanding), and elsewhere You praised knowledge (as in 'the night of all beings'); at one place You taught that I am qualified for action alone ('you are qualified only for action'), and elsewhere You taught qualification for the path of knowledge ('the Vedas deal with the three gunas; be free of the three gunas, Arjuna'). With such mixed statements -- what is superior, what is inferior, where do I have a qualification and where not -- doubt naturally arises. Though the Lord speaks distinctly, to Arjuna's dull intellect His words appear mixed -- hence the word 'as it were' (iva); since the Lord has set out only to remove the confusion of Arjuna's understanding, how could He actually cause confusion? Hence Arjuna says, 'You confuse my understanding, as it were.' If the Lord holds that knowledge and action, being suited to persons of different qualification, cannot both be practised by one person, then Arjuna asks Him to determine and state which one -- knowledge or action -- is truly suited to him, so that by following whichever one is prescribed he may attain the highest good, liberation.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Explaining the reasoning behind this verse: since the intellect (buddhi) is insentient, speaking of it as 'confused' is figurative, hence Arjuna says 'confuses me'. The word 'as it were' (iva) conveys that it only 'appears so' -- out of compassion the Lord does not truly confuse Arjuna; rather, being of dull understanding, Arjuna himself is confused. The contradiction implied by the word 'mixed' (vyamishra) is twofold: how can action, being of the opposite nature to knowledge, serve as a means to it, and how can something opposed to knowledge be undertaken by one who seeks it -- these are the two contradictions Arjuna has in mind. The word 'one' (ekam) does not mean a choice between knowledge and action, since at this point only action has been taught; rather, since the teaching appears inconsistent to him, Arjuna is asking for a single, unmixed statement that removes his confusion. As for 'nishchitya' (having determined), it is not that the omniscient Lord's teaching was ever itself uncertain -- even a 'mixed' statement is only suspected of confusing the listener; it is Arjuna who seeks certainty for himself, so the sense is 'having determined for myself, may I attain the highest good', with what is to be practised remaining the point still in question.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Because he lacks clear understanding, Arjuna's intellect is bewildered, and he asks the Lord to instruct him plainly so that he may attain Him -- hence the mixed-seeming words: praising action at one place and knowledge at another give rise to doubt, and so, 'You confuse my understanding, as it were.' The Lord's words are not actually mixed or contradictory; it is only that living beings fail to understand them -- this is what the word 'as it were' (iva) indicates. Likewise, in 'mohayasi' the word 'iva' shows that real confusion is not fitting in the Lord's presence. Therefore Arjuna asks that, so his confusion may be dispelled, the Lord determine and, out of His willingness to bestow it, speak the one truly beneficial statement -- one that teaches devotion (bhakti) -- by which he may attain the Lord. None of the previously mixed statements about knowledge or action alone constitutes the highest good, since taken separately each can still confuse; only devotion is, in every way, the true means of attaining the Lord, as declared in the Bhagavatam (11.20.31) that for one endowed with devotion, that alone is the true good here. Hence the common reading that 'tell me the one of the two, knowledge or action' merely asks for a choice between them is not correct.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Anticipating the objection -- 'since you are not qualified for the path of knowledge, I am simply telling you to perform action' -- Arjuna says 'as if with mixed words'. 'Mixed' means not clearly distinguished; the word 'as it were' (iva) indicates that although the Lord's teaching may in fact be clear, Arjuna, owing to a fault in his own understanding, perceives it as unclear. By such statements, Krishna sometimes urges Arjuna to give up reliance on the Vedas ('the Vedas deal with the three gunas; be free of the three gunas, Arjuna'), and elsewhere has him hold to that very reliance ('you are qualified for action alone'); likewise He teaches the path of renunciation ('free from dualities, ever established in goodness, unconcerned with gain and safekeeping, be one who possesses the Self'), and also teaches the path of engagement ('there is nothing more auspicious for a kshatriya than righteous war'). But it is not possible for one person to practise both at once, like standing still and moving simultaneously. Hence, 'You confuse my understanding, as it were' -- though in truth the Lord has set out only to dispel Arjuna's confusion, which is why the word 'as it were' is used. So, of the two, Arjuna asks the Lord to determine which one is chiefly suited to him and to tell him that, so that by practising it he may attain the highest good.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
My intelligence is bewildered by Your equivocal instructions. Therefore, please tell me decisively which will be most beneficial for me.

 In the previous chapter, as a prelude to the Bhagavad-gītā , many different paths were explained, such as sāṅkhya-yoga, buddhi-yoga, control of the senses by intelligence, work without fruitive desire, and the position of the neophyte. This was all presented unsystematically. A more organized outline of the path would be necessary for action and understanding. Arjuna, therefore, wanted to clear up these apparently confusing matters so that any common man could accept them without misinterpretation. Although Kṛṣṇa had no intention of confusing Arjuna by any jugglery of words, Arjuna could not follow the process of Kṛṣṇa consciousness – either by inertia or by active service. In other words, by his questions he is clearing the path of Kṛṣṇa consciousness for all students who seriously want to understand the mystery of the Bhagavad-gītā .
