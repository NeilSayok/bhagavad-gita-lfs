Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
In all branches of science, it is accepted that an entity cannot be defined without describing its characteristics. The same is true regarding a disease or an emotion. Similarly, these Gunas cannot be defined in themselves. In the subsequent verses, their characteristics are described along with how an individual influenced by a particular Guna behaves when that Guna becomes predominant. Undoubtedly, this descriptive analysis will be very helpful for us seekers, because by observing and analyzing the thoughts and emotions arising in our minds, we will be able to determine which Guna dominates us at any given moment. Thus, we can strive to free ourselves from the bondage of that influence.

BEING PURE, SATTVA IS LUMINOUS — Just as water is naturally pure, but becomes impure when mixed with other substances, similarly Sattvaguna, being naturally pure, is illuminating, i.e., it clearly reflects Self-consciousness. It is free from the distraction of Rajoguna and the dense ignorance of Tamoguna.

ANAMAYAM — This word means free from trouble or defects. Ignorance of the Self and the resulting egoism and selfishness are the root defects from which all other evils arise. These defects arise from Rajoguna and Tamoguna. Therefore, it is said here that Sattvaguna is naturally free from these defects. Although Sattvaguna is pure, it is still binding. The bondages arising from it are indicated here.

SATTVA BINDS THE JIVA THROUGH ATTACHMENT TO HAPPINESS AND KNOWLEDGE — Not knowing its own joyful nature, the jiva constantly searches for happiness in sensory objects. This ignorance is the characteristic of Tamoguna, and the imagination of happiness in objects along with restlessness is the characteristic of Rajoguna. When a desired object is obtained through effort, the restlessness calms down for a moment. In that peaceful state, the bliss of the Self is expressed. But the jiva imagines that the happiness came from the object, and identifying with that happy mental state, says, 'I am happy.' Thus, although this happy state generated by sense-enjoyment is a property of the field (kshetra), mistaking it as one's own property and becoming attached to it is the bondage born of Sattvaguna.

An intellect dominated by Sattvaguna is naturally steady. Therefore, the light of Consciousness is clearly reflected in it. This reflected light is what we call the light of the intellect, through which we gain knowledge of objects. This is why due to the variation of this Guna, the intellectual capacity of all individuals is not equal.

Thus, illuminated by this light of the intellect, the thought of knowledge of an object arises in the mind. Man identifies with this thought and proudly says, 'I am the knower of this object.' Here too, there is identification with the property of the field, and this is the bondage of attachment to knowledge.

The simple meaning of both is that when a person experiences subtler happiness or knowledge, their mind becomes so deeply attached and absorbed in it that their attention is not easily drawn toward the subtlest reality. This is the bondage of Sattvaguna. This is a golden chain, but it is a chain nonetheless.

The Lord says that Sattvaguna binds through attachment to happiness and knowledge. Once a person experiences the sattvic bliss of creative thinking, noble conduct, and an inspired life of knowledge, they become so attached to it that they are ready to sacrifice everything for it. A true scientist working in a laboratory dedicating his life to science, a painter creating a painting in his studio despite being weakened by hunger and illness, a poet ostracized by society living in public parks absorbed in the joy of his imagination, feelings, and words, a patriot enduring cruel torture, a politician living a long life of exile, a mountaineer embracing death — all these are examples of men who experience sattvic joy and become attached to it, just as gross-minded people are attached to wealth and physical possessions.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
In response to the query as to what are the characteristics of the Guna and by what attachment it binds, the Lord says 'tatra' etc. Explaining the locative case used in the sense of selection, Anandgiri notes: 'among Sattva and other Gunas'. The repetition of 'tatra' is merely a restatement. 'Nirmalatvam' means purity, i.e., the capacity to ward off the veil of ignorance; hence it is 'prakasakam', that which manifests Consciousness, and 'anamayam', harmless or trouble-free. Being pure means it manifests joy. Asking 'Through what means does it bind the Self?', the question 'katham' (how?) is posed. Answering 'it binds through attachment to happiness', he elaborates upon it with 'I am happy' etc. The modification of Sattva that manifests primary happiness is here called object-born happiness. Clarifying the superimposition of connection, he says 'falsely indeed' (mrisaiwa). To the objection that attachment might be a real entity, he replies with 'that very nescience' etc. If it is argued that desire, attachment, and intent are one and the same, and since desire is an attribute of the Self, what need is there for nescience? He answers that desire etc. are attributes of the mind, not of the Self. To the query regarding proof that desire etc. belong to the non-Self, he cites 'desire etc. (belong to the field)'. Showing the resulting conclusion, he states 'therefore' etc. 'Sattva binds as it were' is the remainder. Explaining the use of 'iva' (as it were), he mentions 'through nescience'. In reality, there is no relation to the Self, yet due to lack of another relatum and lack of independence, it is treated as an attribute of the Self. The glory of nescience is such that it creates attachment in what is non-Self and non-attribute. He explains 'as if attached'. Presenting another mode of Sattva's binding nature, he says 'similarly'. Knowledge is that modification of Sattva through which things are known; through the inverted pride 'I am knowledgeable', Sattva binds the Self. Showing the defect in the counter-argument, he notes that if knowledge were an inherent attribute of the Self, liberation from bondage would be impossible. Attachment to knowledge and lordliness, which are properties of the field, is also created by nescience, just as with happiness. Addressing Arjuna as 'Anagha' indicates that only one free from sinful blemishes is qualified for this scripture.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O Sinless Arjuna! Of those gunas, Sattvaguna, being pure (stainless), is illuminating and flawless. It binds (the embodied soul) through attachment to happiness and attachment to knowledge.

Commentary —
Tatra sattvaṃ nirmalatvāt — In the previous verse, the three gunas — Sattva, Rajas, and Tamas — were mentioned. Among these three gunas, Sattvaguna is pure (free from impurity). This means that unlike Rajoguna and Tamoguna, there is no dirtiness in Sattvaguna; rather, it is pure and transparent compared to Rajoguna and Tamoguna. Being pure, it is helpful in revealing the knowledge of the Supreme Reality (Paramatma-tattva).

Prakasakam — Sattvaguna, being pure and clear, is illuminating. Just as objects are seen clearly under light, similarly, when Sattvaguna increases, the tendencies of Rajoguna and Tamoguna are clearly seen. The flaws arising from Rajoguna and Tamoguna, such as lust, anger, greed, pride, envy, etc., are also clearly perceived, meaning that a clear knowledge of all these modifications is obtained.

When Sattvaguna increases, light, awareness, and lightness are particularly felt in the senses, due to which the intellect functions fully in understanding every spiritual or worldly subject well, and there is great enthusiasm to act.

Sattvaguna has two forms: (1) Suddha Sattva (Pure Sattva), where the goal is the Supreme Lord, and (2) Malina Sattva (Impure Sattva), where the goal is worldly enjoyment and accumulation. In Pure Sattva, because the goal is the Supreme Lord, there is a natural inclination toward God. In Impure Sattva, because the goal is accumulation of objects and enjoyment of pleasures, there is inclination toward worldly activities, by which man gets bound.

Even in Impure Sattva, the intellect is capable of understanding worldly subjects well. For example, when Sattvaguna increases, a scientist makes new inventions, but because his goal is not God-realization, he remains bound to the world through ego, pride, fame, wealth, etc.

Anamayam — Sattvaguna is free from modification compared to Rajas and Tamas. In reality, being an effect of Prakriti, it is not completely changeless. Truly changeless is one's own Real Self or Paramatma-tattva, which is beyond the gunas (gunatita). Because it helps in attaining Paramatma-tattva, the Lord has called Sattvaguna flawless/changeless as well.

Sukhasangena badhnati jnanasangena canagha — When a sattvic thought arises in the inner instrument (antahkarana) and there is no flaw, a sense of happiness and peace is experienced. At that time, the seeker thinks: 'May such happiness remain forever, may such peace remain forever, may such stainlessness remain forever.' But when such happiness, peace, or stainlessness does not remain, the seeker feels distressed. This liking and disliking is attachment to the happiness of Sattvaguna, which is binding.

When the clear knowledge of the three gunas — Sattva, Rajas, and Tamas —, their modifications, and flaws arises, and the seeker comes to know many wonderful things never known before, the seeker thinks: 'May this knowledge remain forever.' This is attachment to knowledge, which is binding. The pride 'I know more (special) than others' is also binding.

In this way, Sattvaguna binds the seeker through attachment to happiness and knowledge, i.e., it does not allow him to become gunatita (beyond gunas). This attachment itself is Rajoguna, which binds (Gita 13.21). If the seeker does not form attachment to happiness and knowledge, Sattvaguna does not bind him; rather, it makes him gunatita. The point is that if there is no attachment, the seeker rises even above Sattvaguna and experiences his own Guna-transcendent nature.

When happiness and knowledge arise from Sattvaguna, the seeker should remain cautious that this happiness and knowledge are not my goal. These are not my ultimate attainment. They are merely instruments in attaining the goal. I have to attain that goal which illuminates even this happiness and knowledge.

Happiness, knowledge, etc., are all modifications of Sattvaguna. They sometimes decrease, sometimes increase, sometimes come, and sometimes go. But one's own Self is ever-steady and uniform. There is never any increase or decrease in it. Therefore, the seeker should always remain detached and neutral toward the modifications of Sattvaguna. He should not consume or indulge in them. Thus he will not fall into the trap of attachment to happiness and knowledge.

If the seeker does not attach himself to the happiness and knowledge arising from Sattvaguna, he quickly attains God. But even if he does not discard attachment immediately, by keeping the goal of God-realization, in due course of time a natural disinterest in this happiness and knowledge arises, and he attains God-realization.

Relation — The nature of Rajoguna and how it binds will be explained in the next verse.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Of 'these', i.e., of Sattva, Rajas and Tamas, the characteristic nature of the Sattva is this:  it illuminates on account of its being pure. What is called purity is to be bereft of alities which veil light and happiness. Because its nature is solely the generation of light and happiness, it constitutes the cause of light and happiness. 'Light' or illumination is enlightenment about a thing as it is. It is 'not morbid,' i.e., an effect called morbidity (disease) does not exist in its presence. The meaning is, that Sattva is the cause of health.

The Guna, called Sattva, however, binds the self by attachment to happiness and knowledge. The meaning is that it causes attachment to happiness and knowledge. When attachment to knowledge and happiness is born, one engages oneself in secular and Vedic means for securing them. Conseently, one is born in such bodies which constitute the means for realising such fruits. Hence the Sattva binds the self through attachment to happiness and knowledge. What is said is this:  Sattva generates knowledge and happiness; again it generates attachment to them.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Tatra, among them, among sattva etc.;-the characteristics of sattva itself is being stated first-sattva, nirmalatvat, being pure like a crystal stone;is prakasakam, an illuminator; and anamayam, harmless. Anagha, O sinless one; badhnati, it binds. How? Sukhasangena, through attachment to happiness. Bringing about the association of happiness, which is the object, with the Self, which is the subject, in the form of the idea, 'I am happy', is certainly an unreal contact with happiness. This as such is nescience, for the ality of an object cannot belong to a subject. And it has been said by the Lord that all the alities, from 'desire' to 'fortitude' (see 13.6), are, indeed, of the field, which is the object. Therefore, it is certainly through nescience, which is an attribute [In reality, though nescience has no connection with the Self, yet, since there is none other with which it can become associated and since it has no independence, therefore the Commentator imagines it as an attribute of the Self.] of the Self and has the characteristics of non-discrimination between object and subject, that sattva apparently brings about the association with happiness, which is not the Self. It makes (the Self) attached, as it were; [Here Ast. adds 'asangam saktam iva, (makes) the Unattached attached, as it were'.-Tr.] makes one not possessed of happiness as though possessed of it!
Similarly, it binds also jnana-sangena, through attachment to knowledge. [Jnana, derived in the sense of 'that through which one knows,' means an instrument of knowledge, and not Consciousness.
(S.: Knowledge arising from the study of the import of various scriptures; or, jnanam, means the scriptures, through which the supreme God is known and which leads to devotional practices, but not to steadfastness in (the absolute) Brahman.] Because of its concomitance with happiness, knowledge here is an attribute of the internal organ, the field, but not of the Self. Were it an attribute [If knowledge were a natural attribute of the Self, then there can be no estion of the latter again becoming bound through association with the former.] of the Self, there could be no contact (between it and the Self), and 'bondage' would become illogical. Association with knowledge etc. should be understood in the same sense as with happiness.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Stating in order the characteristics of the Gunas and their mode of binding, Vallabhacharya first explains Sattva — 'tatra' etc. Sattva, being material (prakrita), binds the individual soul through attachment to worldly happiness and attachment to knowledge; the meaning is that it generates attachment to happiness and knowledge in man. When attachment to knowledge and happiness arises, man engages in their means, both secular and Vedic; consequently, he is born in births (yonis) that serve as instruments for experiencing their fruits. Thus Sattva properly binds the person through those means. The same applies to the other Gunas. The characteristic feature is stated in the verse itself by 'nirmalatvat' (purity), and the characteristics of the other two are also clear in the text. Thus, material Sattva is binding by creating attachment to knowledge and happiness in the form 'I am happy, I am the knower of sound etc.'

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Stating which Guna among them binds through what attachment, Madhusudan Saraswati says 'tatra' etc. Among those Gunas, Sattva, being pure (transparent) because it removes the veil of Consciousness created by Tamoguna, is 'illuminating' (prakasakam); that is to say, it is capable of receiving the reflection of Consciousness (Chit). It is not only a manifestor of Consciousness, but also 'anamayam' (harmless/healthy). 'Amaya' means sorrow; hence it also manifests happiness which is opposed to sorrow. Such Sattva, O sinless one (anagha), binds the embodied soul through attachment to happiness and attachment to knowledge. Here, the terms 'happiness' and 'knowledge' denote those modifications of the internal organ (antahkarana) that manifest them. For in the verse 'Desire, hatred, pleasure, pain, aggregate, intelligence, fortitude...' (Gita 13.6), pleasure and consciousness are listed as attributes of the field (kshetra) just like desire. There, the superimposition of happiness and knowledge—which are attributes of the antahkarana—upon the Self is attachment, in the form 'I am happy', 'I know'. The property of the object can never belong to the subject; therefore, this is purely nescience (avidya), as has been stated hundreds of times before.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
There, Sridhara Swami explains the characteristic of Sattvaguna among the Gunas and its manner of binding — 'tatra' etc. Among those Gunas, Sattva, on account of being pure like a crystal, is illuminating (luminous) and harmless (anamayam, peaceful). Therefore, being peaceful, it binds by attachment to its effect, happiness. And being illuminating, it binds by attachment to its effect, knowledge. O sinless one (anagha), it connects the properties of the mind, such as 'I am happy, I am knowledgeable', to the embodied soul (Kshetrajna) who identifies with them.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
To the query as to what characteristic the Guna possesses and by what attachment it binds, Dhanpati says 'tatra' etc. Among those Gunas, Sattva, being pure like a crystal, is illuminating (manifesting Consciousness) and harmless. Such Sattva binds through attachment to happiness by falsely superimposition the objectified happiness upon the subject, the inner Self, in the form 'I am happy'. Only through nescience is the property of one superimposed upon another; 'desire etc. up to fortitude are properties of the field' as declared by the Lord. Therefore, through its own nescience consisting of non-discrimination between subject and object, it attaches the unattached Self as it were to non-Self happiness, making the unhappy appear happy. This is the glory of nescience that it creates attachment in what is non-Self and non-attribute. Showing another reason for Sattva's binding nature, he states 'and through attachment to knowledge'. Knowledge is the modification of Sattva through which things are known; Sattva binds the Self through the inverted egoism 'I am knowledgeable'. Due to its association with happiness, knowledge is also an attribute of the internal organ (kshetra), not of the Self; if it were an attribute of the Self, attachment and bondage would be illogical. Attachment to knowledge should be understood just like happiness. Addressing Arjuna as 'Anagha' (sinless) indicates that being free from vice, he is not fit for the bondage imposed by Sattva.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Explaining the context of the three verses beginning with 'tatra', Venkatanatha states that 'akara' means defining nature. The word 'tatra' is used in the sense of selection from the group, i.e., 'among Sattva, Rajas, and Tamas'. To the objection that pure crystals etc. are not seen to be luminous, he explains that 'mala' here refers to the nature opposed to light, which belongs to Tamas. Mention of happiness here is made keeping in view subsequent references. To the query that space and air, despite lacking a veiling nature, are not illuminating, he states that Sattva is solely of the nature of generating light and happiness. Light means true comprehension of an object as it is. Even in rajasika and tamasika intellects, the illumination of the underlying reality is due to the Sattva component. Free from morbidity means that the effect named morbidity (disease/flaw) does not exist in it, implying that Sattva is the cause of health. The third case ending indicates that Sattva causes attachment to happiness and knowledge in the individual soul, leading to engagement in secular and scriptural means, resulting in birth in appropriate bodies. The address 'Anagha' (sinless) implies that just as Arjuna previously declared 'I do not desire victory', he is free from attachment.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Purushottamji explains the mode of bondage along with the characteristics of the three Gunas — 'tatra' etc. Among the three Gunas, Sattva, on account of its purity (nirmalatvat)—being the cause of the maintenance of objects according to the divine will—is illuminating (prakasakam), i.e., capable of revealing the all-encompassing divine sport (bhagavad-ramana). It is harmless (anamayam), i.e., free from attachment and other flaws that obstruct divine service. Therefore, it binds through attachment to happiness, i.e., through attachment to the excellence of the body etc. which generate the bliss of devotional service to the Lord. And it binds through attachment to knowledge, i.e., by being instrumental in generating knowledge. Addressing Arjuna as 'Anagha' (sinless) indicates: 'Because you are blessed with My grace, there is no bondage for you.'

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Neelkantha explains which Guna binds through what attachment — 'tatra' etc. Among those Gunas, Sattva, being pure (free from the impurities of sorrow and delusion), is illuminating like light, revealing all objects. Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
O sinless one, the mode of goodness, being purer than the others, is illuminating, and it frees one from all sinful reactions. Those situated in that mode become conditioned by a sense of happiness and knowledge.

 The living entities conditioned by material nature are of various types. One is happy, another is very active, and another is helpless. All these types of psychological manifestations are causes of the entities’ conditioned status in nature. How they are differently conditioned is explained in this section of Bhagavad-gītā . The mode of goodness is first considered. The effect of developing the mode of goodness in the material world is that one becomes wiser than those otherwise conditioned. A man in the mode of goodness is not so much affected by material miseries, and he has a sense of advancement in material knowledge. The representative type is the brāhmaṇa, who is supposed to be situated in the mode of goodness. This sense of happiness is due to understanding that, in the mode of goodness, one is more or less free from sinful reactions. Actually, in the Vedic literature it is said that the mode of goodness means greater knowledge and a greater sense of happiness. The difficulty here is that when a living entity is situated in the mode of goodness he becomes conditioned to feel that he is advanced in knowledge and is better than others. In this way he becomes conditioned. The best examples are the scientist and the philosopher. Each is very proud of his knowledge, and because they generally improve their living conditions, they feel a sort of material happiness. This sense of advanced happiness in conditioned life makes them bound by the mode of goodness of material nature. As such, they are attracted toward working in the mode of goodness, and, as long as they have an attraction for working in that way, they have to take some type of body in the modes of nature. Thus there is no likelihood of liberation, or of being transferred to the spiritual world. Repeatedly one may become a philosopher, a scientist or a poet, and repeatedly become entangled in the same disadvantages of birth and death. But, due to the illusion of the material energy, one thinks that that sort of life is pleasant.
