Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
In the entire Gita, this is the only verse spoken by the old blind king Dhritarashtra; all the remaining verses are spoken by Sanjaya, who was narrating to Dhritarashtra the events preceding the war. Surely the old blind king Dhritarashtra was fully aware of the grave injustice done to his nephews, the Pandavas. He knew the relative strengths of the two armies and had full confidence in the might of his son's vast army. Even so, in his heart he felt the burden of guilt for his own misdeeds, and he was also doubtful about the final outcome of the war. He asks Sanjaya what happened at Kurukshetra. The sage Vedavyasa had granted Sanjaya such divine vision by which he could see and hear all the events taking place on the entire battlefield.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Having thus established that the Gita-shastra -- which deals with the twofold spiritual stance (nishtha) of the goal and the means, and which has both a higher and a lower subject-matter and purpose -- is fit to be expounded, and wishing to expound it, the teacher states the purport of a portion of it, namely the first chapter together with a part of the second. When the first verse of the first chapter of the Gita, which serves to show the connection of the narrative, stands thus, the construction of the words is: 'Dhritarashtra said.' Dhritarashtra, having only the eye of wisdom and lacking external sight, unable to perceive external objects directly, asks the one near him, his well-wishing counsellor Sanjaya: 'dharmakshetre...' That which is the field, i.e., the cause of the growth, of dharma and its increase is called Kurukshetra; assembled there and eager to fight -- some being mine (Duryodhana and others) and the others being the Pandavas (Yudhishthira and the rest) -- what did all of them, having gathered on the battlefield, set about doing; what did they do?

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Dhritarashtra said -- O Sanjaya! What did my sons and the sons of Pandu, gathered on the holy field of Kurukshetra desiring to fight, do?

Commentary -- 'Dharmakshetre,' 'Kurukshetre': In Kurukshetra the gods had performed sacrifices, and King Kuru too had done penance here. Because sacrificial and other righteous acts were performed here, and because it was King Kuru's land of penance, it is called the holy field, Kurukshetra.

By using the word 'kshetra' in both 'dharmakshetre' and 'kurukshetre,' Dhritarashtra's intent is that this is the land of his own Kuru dynasty. It is not merely a battlefield but also a place of pilgrimage, where beings can, even while alive, perform sacred acts and attain their welfare. Thinking that both worldly and otherworldly benefit might thus be gained, and only after taking the counsel of noble men, was this land chosen for the war.

In the world, quarrels generally arise over three things -- land, wealth and woman. Among these too, the fighting of kings among themselves is chiefly over land. The purport of using the word 'kurukshetre' here also lies in fighting over land. In the Kuru dynasty, the sons of Dhritarashtra and of Pandu become one; being of the Kuru line, both have an equal right in Kurukshetra, i.e., over King Kuru's land. Therefore (because the Kauravas would not give the Pandavas their land) both have come to fight for the land.

Although, since it is their own land, using the word 'kurukshetre' for both is reasonable and just, yet our eternal Vedic culture is so remarkable that whatever act is to be done is done keeping dharma in front. Even an act like war is performed in a holy land -- a place of pilgrimage -- so that those who die in the war may be uplifted and attain welfare. Hence here the word 'dharmakshetre' comes together with Kurukshetra.

Here, at the very beginning, the word 'dharma' reveals yet another thing. If 'dhar' is taken from the opening word 'dharma' and 'ma' from the word 'mama' in the last verse of the eighteenth chapter, the word 'dharma' is formed. So the whole Gita is contained within dharma -- that is, by practising dharma the principles of the Gita are fulfilled, and by performing one's duty according to the principles of the Gita, dharma is accomplished.

From these words 'dharmakshetre kurukshetre' all people should take this lesson: whatever work is to be done should be done keeping dharma in front. Every action should be done with a view to the welfare of all, not merely for one's own comfort; and regarding what ought and ought not to be done, one should keep the scriptures before oneself (Gita 16.24).

'Samaveta yuyutsavah': Even though the kings repeatedly proposed a treaty, Duryodhana did not agree to peace. Not only that, even when Lord Krishna asked him, my son Duryodhana declared plainly that without war he would not give the Pandavas even as much land as the point of a sharp needle. Then, compelled, the Pandavas too accepted war. Thus my sons and Pandu's sons, together with their armies, have assembled desiring to fight.

Though there was a desire for war in both armies, in Duryodhana the desire was especially strong. His chief aim was solely the gaining of the kingdom -- whether by dharma or adharma, by justice or injustice, by prescribed or forbidden means, we must get the kingdom by any means whatever -- such was his attitude. Therefore it was especially Duryodhana's side that was yuyutsu, desirous of war.

Among the Pandavas, dharma was foremost. Their attitude was: we shall sustain ourselves however we can, but we shall not let our dharma be obstructed, nor shall we act against dharma. On this account Maharaja Yudhishthira did not wish to fight. But because of the command of the mother by whose order Yudhishthira, with his four brothers, had married Draupadi, Maharaja Yudhishthira engaged in the war -- that is, only through the dharma of obeying his mother's command did Yudhishthira become desirous of war. The point is that Duryodhana and the others were yuyutsu for the sake of the kingdom, whereas the Pandavas became yuyutsu for the sake of dharma.

'Mamakah Pandavashchaiva': The Pandavas regarded Dhritarashtra as like a father (since he was their father's elder brother) and obeyed his commands. Even when Dhritarashtra gave improper commands, the Pandavas obeyed without considering right or wrong. So here the word 'mamakah' includes both the Kauravas and the Pandavas. Even so, the purpose of stating 'Pandavah' separately is that Dhritarashtra did not have equal feeling toward his own sons and toward Pandu's sons. He was partial; he had attachment to his own sons. He regarded Duryodhana and the others as his own, but did not regard the Pandavas as his own. For this reason he used 'mamakah' for his sons and 'Pandavah' for Pandu's sons; for the feelings within generally come out in speech. It was because of this divided feeling that Dhritarashtra had to suffer the destruction of his own lineage. From this every human being should learn not to keep a divided feeling -- 'these are mine, these are others' -- in their homes, neighbourhoods, villages, provinces, countries or sects. For from divided feeling there is no love or affection among one another, but rather quarrel.

The purpose of using 'eva' with 'Pandavah' here is this: the Pandavas are great, righteous souls; therefore they ought not to have fought. But they too came to the battlefield for war -- so, having come there, what did they do?

'Mamakah' and 'Pandavah': of these, Sanjaya will first give the answer regarding 'mamakah' from the second verse up to the thirteenth -- that your son Duryodhana, seeing the Pandava army, went to Dronacharya and, to create hostility toward the Pandavas in his mind, named their chief commanders; then Duryodhana named the chief warriors of his own army and praised their skill in battle. To please Duryodhana, Bhishma blew his conch loudly. Hearing it, the conches and other instruments resounded in the Kaurava army. Then, from the fourteenth verse up to the nineteenth, he will give the answer regarding 'Pandavah' -- that Sri Krishna of the Pandava side, seated in the chariot, blew his conch; after him Arjuna, Bhima, Yudhishthira, Nakula, Sahadeva and others blew their respective conches, by which the heart of Duryodhana's army was shaken. After that too, while still speaking of the Pandavas, Sanjaya, from the twentieth verse, will begin the occasion of the dialogue between Sri Krishna and Arjuna.

'Kimakurvata': The word 'kim' has three meanings -- alternative (vikalpa), censure (ninda, reproach), and question (prashna).

'Did the war happen or not?' -- such an alternative cannot be taken here, because the war had already gone on for ten days, and after Bhishma had been felled from his chariot, Sanjaya came to Hastinapura and is narrating those events to Dhritarashtra.

'What is this that my sons and Pandu's sons did, that they went and fought! They ought not to have fought' -- such censure or reproach also cannot be taken here, because the war was already going on, and within Dhritarashtra there was no intention of asking reproachfully. Here it is fitting to take the word 'kim' to mean a question. Dhritarashtra is asking only in order to know accurately, in sequence and in detail, all the events great and small from Sanjaya. Connection -- Sanjaya begins to give the answer to Dhritarashtra's question from the next verse onward.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
There, by 'You have grieved for those who should not be grieved for' (2.11) and so on, through the teaching of the means for removing all demoniac sins such as grief and delusion, it is taught that the supreme human goal is to be attained through the practice of one's own dharma -- this teaching of the Lord is universal, meant for all. And this narrative, in the form of the dialogue between the Lord and Arjuna, is for the praise of knowledge (vidya), like the dialogues of Janaka and Yajnavalkya and others in the Upanishads. It shows how even the renowned, great-souled Arjuna, his discernment and understanding overwhelmed by grief and delusion arising from the affection born of the notion 'I am theirs, these are mine' with regard to kingdom, teachers, sons, friends and the rest -- though himself engaged in war, the kshatriya's dharma -- desisted from that war, and set about performing another's dharma, such as living on alms, which is forbidden for a kshatriya. Thus he sank into a great calamity. And having obtained this knowledge through the Lord's teaching, having cast off grief and delusion, again engaging in his own dharma, he became one who had fulfilled his purpose. Thus this most excellent knowledge, serving a great purpose, is praised. And through the pretext of Arjuna, the person qualified for the teaching is shown. Even when engagement in one's own dharma had arisen, the grief and delusion that cause deviation from it are shown by Arjuna in 'How shall I in battle [fight against] Bhishma...' (2.4). Seeing that Arjuna's engagement in his own dharma called war came about even without discernment, its cause -- the activity of the enemy army -- is stated in 'But seeing the Pandava army...' (1.2). As an introduction to that comes Dhritarashtra's question to Sanjaya by the verse 'dharmakshetre' and so on. There, 'Dhritarashtra said' is the statement of Vaishampayana to Janamejaya. Having earlier heard of the many causes of the Pandavas' victory, Dhritarashtra, afraid of the loss of his sons' kingdom, asked, hoping [to hear] of a cause for his sons' victory. Though desirous of war, my people (Duryodhana and the rest) and the Pandavas (Yudhishthira and the rest), assembled in Kurukshetra -- what did they do? Did they merely fight according to the previously arisen desire for war, or, through some cause, did they do something else by the ceasing of that desire? The seen fear caused by heroic men like Bhishma and Arjuna, a cause for ceasing the desire to fight, is already well known; to show the unseen fear as well, he says 'dharmakshetre.' Kurukshetra is the field -- like a field for crops -- that is the cause of the origination of dharma not previously present and of the growth of dharma already present, and it is renowned in all shruti and smriti. As in the Jabala shruti: 'Brihaspati said to Yajnavalkya: since Kurukshetra is the sacrificial ground of the gods, the abode of Brahman for all beings'; and in the Shatapatha shruti: 'Kurukshetra is indeed the sacrificial ground of the gods.' The Pandavas, gone there and being already righteous, if they should turn back from the adharma occasioned by the mutual violence of the two sides, then my sons would obtain the kingdom; or else, by the greatness of the dharmakshetra, even my sinful sons might at some time gain clarity of mind, and then, repenting, if they should give back to the Pandavas the kingdom they had seized by deceit, then even without war my sons are as good as destroyed. Thus the great agitation of Dhritarashtra, who saw no sure means either for his sons' or for the Pandavas' gaining the kingdom, is itself the seed of the question. And the address 'Sanjaya' is meant to indicate: since you have thoroughly conquered faults like attachment and aversion, it should be told by you without any guile. Although the question could be managed merely by 'what did my people do,' yet by indicating 'and the Pandavas' separately, by displaying the absence of any sense of 'mine' toward the Pandavas, he reveals his hostility toward them.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
In this world, the supremely compassionate Lord, the son of Devaki, who descended for the welfare of all beings and whose feet are adored by all, rescued Arjuna -- who had lost his discernment through the grief and delusion born of ignorance of the truth, and who was inclined to abandon his own duty and take up another's -- from that ocean of grief and delusion by means of the boat of the secret teaching of dharma and knowledge. That very teaching imparted by the Lord, Krishna-Dvaipayana (Vyasa) set down in seven hundred verses. Therein he mostly recorded the verses that issued from Sri Krishna's own mouth, and composed some himself to link them. As stated in the Gita-Mahatmya: 'The Gita alone should be well sung; what need is there of other elaborate scriptures? -- for it issued from the lotus-mouth of Padmanabha himself.' There, from 'dharmakshetre' up to 'grieving, he said this,' the narrative is set forth to introduce the dialogue of Sri Krishna and Arjuna: 'dharmakshetre.' O Sanjaya, on the holy field of Kurukshetra, my sons and the sons of Pandu, having assembled together eager to fight, what did they do?

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Here indeed the supremely compassionate Lord Vasudeva -- whose very nature is perfect bliss, who is endowed with all lordship, and who assumed a body through his own maya (composed of the three gunas and brought under his control) -- desiring to uplift the multitude of living beings overwhelmed by grief and delusion, caused Arjuna, his own very form, to grasp the Gita-shastra, which is the essence of all the Vedas, consisting of three sections, and expounding the indivisible meaning of the phrase 'tat tvam [asi].' That very teaching, as it came in due sequence, the treasury of compassion Vedavyasa sets down in the Mahabharata: 'Dhritarashtra said,' and so on. There, Dhritarashtra's question -- 'O Sanjaya, whose warriors, delighted, are fighting there in the front? Who are high-spirited and who are wretched and confused? Who struck first there in that heart-shaking battle -- my people or the Pandavas? Tell me that, O Sanjaya,' and so on -- this question Vaishampayana, abridging it, restates to Janamejaya as an introduction: 'Dhritarashtra said.' Mamakah -- mine, Duryodhana and the rest; Pandavah -- Pandu's sons, Yudhishthira and the rest; yuyutsavah -- desirous of fighting. Because it is a place of the increase of dharma, having assembled in the dharmakshetra Kurukshetra -- renowned in shruti, smriti and the world -- what did they do? Whether they waged the dharma-yuddha (righteous war) which is their own duty, or an adharma-war, is indicated by the word 'dharmakshetra.' 'Assembled with the desire to fight' -- this I have heard in detail; thereafter, tell me in detail what and how they did -- this is the intent. Suggesting that, by the fall of Bhishma, the truth of the words of you and others, which indicated the calamity of the quarrel, has been fully borne out, he addresses him as 'Sanjaya.' Some say the address 'Sanjaya' is to indicate: since you have thoroughly conquered faults like attachment and aversion, you should tell it without guile. By 'kim' a reproach too is suggested -- meaning 'they did what was improper' -- because a war predominantly of violence is unfitting in a dharmakshetra. Though that impropriety is possible for my sons owing to their unrighteousness, indicating 'but what did the Pandavas -- Yudhishthira and the rest, renowned as supremely righteous -- do in felling Bhishma and others?', he says 'and the Pandavas.' Some say: by showing the absence of any sense of 'mine' toward the Pandavas, he reveals his hostility toward them. But the view of those who say -- 'Having earlier heard of the many causes of the Pandavas' victory, Dhritarashtra, afraid of the loss of his sons' kingdom, asked hoping for the cause of his sons' victory... the great agitation is the seed of the question' -- that should be set aside, because it contradicts the earlier text, which is of the nature of a brief statement: 'Then the wise Sanjaya, son of Gavalgana, a direct seer of all past, future and present, coming from the battle, suddenly approaching the meditating Dhritarashtra, sorrowful, announced that Bhishma, grandsire of the Bharatas, had been slain: "O great king, I am Sanjaya; salutations to you, O bull of the Bharatas. Bhishma the son of Shantanu, grandsire of the Bharatas, who protected the assembled army for ten nights, the destroyer of armies, has been slain; having done a most difficult deed, he has set like the sun; he who, unshakeable like Indra, raining thousands of arrows, slew in battle a crore of warriors in ten days, now lies slain on the ground like a tree shattered by the wind,"' and so on. If it be objected -- 'even though he had heard it in brief, did Dhritarashtra, forgetting through delusion, put the question?' -- no; because, in the manner we have stated, in conformity with the earlier text, the question is well established. It is rather those who explain the question in contradiction to the earlier text who are deluded -- so much by way of indication. And as for others who say -- 'the word dharmakshetra is meant so that from the word Kurukshetra there be no cognition of the Avimukta-kshetra' -- that is not correct; because, toward Sanjaya who came from Kurukshetra and is free of the doubt 'were they assembled in the Avimukta-kshetra?', such a qualifier is purposeless; and because for others too the determination already exists through worldly renown and the earlier text.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
'Dharmakshetre' means: in the field that is the very abode of dharma, fit for the sacrifice-like battle. 'Kurukshetre' means: since it is designated by the name of the common ancestor (Kuru) of both the Pandavas and the Dhartarashtras, it is an object of high regard for both parties. 'Yuyutsavah samavetah' means: arrayed in battle-formation facing one another as opponents. The two particles 'cha' and 'eva' add no special meaning; or, though the kings of the entire earth had gathered there, because of their common purpose only two sides were formed -- this is the import of 'eva'. The use of the middle-voice verb 'akurvata' indicates that the fruit of the action accrues to the doers themselves.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Vaishampayana, in order to relate to Janamejaya the connection of the narrative, first recounts the dialogue of Dhritarashtra. There Dhritarashtra, knowing the Pandavas to be devoted to dharma in many ways, asks with the intent: 'How did they commit the adharma marked by [the slaying of] kinsmen?' The order of the story here is thus: Sanjaya, having arrived, first speaks of the death of the commander (Bhishma); then, after Dhritarashtra has lamented over it and that lamentation has subsided, he narrates the whole story in detail. There [Dhritarashtra reflects]: the Pandavas' army was small, his own was large, and his own warriors were many and heroic; while all of them looked on, was Bhishma felled in battle because they neglected him, or was he forcibly slain by the Pandavas? And how did the Pandavas commit the adharma of slighting the grandsire in such a (holy) field? To know this, [he says]: 'O Sanjaya! In the dharmakshetra, the birthplace of dharma, in Kurukshetra, my sons and the sons of Pandu, eager to fight and assembled together, what did they do?'
Since his own sons were devoted to adharma, [the question about them is]: did they commit only adharma even in the dharmakshetra, or dharma? And since the Pandavas were devoted to dharma, [the question about them is]: how did they slay teachers like Drona in that dharmakshetra? This very devotion of theirs to dharma is indicated by the word 'cha.' And [the sense is]: even having ascertained that by a single death another gains the kingdom, what did they do? The address to Sanjaya is made in view of the omniscience he obtained through a boon.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
There, hearing of the preparation for war and eager out of curiosity to know the ensuing events, Dhritarashtra said: 'dharmakshetre.' In the Vedas there is one Kurukshetra, well known in the ritual portion as 'this Kurukshetra of the Kurus was the sacrificial ground of the gods'; and there is another Kurukshetra called Avimukta, the site for attaining Brahman, spoken of in the Sruti as 'Avimukta indeed is Kurukshetra, the sacrificial ground of the gods, the abode of Brahman for all beings.' Its being the abode of Brahman is established by the concluding statement, 'here, when the breaths of a creature are departing, Rudra expounds the liberating Brahman, by which one becomes immortal and freed.' To distinguish it from that, the qualifier 'dharmakshetre' is given. The Kurukshetra lying within the Kuru country is only a dharmakshetra (field of righteousness), not that abode of Brahman; for in the Pravargya section only its being a dharmakshetra is mentioned. Assembled there, eager to fight (what did my sons and Pandu's sons set about doing?). The separate mention of the Pandavas indicates the absence of any sense of 'mine-ness' toward them.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Dhṛtarāṣṭra said: O Sañjaya, after my sons and the sons of Pāṇḍu assembled in the place of pilgrimage at Kurukṣetra, desiring to fight, what did they do?

 Bhagavad-gītā is the widely read theistic science summarized in the Gītā-māhātmya ( Glorification of the Gītā ). There it says that one should read Bhagavad-gītā very scrutinizingly with the help of a person who is a devotee of Śrī Kṛṣṇa and try to understand it without personally motivated interpretations. The example of clear understanding is there in the Bhagavad-gītā itself, in the way the teaching is understood by Arjuna, who heard the Gītā directly from the Lord. If someone is fortunate enough to understand the Bhagavad-gītā in that line of disciplic succession, without motivated interpretation, then he surpasses all studies of Vedic wisdom, and all scriptures of the world. One will find in the Bhagavad-gītā all that is contained in other scriptures, but the reader will also find things which are not to be found elsewhere. That is the specific standard of the Gītā. It is the perfect theistic science because it is directly spoken by the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Lord Śrī Kṛṣṇa. The topics discussed by Dhṛtarāṣṭra and Sañjaya, as described in the Mahābhārata, form the basic principle for this great philosophy. It is understood that this philosophy evolved on the Battlefield of Kurukṣetra, which is a sacred place of pilgrimage from the immemorial time of the Vedic age. It was spoken by the Lord when He was present personally on this planet for the guidance of mankind. The word dharma-kṣetra (a place where religious rituals are performed) is significant because, on the Battlefield of Kurukṣetra, the Supreme Personality of Godhead was present on the side of Arjuna. Dhṛtarāṣṭra, the father of the Kurus, was highly doubtful about the possibility of his sons’ ultimate victory. In his doubt, he inquired from his secretary Sañjaya, “What did they do?” He was confident that both his sons and the sons of his younger brother Pāṇḍu were assembled in that Field of Kurukṣetra for a determined engagement of the war. Still, his inquiry is significant. He did not want a compromise between the cousins and brothers, and he wanted to be sure of the fate of his sons on the battlefield. Because the battle was arranged to be fought at Kurukṣetra, which is mentioned elsewhere in the Vedas as a place of worship – even for the denizens of heaven – Dhṛtarāṣṭra became very fearful about the influence of the holy place on the outcome of the battle. He knew very well that this would influence Arjuna and the sons of Pāṇḍu favorably, because by nature they were all virtuous. Sañjaya was a student of Vyāsa, and therefore, by the mercy of Vyāsa, Sañjaya was able to envision the Battlefield of Kurukṣetra even while he was in the room of Dhṛtarāṣṭra. And so, Dhṛtarāṣṭra asked him about the situation on the battlefield. Both the Pāṇḍavas and the sons of Dhṛtarāṣṭra belong to the same family, but Dhṛtarāṣṭra’s mind is disclosed herein. He deliberately claimed only his sons as Kurus, and he separated the sons of Pāṇḍu from the family heritage. One can thus understand the specific position of Dhṛtarāṣṭra in his relationship with his nephews, the sons of Pāṇḍu. As in the paddy field the unnecessary plants are taken out, so it is expected from the very beginning of these topics that in the religious field of Kurukṣetra, where the father of religion, Śrī Kṛṣṇa, was present, the unwanted plants like Dhṛtarāṣṭra’s son Duryodhana and others would be wiped out and the thoroughly religious persons, headed by Yudhiṣṭhira, would be established by the Lord. This is the significance of the words dharma-kṣetre and kuru-kṣetre, apart from their historical and Vedic importance.
