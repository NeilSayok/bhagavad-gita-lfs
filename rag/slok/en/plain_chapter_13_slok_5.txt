Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The discussion being presented in this chapter is not idle talk or a mere figment of Sri Krishna's imagination. Here the Lord Himself clearly states that He is reiterating the Truth experienced and propounded by the sages. In short, the exposition of the Brahman-principle as expounded in the Upanishads is the subject matter of this chapter. Someone might question why we should readily accept the statements of the Upanishadic sages. Such a question can only be raised by those who lack reverence for the seers. Lord Krishna says that even if one does not possess great reverence and respect for the sages, one must still accept the Truth propounded by them because they are the well-reasoned and conclusive statements of the Upanishads. Their declarations are not mere intellectual essays or divine commandments imposed upon helpless ordinary people by some messenger claiming special divine rights. When a Truth is proved through evidence, logic, and experience, any intelligent person must accept it due to its logical consistency. After inspiring interest in Arjuna's mind, the Lord says:

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:

Commentary -- Rishibhir bahudha gitam -- The seers, who were the revealers of Vedic mantras and authors of scriptures, Smritis, and Puranas, have described the field and the Knower of the field in great detail in their respective texts using terms like inert-conscious, real-unreal, body-embodied, eternal-transient, etc.
Chandobhir vividhaih prthak -- Here the term 'chandobhih' along with the adjective 'vividhaih' refers to the mantras of the Samhita and Brahmana portions of all four Vedas—Rig, Yajur, Sama, and Atharva. All Upanishads and various branches of the Vedas should also be understood within this. In these, the field and the Knower of the field have been described separately.
Brahmasutrapadais caiva hetumadbhir viniscitaih -- The principle of the field and its Knower has also been described through the aphorisms of the Brahma-sutras which are full of reasoning and conclusively established.
The Lord's intention in this verse appears to be that if anyone wishes to study in detail the principle of the field and the Knower of the field that I am describing in brief, they can consult the aforementioned scriptures.
Context -- Out of the six points regarding the field and the Knower of the field that were commanded to be heard in brief in the third verse, the Lord describes two aspects of the field—namely its nature and its modifications—in the next two verses.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
It is this truth regarding the Kestra and Ksetrajna that has been sung in various ways by Parasara and others seers. For example, 'I and you and others are composed of the elements; and the elements, following the stream of alities, assume a shape; these alities, Sattva and the rest, are dependent on Karma; and Karma, accumulated by nescience, influences the condition of all beings. The self is pure, imperishable, tranil, void of alities and is pre-eminent over Prakrti' (V. P., 2.13.69-71). Similarly:  'The body, characterised by head, hands, feet and the like is different from Purusa.' Which of these can I designate by the name I?'  (Ibid., 2.13.89). And also:  'Are you the head or the belly?  Are you indeed the feet and other limbs, or do they belong to you, O King?  You are distinct in your nature from all your members, O King. Know, O King, and understand "Who am I" '. (Ibid., 1.13.102-3).

Moreover they state that Vasudeva constitutes the Self of the distinct entities (Ksetra and Ksetrajna):  'The senses, Manas, Buddhi, vigour, splendour, strength, courage, both Ksetra and Ksetrajna have Vasudeva for their self. (Ma. Bha. Sa., 149.136).

In various distinctive hymns, namely, in the Vedas, Rg, Yajus, Saman and Atharvan, the distinction of body and the self has been sung. The nature of the body is described in the following text:  'From this Self, verily, ether arose; from the ether, air; from air, fire; from fire, water; from water, the earth; from the earth, herbs; from the herbs, food; from food, the person. The same person, verily, consists of the essence of food' (Tai. U., 2.1.2). Afterwards that which is inner than this (body) and which consists of Prana (or the vital breath), and that which is inner than this and which consists of mind are described. The nature of Ksetrjna is stated in the passage:  'Verily, other than, and within, that one that consists of mind, that (the individual Self) consists of understanding' (Ibid., 2.4.2). Later, the Supreme Brahman is stated in the text; 'Verily, other than, and within, that one consisting of understanding, is the Supreme Self that consists of bliss' (Ibid., 1.5.2). This is stated to be the Surpeme Self, consisting of bliss, as forming the inner Self of the individual self. Similarly in the three Vedas, Rg, Saman and Atharvan, here and there, the distinctive existence of the Ksetra and the Ksetrajna is affirmed with Brahman for their Self.

Likewise, the same purpose is taught in the words of the Brahma-sutras, namely, the aphorisms about Brahman, known also as the Sariraka-sutras, which are characterised by reasoning, decision and conclusion. In the Sutras commencing with, 'Not ether, on account of the absence of the Sruti' (B. S., 2.3.1), the nature and the mode of the Ksetra is determined. In the Sutras commencing with 'Not the self, on account of the Sruti and on account of the eternity, (which is made out) from them' (Ibid., 2.3.18), the true nature of the Ksetrajna is determined. In the Sutras 'But from the Supreme, this being declared by Sruti' (Ibid., 2.3.40), that Ksetrajna has the Lord for Its Self on account of Its being under the control of the Lord, is declared. It has been sung in various ways; the meaning of this Sloka is this:  Listen about the truths of the Ksetra and the Ksetrajna which have been expounded in numerous ways and declared by Me in a lucid and brief manner.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Anticipating the query as to by whom this summary was originally taught in detail, the Lord eulogizes the subject to create interest in the hearer: "Rishibhir..." By sages such as Vasistha and others, it has been described in many ways in Yoga scriptures as the object of concentration and meditation. By this, its propounding in Dharma-shastras is stated. By various hymns (chandas)—the Rik and other mantras and Brahmanas dealing with obligatory, occasional, and desired rites—it has been sung with distinct discrimination. By this, its propounding in the Karma-kanda (ritual section) is stated. "Brahma-sutra-padaih" -- Brahma-sutras are those Upanishadic sentences which indirectly indicate Brahman through indirect characteristics (tatastha-lakshana), such as "From where these beings are born..." Padani are sentences that directly express the essential nature (svarupa-lakshana) of Brahman, such as "Brahman is Truth, Knowledge, Infinite." By these Brahma-sutras and pads, which are rational (hetumadbhih)—as in the Chandogya passage starting with "In the beginning, my dear, this was Being alone, one without a second..." introducing the nihilist view and then refuting it with logic "How could Being arise from Non-being?"—and conclusive (viniscitaih), giving doubtful-free meaning through uniformity of beginning and end, it has been sung in many ways. By this, its propounding in the Jnana-kanda (knowledge section) is stated. Thus, the true nature of the field and the Knower of the field described in great detail by all these, I shall tell you in brief; listen to that—this is the meaning. Alternatively, "Brahma-sutras" and "pads" form a Karmadharaya compound: Vidya-sutras like "Let him meditate on It as the Self" and Avidya-sutras like "He knows not, he is like a beast", by these it has been sung.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
In response to the query as to by whom this summary was declared in detail, the Lord says: "Rishibhir..." By seers such as Vasistha and others, it has been described in many ways in Yoga scriptures as the object of meditation and concentration in forms such as Vairaja. By various wonderful hymns (chandas)—the Vedas—dealing with obligatory, occasional, and desire-driven rites, it has been sung in the forms of various worshipable deities. Also by aphorisms (sutras) and words (pads) indicative of Brahman. Brahma-sutras are those Upanishadic sentences expressing indirect characteristics (tatastha-lakshana), such as "From where these beings are born..." Similarly, pads are sentences expressing essential nature (svarupa-lakshana), such as "Brahman is Truth, Knowledge, Infinite," and by these it has been sung in many ways. Furthermore, by rational sentences (hetumadbhih), such as "In the beginning, my dear, this was Being alone... how could Being arise from Non-being?" and "Who indeed could breathe or live if this ether were not bliss? For He alone bestows bliss," which contain logical arguments ('anyat' means who could perform apana action, 'pranyat' means who could perform prana function). And conclusive sentences (viniscitaih), meaning those that give unambiguous meaning through consistency of preamble and conclusion. Thus, what was spoken by them in detail and difficult to comprehend, I shall declare to you in brief; listen to that. Alternatively, Brahma-sutras refers to aphorisms such as "Athato brahma-jijnasa", which are pads because Brahman is determined by them; by these, which are full of reasoning (e.g. "Ikshater nasabdam", "Anandamayo 'bhyasat") and conclusive in meaning, it has been sung. Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
By the verse beginning with "Rishibhir", the Lord states that the principle being taught by Him is established by numerous Srutis supported by Itihasas, Puranas, and Meemamsa. To determine the meaning of unclear Vedic passages following clear explanatory texts, he first mentions that it has been sung by seers. To exclude Rajasic and Tamasic explanations, he qualifies "seers" as "Parasara and others". "In various ways" means even though the subject is one, there are different modes of expression such as analogies of chariot wheels, rivers, etc., or in terms of brevity and detail. The statement "I, you..." refutes the idea that the king, who has not discriminated the nature of body and Self, is a carrier or carried. The answer to the question "Who are you?" rooted in hearing spiritual statements is "a body". The description "characterized by head, hands, etc." indicates the options of whole or partial consciousness. The phrase "Are you..." reinforces the presented truth in the hearer's own conviction. Thus, corroboration of what was stated in verse 13.2 ("This body...") is provided. An objection may be raised: In the mantra "Dva suparna...", "one eats the sweet fruit" refers to the Jiva enjoying karmic fruit, while "the other looks on without eating" refers to Paramatman as elaborated in Sariraka Sutras ("Guham pravishtau...", "Sthityadanabhyam ca"). But in Brahmanas, "one eats the sweet fruit refers to sattva" uses 'sattva' for Jiva, and "the other looks on without eating... is Kshetrajna" uses 'Kshetrajna' for Paramatman. The terms 'Kshetrajna' and 'Upadrashta' here are recognized as "tam prahuh kshetrajnah" (13.2) and "upadrashtanumanta" (13.23). Manu also says "He who causes this self to act is called Kshetrajna; but he who performs acts is called the Bhutatman by the wise" (Manu 12.12), using 'Kshetrajna' for Paramatman. How then is Kshetrajna said to be the Jiva here? Resolving this doubt, he introduces corroboration for "Know Me also as Kshetrajna" (13.3). Since usage of the word Kshetrajna is seen for the Jiva as well, Kshetrajna being the Jiva is valid. The Jiva is Kshetrajna because it knows the field ("this is my head, my hands, my body"). Paramatman is Kshetrajna because He knows the true nature of the field ("this body is for commencing karma, for enjoying karma fruit"). This is indicated in Ramanuja's Bhashya and Manu's statement. Similarly, being a witness (upadrashtritva) applies to Jiva regarding its own body, and to Paramatman regarding all conscious and unconscious entities; thus both being witnesses is uncontradicted. Therefore no scripture is contradicted. Since plurality of forms is gained by the plural "chandobhih", the word "vividhaih" refers to variety in the modes of presentation; hence he says "prthag-vidhaih". Based on usage like "Amnayas chandasam dandah", the word 'chandas' means Vedas, not metrics like Gayatri, hence he says "Rig, Yajur..."

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
That knowledge of the field of activities and of the knower of activities is described by various sages in various Vedic writings. It is especially presented in Vedānta-sūtra with all reasoning as to cause and effect.

 The Supreme Personality of Godhead, Kṛṣṇa, is the highest authority in explaining this knowledge. Still, as a matter of course, learned scholars and standard authorities always give evidence from previous authorities. Kṛṣṇa is explaining this most controversial point regarding the duality and nonduality of the soul and the Supersoul by referring to a scripture, the Vedānta, which is accepted as authority. First He says, “This is according to different sages.” As far as the sages are concerned, besides Himself, Vyāsadeva (the author of the Vedānta-sūtra ) is a great sage, and in the Vedānta-sūtra duality is perfectly explained. And Vyāsadeva’s father, Parāśara, is also a great sage, and he writes in his books of religiosity, aham tvaṁ ca tathānye.… “we – you, I and the various other living entities – are all transcendental, although in material bodies. Now we are fallen into the ways of the three modes of material nature according to our different karma. As such, some are on higher levels, and some are in the lower nature. The higher and lower natures exist due to ignorance and are being manifested in an infinite number of living entities. But the Supersoul, which is infallible, is uncontaminated by the three qualities of nature and is transcendental.” Similarly, in the original Vedas, a distinction between the soul, the Supersoul and the body is made, especially in the Kaṭha Upaniṣad. There are many great sages who have explained this, and Parāśara is considered principal among them. The word chandobhiḥ refers to the various Vedic literatures. The Taittirīya Upaniṣad, for example, which is a branch of the Yajur Veda, describes nature, the living entity and the Supreme Personality of Godhead. As stated before, kṣetra is the field of activities, and there are two kinds of kṣetra-jña: the individual living entity and the supreme living entity. As stated in the Taittirīya Upaniṣad (2.5), brahma pucchaṁ pratiṣṭhā. There is a manifestation of the Supreme Lord’s energy known as anna-maya, dependence upon food for existence. This is a materialistic realization of the Supreme. Then, in prāṇa-maya, after realizing the Supreme Absolute Truth in food, one can realize the Absolute Truth in the living symptoms or life forms. In jñāna-maya, realization extends beyond the living symptoms to the point of thinking, feeling and willing. Then there is Brahman realization, called vijñāna-maya, in which the living entity’s mind and life symptoms are distinguished from the living entity himself. The next and supreme stage is ānanda-maya, realization of the all-blissful nature. Thus there are five stages of Brahman realization, which are called brahma puccham. Out of these, the first three – anna-maya, prāṇa-maya and jñāna-maya – involve the fields of activities of the living entities. Transcendental to all these fields of activities is the Supreme Lord, who is called ānanda-maya. The Vedānta-sūtra also describes the Supreme by saying, ānanda-mayo ’bhyāsāt: the Supreme Personality of Godhead is by nature full of joy. To enjoy His transcendental bliss, He expands into vijñāna-maya, prāṇa-maya, jñāna-maya and anna-maya. In the field of activities the living entity is considered to be the enjoyer, and different from him is the ānanda-maya. That means that if the living entity decides to enjoy in dovetailing himself with the ānanda-maya, then he becomes perfect. This is the real picture of the Supreme Lord as the supreme knower of the field, the living entity as the subordinate knower, and the nature of the field of activities. One has to search for this truth in the Vedānta-sūtra, or Brahma-sūtra. It is mentioned here that the codes of the Brahma-sūtra are very nicely arranged according to cause and effect. Some of the sūtras, or aphorisms, are na viyad aśruteḥ (2.3.2), nātmā śruteḥ (2.3.18), and parāt tu tac-chruteḥ (2.3.40). The first aphorism indicates the field of activities, the second indicates the living entity, and the third indicates the Supreme Lord, the summum bonum among all the manifestations of various entities.
