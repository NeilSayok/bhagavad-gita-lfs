Life Application:
Accept physical aging and life transitions as natural changes, keeping your focus on the unchanging observer within.

Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:
As the soul experiences in this body infancy, youth and old age, so finally it passes into another. The wise have no delusion about this.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
It is a rule of memory that the one who experiences and the one who remembers must be the same person — only then does recollection of anything become possible. I cannot remember your experiences, nor can you remember mine, but each of us can remember our own experiences.

In old age we can remember our childhood and youth. When childhood ends, youth arrives, and after that, old age. Now it is clear that in old age a person no longer has either the childhood or the youthful state, and yet he can remember the experiences gained in those states. By the very law of memory, this proves that there is something in a person which remains unchanging through all three states — something which, through the child's and the young man's body, both receives experiences and also remembers them.

Seen in this light, it becomes clear that the death of childhood is the birth of youth, and the death of youth is itself the birth of old age. And yet these constant changes cause us no grief whatsoever — rather, passing through these states and gaining countless experiences, we remain joyful.

Using this very personal experience common to every person in the world as an illustration, Sri Krishna wishes to explain to Arjuna that the wise man does not grieve when the individual soul, leaving one body, enters another.

This verse further strengthens this truth hidden behind the doctrine of rebirth. Hence, for the wise man, there remains no fear of death whatsoever. When childhood and the other stages die, we do not grieve, because we know that our existence continues, and that we are attaining a higher state than the previous one. In the very same way, having abandoned one particular body, the individual soul takes on another body according to its prior tendencies (vasanas). In this matter, the wise man is not deluded.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
One might object: this teaching concerns the embodied one's continued existence, but that very existence is itself unproven. Not so: just as, despite the difference of bodies in childhood and the rest, the embodied one who witnesses them is established, so too in attaining another body, since the witnessing continues. For a non-conscious body cannot possibly experience childhood and the rest, since a dead body exhibits no such experience — the absence of experience in the dead follows from the departure of the vital breaths and the like. If it be objected that mere experiences like 'I am a man' do not prove this, since even in deep sleep, where the body is equally present, no such distinctive cognition is seen — one might reply that the ego-sense, being seated in the mind, is like that of wood and the like [i.e., a mere adjunct] — but this too is refuted, on the authority of scripture. And this scriptural authority stands on its own, exactly like perception, not merely on human say-so, since it is authorless. Human doubts, errors and the like cannot be supposed of what has no human author; and without accepting some such authorless text, none of the universally accepted matters of dharma and the like could ever be established, since anyone could otherwise refuse to accept it and undermine all common linguistic practice. This scriptural authority cannot be undone by mere clever argument (tarka) — hence the steadfast is not deluded by such sophistry. Or, alternatively: grief arises with reference to the destruction either of the living being itself or of the body. It is not the destruction of the living being, since it is eternal — this is stated in 'na tv eva...'; nor is it the destruction of the body — this is stated in 'dehinah...': just as there is no grief in losing the state of childhood upon attaining old age, so too there is no grief in losing a worn-out body upon attaining another.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
One might object: for one who abandons a former body and takes on a new one, would not the very fact of undergoing change imply the confused notion of origination and destruction? Anticipating this, he states an illustration, in 'Tatra...': that is, given that eternality has been established as the reason for not-to-be-grievability. Though there is a difference of states, since in reality no true modification occurs, the Self's eternality is thereby established — this is what the following verse teaches through the support of an illustration, is the sense. Not merely through scripture alone, but also because prior impressions carry over into a subsequent birth just as they do into another state, the Self's eternality is established, is the sense, in 'Dehinah...': possessing a body means being the object of the sense of 'I' and 'mine' with respect to it. The genitive 'tasam' (of these) is used in the sense of specification. Knowledge of the Self's eternality comes through scripture, tradition (smriti), and reasoning. In 'dhiman' (the wise), 'dhi' (intellect/wisdom) is what is intended. 'Evam sati' means: given that, in reality, no true modification occurs, eternality having thus been ascertained.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Just as in this human body of the embodied one there is childhood, youth and old age, so too there is the attainment of another body. In this matter the steadfast person is not deluded.

Explanation: 'Dehino'smin yatha dehe kaumaram yauvanam jara' — in this body of the embodied one, first childhood comes, then youth arrives, and then old age. The point is: the body never remains in one state; it is constantly changing. Here, by saying 'in this body of the embodied one,' it is established that the embodied one is distinct and the body is distinct. The embodied one is the seer, the body is the seen. Hence the change of states such as childhood in the body is not a change occurring in the embodied one.

'Tatha dehantara-praptih' — just as the body has states such as childhood, youth and so on, so too there is the attainment of another body. Just as the gross body changes from child to youth and from youth to old, and we feel no grief regarding this change of states, so too the embodied one goes from one body to another, and there ought to be no grief in this matter either. Just as while the gross body persists, states such as childhood and youth occur, so too while the subtle and causal bodies persist, the attainment of another body occurs — that is, just as childhood, youth and so on are states of the gross body, the attainment of another body (taking on a new body after death) is a state of the subtle and causal bodies.

That the change of states such as childhood occurs while the gross body persists is a gross-level view. Seen subtly, change occurs in the gross body constantly, just as in the states — the body that existed in childhood is not the same in youth. In truth, there is no moment at all in which the gross body is not changing. Likewise, the subtle and causal bodies too change every moment, which becomes clearly visible in the form of the attainment of another body.

We have knowledge of the gross body, but we have no knowledge of the subtle and causal bodies. So when we have no knowledge even of the subtle and causal bodies, how can we have knowledge of their change? The answer is: just as knowledge of the gross body comes through its states, so too knowledge of the subtle and causal bodies comes through their states. The waking state is attributed to the gross body, the dream state to the subtle body, and deep sleep to the causal body. In childhood a person, in dream, sees himself as a child; in youth, sees himself as a youth in dream; and in old age, sees himself as old in dream. This proves that the subtle body changes along with the gross body. Likewise, deep sleep occurs more in childhood, less in youth, and very little in old age — this proves the change of the causal body as well. Further, the freshness that comes to the body and senses after sleep in childhood and youth does not come after sleep in old age — that is, in old age one does not get the rest one gets in childhood and youth. This too proves the change of the causal body.

One who obtains the body of another being — a god, animal, bird and so on — experiences, on account of identification with that body, 'I am this' — this is a change of the subtle body. Likewise, in the causal body resides the disposition (svabhava, or prakriti), which, from the gross standpoint, is called habit. That habit is godly in one case, and animal- or bird-like in another — this is a change of the causal body.

If the embodied one (the deha) itself changed, then even after the states changed, there would be no knowledge of 'I am the same.' But even after the states change, there is the knowledge: 'the one who was formerly a child, then a youth, is the very same I who am now.' This proves that no change has occurred in the embodied one, that is, in the Self.

A doubt may arise here: when the states of the gross body change, we have knowledge of it, but why do we have no knowledge of the previous body when another body is attained? The reason for not having knowledge of the previous body is that at the time of death and birth there is very great suffering. Because of that suffering, the memory of the previous birth does not remain in the intellect. Just as, when paralysis strikes, or in extreme old age, the intellect no longer retains knowledge as before, so too, at the time of death and birth, when a very great shock is received, knowledge of the previous birth does not remain. But one whose death involves no such suffering — that is, one who attains another body as effortlessly as one attains a change of bodily state — may retain in the intellect the memory of the previous birth.

Now let us consider: the kind of knowledge that occurs in attaining another state does not occur in attaining another body; but the knowledge of one's own existence in the form 'I am' remains with everyone. Just as in deep sleep one has no knowledge of anything, yet on waking a person says, 'such deep sleep came that I knew nothing at all' — the very knowledge that 'I knew nothing' is itself present. The one I was before sleeping is the very same I am after waking; so even during deep sleep I was the same — thus the knowledge of one's own existence continues unbroken, uninterrupted. No one ever has knowledge of the absence of their own existence. The existence of the embodied one remains unbroken — it is precisely because of this that liberation occurs, and in the liberated state that existence continues. Yes, in the state of liberation-while-living, one may have no knowledge of other bodies, but the experience 'I am distinct from all three bodies' certainly remains.

'Dhiras tatra na muhyati' — the steadfast (dhira) is one who has attained discrimination between the real and the unreal. Such a steadfast person is never deluded in this matter, never has any doubt. This does not mean that the steadfast person attains another body. The cause of birth in higher and lower species is association with the gunas, and once the connection with the gunas is severed, the steadfast person cannot attain another body at all.

Here the word 'tatra' does not mean 'in the matter of attaining another body,' but rather 'in the matter of the body and the embodied one.' The point is: what is the body? What is the embodied one? What is changing? What is unchanging? What is impermanent, what is permanent? What is unreal, what is real? What is subject to modification, what is not? — in this matter he is not deluded. That the body and the embodied one are entirely distinct — in this matter he is never deluded. He retains an unbroken knowledge of his own non-attachment.

Transition: he now speaks, for the removal of the grief that arises regarding impermanent things such as the body—

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Just as the embodied one, existing in one body, does not grieve, through the steadfast conviction of the Self's persistence, upon abandoning childhood and attaining youth and the other states — thinking 'the Self has perished' — so too, upon attaining another body from this one, the wise person, convinced in the very same way that the Self remains steadfast, does not grieve. Hence, the Self being eternal, the Self is no ground for grief. This alone is to be done here: for the eternal Selves, bound by beginningless karma and thus in contact with bodies suited to their respective actions, who perform, through those very bodies, the scripturally enjoined action proper to their own class — such as battle — undertaken without desire for its fruit, for the sake of freedom from bondage, the contacts of the senses with their objects, which unavoidably yield pleasure and pain caused by heat, cold and the like, are to be patiently endured until the completion of the scripturally enjoined action. Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
'Dehin' means one possessing a body — of the embodied Self. Just as, in this present body, there occur three mutually distinct states — childhood (the state of boyhood), youth (the state of a young man), and old age (the state of decline) — and just as the destruction of the first state is not the destruction of the Self, nor the arising of the second state its birth, but rather the very same unchanging Self is seen to attain the second and third states, so too, in the very same way, the attainment of another body, distinct from the present body — this too belongs to the very same unchanging Self. The steadfast, the wise one, in this matter, does not fall into delusion — does not become deluded.

Although delusion caused by fear of the Self's destruction cannot arise in one who truly knows the Self to be eternal, nevertheless worldly delusion caused by the experience of heat, cold, pleasure and pain is indeed observed — delusion caused by separation from pleasure, and grief caused by contact with pain. Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
[Condensed summary of an unusually long Nyaya-style epistemological digression.] The word 'adi' (and so on) indicates that the Self is established also as the ground of desire, aversion, and effort, not merely of knowledge; and since knowledge and the like are absent in a corpse (their effects not being seen there), a Self distinct from the body is inferred as their substrate. By this, both the theory that the assembled body itself is conscious and the theory that consciousness is a function of the vital breaths are refuted. The claim that the mind is the perceiver is likewise refuted by analogy: the mind's alleged perceivership fails just as the body's does, since even when the mind is present, in deep sleep no cognition occurs. Should one object that the Self would then also fail to be a knower, the answer is that this is because the Self, then, lacks any contact with the mind — whereas when it does have such contact, the Self, and only the Self, is thereby established as the knower. A further defect is shown in ascribing knowership to the mind: the mind is a mere instrument of knowledge, not its agent, just as firewood is the instrument, not the agent, of cooking, or an axe of cutting — for the direct experience 'I know by the mind' shows the mind is merely the means, and this same reasoning refutes the theory that the Self becomes a knower simply by mind-contact rather than being an independent conscious agent. Similarly, sense-organs are proven mere instruments, not perceivers themselves, from experiences like 'I see with the eye.' The theory that consciousness belongs to the object of cognition itself is clearly untenable, given experiences of memory and other cognitions of past objects that are no longer present. Thus by process of elimination, a Self distinct from the body is established. Against one who might argue that a felt desire for food and the like arises spontaneously from the body's own nature, so that no separate 'recognition' proves a persisting Self, he adduces a further, scriptural proof of the Self's eternality: the Aitareya Upanishad's statement, 'having risen up from this body, having reached that world yonder,' establishes an eternal Self beyond the body. Anticipating the objection that this scriptural citation carries no force for the Buddhist or Charvaka materialist, who does not accept the authority of the Veda, he undertakes an extended defense of the Veda's authority: even the Buddhist, who accepts only perception and inference, and the Charvaka, who accepts only perception, must explain why perception itself is authoritative -- and whatever answer is given (e.g., that it reveals its object) applies equally to testimony (shabda-pramana), which should likewise be accepted. If one objects that testimony is sometimes seen to be erroneous (as with the flawed statements of rival schools), so that scripture too might be suspect, the same objection would equally undermine the general reliability of perception and inference (since these too are sometimes mistaken) -- yet no one abandons them wholesale on that account. The defects that afflict ordinary human speech (falsehood, deception, ignorance) all trace back to a flawed human speaker; the Veda, however, being authorless (apaurusheya), has no such speaker in whom these defects could be grounded, and so cannot be impugned on these grounds. Nor can one hold that no speech at all can be authorless, for then the very validity of dharma and adharma -- accepted implicitly and universally by all people, down to cowherds -- would collapse for lack of any means of establishing them, since neither perception nor inference can directly grasp dharma. This universal, unrefuted conviction regarding the reality of dharma and adharma is itself a form of valid cognition, requiring an authoritative source; and that source can only be a beginningless, authorless scriptural testimony, since ordinary verbal transactions between people (which presuppose that words reliably convey a speaker's intended meaning) would themselves become impossible to account for if testimony and inference were denied any validity whatsoever. As for the Buddhist claim that their own scriptures too are authorless: this is refuted since the founders of these traditions (such as Buddha, son of Shuddhodana) are historically known, whereas no author of the Veda is ever known or remembered by anyone, and the Veda alone is universally regarded, since beginningless time, as having always simply been received, never composed -- hence its authorlessness is uniquely and firmly established, unlike that claimed for rival scriptures. Having thus established the Veda's authority and, through it, the eternal Self's existence, he concludes: it is precisely because these various sophistical arguments (kutarka) of the no-Self (nairatmya-vada, i.e. Buddhist) theorists have all now been refuted that the wise, steadfast person is not deluded regarding the existence of a Self beyond the body. Arjuna's own earlier statement about certain dwelling in hell (1.44) is itself indirect proof he already presupposes an eternal Self who could so dwell -- hence the third-person description here. Finally, offering an alternative reading of the whole illustration for the second half of the verse: just as there is no grief in losing the state of childhood because a better state, youth, is thereby gained, so here too there should be no grief even at death, since a new body is thereby attained; and just as it is far more fitting to feel no grief upon exchanging an inferior state for a better one than merely upon exchanging one state for another of the same order (as when one exchanges mere straw for a fine cloth) -- so, since here what is lost is not merely a state but an entire embodied condition, being replaced by another, there is all the more reason for the steadfast to feel no grief.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Having thus established the not-to-be-grievability of the Self through its birthlessness, he now states the not-to-be-grievability of the body and the rest, in 'Dehinah...': through a worldly illustration — just as in this gross body the attainment of states such as childhood occurs, so too the attainment of another body of the Selves is fixed and certain; hence the steadfast is not deluded, does not grieve over it.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
The materialists (Lokayatikas) hold that the body alone, endowed with consciousness, is the Self — hence the authority of direct perceptions such as 'I am stout,' 'I am fair,' 'I am walking' would remain unrefuted, and how then could the Self be distinct from the body? And even granting distinctness, how could it be free of birth and destruction, since we perceive 'Devadatta was born' and 'Devadatta died,' implying that the Self too is born and destroyed along with the body's birth and destruction? Anticipating this, he says: 'dehin' means one to whom belong all the bodies — past, present, and future, throughout the entire universe — since, being all-pervading, the one Self is connected with every body, and its activity everywhere is thereby accounted for; hence there is no proof of a distinct Self for each separate body — the singular number here suggests this. The plural 'sarve vayam' (all of us) used earlier refers merely to the plurality of bodies, not to any plurality of the Self — hence no fault. For this one and same embodied being, in this present body, the three mutually contrary states — childhood, youth, old age — occur, yet the Self is not thereby differentiated by them, since there is the firm recognition, 'I who experienced my parents in childhood am the very same I who now experience my grandsons in old age' — and since an impression formed in one context does not give rise to recollection in an unrelated context. In the very same way, the attainment of another body — a body utterly unlike this one — occurs to the very same unchanging Self; even in dream and in yogic powers, despite the apprehension of a different body, there is the very same recognition, 'I am the same.' Thus, if the body itself were the Self, then, upon the body's differentiation through childhood and the rest, no such re-cognition would be possible; and even if one argues, by the maxim 'the substance persists throughout re-cognition despite the disparity of childhood and other states,' for a unity of the states-possessing body itself, this too would fail to account for the re-cognition occurring across the very different bodies of dream and yogic experience. Hence, just as the cognition of water in a mirage is necessarily an error, so too the cognition 'I am stout' and the like must be admitted as illusory, the grounds for sublation being equally present in both. This is what shall be elaborated later, at 'na jayate...' (2.20). By this very reasoning, the theory that the Self, though distinct from the body, is born and destroyed along with it, is also refuted — since, though there is re-cognition across differing states, there is no re-cognition across differing substrates (i.e., differing bodies as such). Or, alternatively: just as the attainment of states such as childhood belongs to the very same unchanging Self, so too the attainment of another body belongs to the same Self at the moment of departure from this one — for even without the recognition 'I am the same,' it is observed that a newborn immediately displays joy, fear and the like arising from prior impressions; otherwise, its instinctive inclination toward nursing at the breast would be inexplicable, since such inclination is known to arise only from knowledge of its being a means to a desired end, or from unseen prior merit (adrishta) alone. Hence the identity of the Self across the former and latter bodies is established; otherwise the fault of the loss of what was earned or the appearance of what was not earned would arise. Or again: just as, for you, the one embodied being, there is no real difference between the successive origination and destruction of bodily states — since you are eternal — so too there is, for you, the one all-pervading Self, no difference in the simultaneous attainment of all other bodies as well; for a Self of intermediate size would, being composed of parts, be incompatible with eternality; and if atomic, the impossibility of experiencing pleasure and the like pervading the whole body would follow; hence, pervasiveness being settled, and its effects being observed everywhere, it is settled that you alone are the one Self in all bodies. This being so, only through the false imagination of a distinction between slayer and slain do you, lacking steadfastness, fall into delusion; the wise, however, being learned, do not, since they perceive no such distinction as 'I am their slayer, they are to be slain by me.' Hence all bodies, being the subject of dispute, are enjoyed by one and the same experiencer, since they are bodies, just like your own body. Scripture too says: 'the one God, hidden in all beings, all-pervading, the inner Self of all beings' and the like. By this, the various rival views — that the body alone is the Self (the Charvakas); that the senses, mind and vital breath constitute the Self (one branch of them); that consciousness is momentary (the Buddhists); that the Self, distinct from the body, is fixed and of the body's own size (the Digambara Jains); and that, since a middling size cannot be eternal, the Self must be atomic and eternal (another school) — are all refuted, eternality and all-pervasiveness having now been established.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
One might object: your, the Lord's, freedom from birth and the rest is indeed true, but for ordinary souls, birth and death are well-attested — to this he says, in 'Dehinah...': just as, for the embodied one, the soul identified with the body, the states of childhood and the rest in this gross body are rooted in the body alone, not in the Self as such — since, even upon the destruction of a former state and the arising of another, there is recognition 'I am the same' — so too, upon the destruction of this body, the attainment of another body is likewise rooted in the subtle body alone, not thereby constituting any destruction of the Self. This is evident since even a newborn shows, through prior impressions, an inclination toward nursing at the breast and the like. Hence the steadfast, the wise one, is not deluded regarding either the destruction or the arising of these two bodies — he does not think that the Self itself has died or been born.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
There, he states an illustration, in 'Dehinah...': 'dehin' means one possessing a body, the Self. Just as, for one and the same person, there is a triad of states in this body, so too there is the attainment of another body — in this matter, the wise, one possessed of knowledge of the Self's eternality, is not deluded, does not fall into confusion. One might object: is it not a deficiency in the commentators that they have not raised and examined here the various rival theories, as elsewhere? Not so — since the remaining portion of the text ('the wise do not grieve,' 'the steadfast is not deluded,' 'endure them, O Bharata,' 'these do not afflict him') makes clear that the explanation of the Self's eternality is here intended purely for the removal of grief, in accordance with the plain sense of the words; since nowhere is it stated that one who holds the Self to be non-eternal is thereby a fool; since the refutation of rival theories is already accomplished implicitly through this very account of the Self's eternality; and since, in the Sharirakabhashya, these false doctrines have already been refuted on their own — for these reasons, the absence of such an examination here is no deficiency at all.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
The Self's eternality was stated so that it should not be grieved for. Then how, given the Self's eternality, do the experiences and transactions of birth, death and the like arise? This doubt he resolves through an illustration: 'dehinah.' The word 'asmin' (in this) means 'in one and the same' body. Since the connection with the three states of the body is itself the illustration, it is fitting that the illustration apply to the embodied one alone, in the body as it presently exists. The sequential mention 'kaumaram, yauvanam, jara' brings out that the nature intended is expressed by 'having abandoned.' Since the abandonment of both childhood and youth is also a cause of a particular kind of grief, the word 'dhirah' (the steadfast one) is used to exclude that, and to make explicit the intended meaning of 'yatha' (just as): the notion of the Self's fixity, even upon the cessation of the state of childhood, is the very reason for the absence of grief over its supposed destruction — the same holds here too. In 'dehantarapraptih,' the word 'antara' (other) points to what would occasion grief, namely the abandonment of the former body. The word 'dhira,' specific to this context, means 'one whose understanding is that the Self is fixed.' The connection with the earlier-stated 'asochyan' (not to be grieved for) is made via 'atah' (hence). Thus, across these two verses, what is to be attained and what is to be relinquished have been indicated in due order. Now, gathering together what is implicit here and what results from the next two verses concerning the means of attainment, he sums it up as 'etavat' (this much) down to 'bandhanivrittaye' (for freedom from bondage) — this being the sense of 'amritatvaya kalpate' (2.15). To forestall the objection that beings of pure nature could not be subject to worldly existence, the word 'anadi' (beginningless) is used — the point being that it is due to subjection to karma, not to some inexplicable subjection to ignorance and the like. To account for the diversity of bodily experience among Selves that are inherently entirely alike, the phrase 'tat tat karma' (this or that action) is used. 'Dehair bandhanivrittaye' means 'for freedom from the bondage caused by bodies' — or, alternatively, it connects as 'for those performing action through bodies,' in which case the emphatic sense is that bodies, precisely because they are the cause of bondage, can also serve as the means to liberation. The word 'shastriyam' (scripturally enjoined) implies that otherwise, transgressing the Lord's ordinance, punishment alone would result. 'Svavarnochitam' (proper to one's own class) implies that it would not be preferable for you to abandon warfare and the like to go begging alms. To account for its being a cause of immortality, the word 'anabhisamhita' (without desire for the fruit) is used. The doubt 'how can something of an unpleasant nature be a duty?' is removed by the word 'tu' (but/indeed), which conveys the unavoidability of such contacts — this being the reason that they are to be patiently endured; this much alone is to be done here.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
One might say: it is true that we shall remain together, but I grieve out of doubt as to whether that other-worldly body will be exactly such as this or not — anticipating this concern, he says, in 'Dehinah...': just as for the embodied living being, in this body, the three states — childhood, youth and old age — occur in due course of time, so too, by the Lord's will, the devotee, being the Lord's own, attains another body, that is, attains a second, other-worldly body. The steadfast, the devotee, is not deluded regarding the attainment of that body — does not fall into delusion.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
One might object: even granting this, grief born of separation from a beloved body does indeed occur. Anticipating this, he says, in 'Dehinah...': the two bodies — gross and subtle — belong to it, hence it is 'dehin,' the conscious Self. Just as, in this gross body, despite the difference of bodies across states such as childhood, there is recognition of identity — 'I who was a child am now old' — establishing unity, and yet also distinctness from the child's and other bodies, following the maxim 'the thread, though distinct from the flowers, runs through them all' — so too the attainment of another body is likewise the attainment of subtle bodies (linga-sharira), other than the gross body, which merely resemble the gross body. The meaning is this: just as a single gross body appears as many, differentiated by states such as childhood, so too the subtle body, though eternal, becomes manifold owing to the differences among beings' karma, appearing as god, human, animal and so forth. Hence, by the same reasoning, the Self is shown to be distinct even from the subtle body, just as it is from the gross. And thus, for you, who are distinct even from the subtle body which is the very substrate of grief and the like, grief born of separation from what is beloved is not fitting either. Hence, in this matter, the steadfast is not deluded. The grief and delusion born of mere self-identification do not afflict the steadfast, since he has abandoned identification with both bodies; hence you too should become steadfast, is the intent. In the previous two verses, the plural used in 'gatasun' and 'vayam' (we) refers to the plurality of limiting adjuncts; here, however, the singular 'dehinah' refers to the unity of the conscious Self that is the substrate of those adjuncts. Scripture, too, states that the apparent plurality of the one Self is conditioned: 'just as this single sun, the light-bearing Self, follows the many divided waters, so too, conditioned, this same unborn Self takes on manifold divided forms in the various fields' — 'fields' here referring, as will be described later, to the twofold gross and subtle bodies. And: 'the one God, hidden in all beings, all-pervading, the inner Self of all beings' — from this very oneness, the Self's all-pervasiveness too is established. By this, the rival theories that regard the body and the rest, which are impermanent and non-pervasive, as the Self, are to be understood as refuted.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
As the embodied soul continuously passes, in this body, from boyhood to youth to old age, the soul similarly passes into another body at death. A sober person is not bewildered by such a change.

 Since every living entity is an individual soul, each is changing his body every moment, manifesting sometimes as a child, sometimes as a youth and sometimes as an old man. Yet the same spirit soul is there and does not undergo any change. This individual soul finally changes the body at death and transmigrates to another body; and since it is sure to have another body in the next birth – either material or spiritual – there was no cause for lamentation by Arjuna on account of death, neither for Bhīṣma nor for Droṇa, for whom he was so much concerned. Rather, he should rejoice for their changing bodies from old to new ones, thereby rejuvenating their energy. Such changes of body account for varieties of enjoyment or suffering, according to one’s work in life. So Bhīṣma and Droṇa, being noble souls, were surely going to have spiritual bodies in the next life, or at least life in heavenly bodies for superior enjoyment of material existence. So, in either case, there was no cause of lamentation. Any man who has perfect knowledge of the constitution of the individual soul, the Supersoul, and nature – both material and spiritual – is called a dhīra, or a most sober man. Such a man is never deluded by the change of bodies. The Māyāvādī theory of oneness of the spirit soul cannot be entertained, on the ground that the spirit soul cannot be cut into pieces as a fragmental portion. Such cutting into different individual souls would make the Supreme cleavable or changeable, against the principle of the Supreme Soul’s being unchangeable. As confirmed in the Gītā, the fragmental portions of the Supreme exist eternally ( sanātana ) and are called kṣara; that is, they have a tendency to fall down into material nature. These fragmental portions are eternally so, and even after liberation the individual soul remains the same – fragmental. But once liberated, he lives an eternal life in bliss and knowledge with the Personality of Godhead. The theory of reflection can be applied to the Supersoul, who is present in each and every individual body and is known as the Paramātmā. He is different from the individual living entity. When the sky is reflected in water, the reflections represent both the sun and the moon and the stars also. The stars can be compared to the living entities and the sun or the moon to the Supreme Lord. The individual fragmental spirit soul is represented by Arjuna, and the Supreme Soul is the Personality of Godhead Śrī Kṛṣṇa. They are not on the same level, as it will be apparent in the beginning of the Fourth Chapter. If Arjuna is on the same level with Kṛṣṇa, and Kṛṣṇa is not superior to Arjuna, then their relationship of instructor and instructed becomes meaningless. If both of them are deluded by the illusory energy ( māyā ), then there is no need of one being the instructor and the other the instructed. Such instruction would be useless because, in the clutches of māyā, no one can be an authoritative instructor. Under the circumstances, it is admitted that Lord Kṛṣṇa is the Supreme Lord, superior in position to the living entity, Arjuna, who is a forgetful soul deluded by māyā.
