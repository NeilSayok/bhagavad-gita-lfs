Speaker: Sanjaya

Slok:
sañjaya uvāca .
evamuktvā hṛṣīkeśaṃ guḍākeśaḥ parantapaḥ .
na yotsya iti govindamuktvā tūṣṇīṃ babhūva ha

Life Application:
Observe your own urge to withdraw into silence when a challenge feels too heavy to carry alone.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Sanjaya continues, describing how Gudakesha (conqueror of sleep) and Parantapa (afflictor of foes) Arjuna, having gone to the Lord's refuge, said that he would not fight, and then fell silent.

Except for the blind Dhritarashtra alone, no one had the right or power to stop the battle even at this hour. A faint hope lingered in Sanjaya's heart that Dhritarashtra might stop this inevitable, unavoidable war. Arjuna, the afflictor of foes, had now reached the refuge of Sri Krishna, Govinda, the conqueror of all three worlds — hence his victory was now assured. But the blind-from-birth Dhritarashtra, out of excessive love for his son, would not heed anyone's excellent counsel.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Sanjaya reports to the king that Arjuna disclosed his own view to the Lord in this manner, in 'Sanjaya said...': having thus spoken to the Lord in the aforesaid way, Parantapa Arjuna, saying 'I will not fight, I will not do battle,' owing to the utterly unbearable onset of grief, addressing Govinda, indeed fell silent, is the sense.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Sanjaya said: O Parantapa Dhritarashtra! Having spoken thus, Arjuna, the conqueror of sleep, said clearly to the indwelling Lord Govinda, 'I will not fight,' and fell silent.

Explanation: 'Evam uktva hrishikesham... babhuva ha' — Arjuna, having weighed both his own position and the Lord's, finally arrived at this conclusion: fighting would at most win a kingdom, honor, worldly fame — but the grief, anxiety, and sorrow in my heart would not thereby be removed. Hence not fighting seemed right to Arjuna.

Although Arjuna respects the Lord's words and wishes to accept them, within himself the matter of fighting does not sit rightly. So Arjuna clearly states here what has settled within him: 'I will not fight.' Having thus plainly stated his own view, his own decision, to the Lord, nothing more remains to be said; hence he falls silent.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Sanjaya said: Seeing Partha, who, out of misplaced affection and compassion arising at an improper occasion, had come to regard the Kshatriya's supreme dharma of battle as adharma, and who, desiring to know true dharma, had surrendered to Him, and understanding that Arjuna's delusion would not be pacified without knowledge of the true nature of the Self — knowledge that battle undertaken without desire for its fruit is itself the means to attaining the true nature of the Self — the Supreme Person, the Blessed Lord, undertook the revelation of the science of the Self. As it is said: 'Seeing Partha, bewildered by affection and compassion misplaced at this occasion, and by confused notions of dharma and adharma, surrendered to Him, the Lord undertook the revelation of scripture' (Gitartha-sangraha 5). Seeing him thus overcome with grief born of true knowledge of the relation between body and Self, yet also speaking of dharma arising from knowledge of the Self as distinct from the body — mingling contradictory qualities — and seeing him, in the midst of both armies arrayed for battle, suddenly inactive, the Supreme Person, as though smiling, spoke thus — as though in jest — beginning with 'Never was there a time when I did not exist' (2.12) and continuing through to 'I shall liberate you from all sins; grieve not' (18.66), covering the subject matter of karma-yoga, jnana-yoga, and bhakti-yoga, the means to attaining the true nature of the Self and of the Supreme Self and union with Him.

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
On 'Karpanya...' and the rest: through the words beginning 'between the two armies' and the rest, it is indicated that Arjuna, overcome with doubt, has not merely ceased fighting through one particular line of reasoning; and it is precisely for this reason that he says 'instruct me, who have taken refuge in You.' Hence he is being taught by the Blessed Lord as one caught between both knowledge and ignorance.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
In answer to the expectation of what Arjuna did after speaking thus, Sanjaya said, in 'Evam...': 'Gudaka' means sleep; its master, one free of drowsiness — or, 'gudakesha' meaning curly-haired. Having addressed Govinda, the overseer of all the senses, the Lord of Vraja, as one surrendered to Him, saying 'I will not fight,' he fell silent.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Thereafter, in answer to Dhritarashtra's eager desire to know what Arjuna did, Gudakesha, conqueror of sloth, Parantapa, the afflictor of foes, Arjuna, addressed Hrishikesha, the indwelling controller who impels all the senses, as Govinda — one who, by the etymology of 'knowing the speech characterized by the Vedas,' is omniscient, being the very source of all the Vedas. Having first, beginning with 'How shall I fight Bhishma in battle...,' declared the very impropriety of battle itself, and thereafter, saying 'I will not fight,' declared the absence of any fruit in battle, he fell silent. This means that his outward sensory activity, previously engaged for battle, having ceased, he became inactive. The word 'ha' serves to show that this laziness and this affliction of foes were adventitious, having no proper place in one who was by nature a conqueror of sloth and an afflictor of all foes. By the two words 'Govinda' and 'Hrishikesha,' which indicate omniscience and omnipotence, it is suggested that the Lord's dispelling of this delusion would be accomplished without effort.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Indicating that Arjuna, though a conqueror of ignorance-sleep and an afflictor of foes, whose every sense was governed by Hrishikesha, having accepted his own ignorance and his withdrawal from battle, thus addressed the all-knowing Supreme Self, Govinda, for the sake of revealing the meaning of the Vedas for the world's upliftment, and fell silent — he says, in 'Evam...': having thus, in the aforesaid manner, addressed Hrishikesha, the controller of all the senses, Gudakesha Arjuna, Parantapa, the afflictor of foes, saying 'I will not fight, I will not do battle,' addressing Govinda, fell silent, freed from the activity of speech, is the sense. 'Ha' is used as a mere expletive. The word 'ha' serves to show that this laziness and this affliction of foes were, in one naturally a conqueror of sloth and afflictor of all foes, adventitious and without proper place, say some. By the two words 'Govinda' and 'Hrishikesha,' indicating omniscience and omnipotence, it is suggested that the Lord's dispelling of this delusion would be effortless.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
In the verse 'Evam uktva,' the word 'Hrishikesha' conveys both His role as the impeller of Arjuna's senses for hearing the good teaching, and, per 'that which would remove this grief drying up my senses' (2.8), His capacity to pacify the disturbance of the senses. 'Hrishikani' are the senses, since they delight or cause delight. 'Evam uktva' means having spoken in the manner of his own state, as described. 'Gudaka' means sleep or sloth, per the rule 'gudaka means sleep'; its master is 'Gudakesha,' meaning one of naturally awakened disposition; or, alternatively, curly-haired. By the word 'Govinda' is conveyed both his fitness of speech for the removal of grief, and his readiness, as indicated by the word 'go' (earth), to undertake the lifting of the earth's burden.

Thus, through this introduction, stating the connection of the upcoming revelation of scripture at the proper occasion, he also, by implication, explains the summary verse of introduction, in 'Evam...': he shows that the word 'improper occasion' refers to what was stated in 'arisen at an improper juncture' (2.2), and, to connect it specifically with affection and compassion, states them separately. 'Aprakritim gatam' explains the sense of the word 'confused' — thereby suggesting his natural steadfastness, since he himself said 'my nature overpowered' (2.7); this also shows that the fault-of-miserliness-overpowered-nature was stated as the cause of the confused-mind-regarding-dharma. The explanation of 'confused about dharma' (2.7), namely 'dharma-adharma-dhih,' is stated through 'for Kshatriyas...' and the rest. 'Dharma-adharma-dhih' means the notion of adharma in what is dharma — like the notion of silver in mother-of-pearl — where, on the true-perception view, absence of discrimination between the two is intended. This very notion is tamasic, as will be said later: 'that which regards adharma as dharma' (18.32). Here, the notion of dharma-adharma born of affection and compassion misplaced at an improper occasion is to be understood as the intended construction, following the older commentarial tradition of 'confused by affection, compassion, dharma and adharma' — though in the reading 'confused by fear of dharma and adharma,' it becomes a compound of three terms. The word 'prapanna' (surrendered), used together with 'I ask you' (2.7), is explained as 'desiring to know dharma, surrendered.' Thus, it is stated that the Lord's action was undertaken with reference to a worthy recipient — Partha — not merely as a pretext.

The purport of 'addressing bewildered Partha' (Gitartha-sangraha 5) is explained through the phrase from 'atma' onward to 'nama-tvena.' The true nature of the Self — its eternality, its subordination to the Lord, and so on — as conveyed by 'I do not see...' (2.8) and the rest, is itself the means of dispelling this very grief, is the sense. In answer to the question 'by whom was this done,' to forestall any doubt that Vyasa or some other author of the text was responsible, it is stated 'by the Blessed Lord, the Supreme Person' — these two words conveying His scriptural authority and freedom from partisan bias. To exclude other, extraneous scriptures, it is qualified as 'adhyatma' (concerning the Self). To establish the traditional authority of this interpretation, it is said 'thus stated.' Though each chapter's meaning differs in its own summary verse, this passage clarifies which portion, up to this point, constitutes merely the introduction to the revelation of scripture, and which portion thereafter constitutes the revelation itself — the summary verse covering, in essence, the unstated first chapter through the phrase 'improper occasion.' The great sage (Vyasa), for the sake of connecting the subject of grief and its removal, incorporated that first-chapter material into this second chapter as well — this too is indicated by those who summarize the second chapter's fruit as 'for the pacification of that delusion' (Gitartha-sangraha 6). Hence it is also shown that the substance of the first chapter — being overcome by affection and the rest at an improper occasion — is what is being recapitulated here for the sake of connection.

One might object: how, then, is scripture concerning karma-yoga, jnana-yoga, bhakti-yoga and the rest — subjects not even asked about — taught to one addressed in this manner? For it is remembered, per Manu (2.110), 'one should not speak to one who has not asked.' And especially, this subject matter, being of a secret, more secret, and most secret order, is not fit to be taught abruptly. Moreover, in passages such as 'therefore fight, O Bharata' (2.18) and 'resolved upon battle' (2.37), it appears the intent is merely contextual encouragement to battle; hence this scripture is not truly concerned with the Self at all — so it might be objected. To this we reply: in the questioning verse 'what would be truly good' (2.7), the word 'shreyah' (the good) is seen without any specified particular. Nor is there any rule that Arjuna's inquiry must concern battle alone as the good; rather, since he is deeply pious and in the Lord's presence, an inquiry extending, on the pretext of the immediate matter, all the way to the supreme good is entirely fitting. Or, granting that his inquiry concerned battle alone, still, the Lord, out of supreme compassion, taking hold of the general statement 'what would be the good,' rightly gave the supreme beneficial teaching. That 'fight' and the rest are likewise taught as means to the supreme good is made clear at various points throughout. Hence this is rightly called 'the revelation of the science of the Self.'

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
In answer to the expectation of what Arjuna did after speaking thus, he says, in 'Evam uktva...': Gudakesha Arjuna, having thus addressed Hrishikesha, the impeller of the senses, in the aforesaid manner, said to Govinda, the protector of devotees, 'I will not fight,' and, remarkably, fell silent. Even after being addressed by the Lord, he did not become desirous of the kingdom. 'Parantapa' (afflictor of foes) is a vocative here, meaning: your own kinsmen, having given up their lives before Sri Krishna, shall attain fulfillment — with this very intent it is said, per the celebrated verse, 'the arrows of the Partha-warriors, purified, attain that supreme abode' (Bhagavata 3.2.20).

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
One might object: has it not already been said, 'abandoning this petty weakness of heart, arise, O Parantapa' — that battle alone is the true good? Why then do you ask again? Anticipating this, he says, in 'Na hi...': the grief caused by the destruction of my kinsmen would not be removed by the attainment of a kingdom nor by the attainment of heavenly sovereignty; hence, tell me some other means of pacification, some form of cessation, other than battle — this is the intent. Here, under the guise of Arjuna's despondency, the qualifications of one fit for the knowledge of Brahman are shown: the practice of begging alms, and dispassion toward the fruits of enjoyment both in this world and the next.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Sañjaya said: Having spoken thus, Arjuna, chastiser of enemies, told Kṛṣṇa, “Govinda, I shall not fight,” and fell silent.

 Dhṛtarāṣṭra must have been very glad to understand that Arjuna was not going to fight and was instead leaving the battlefield for the begging profession. But Sañjaya disappointed him again in relating that Arjuna was competent to kill his enemies ( paran-tapaḥ ). Although Arjuna was, for the time being, overwhelmed with false grief due to family affection, he surrendered unto Kṛṣṇa, the supreme spiritual master, as a disciple. This indicated that he would soon be free from the false lamentation resulting from family affection and would be enlightened with perfect knowledge of self-realization, or Kṛṣṇa consciousness, and would then surely fight. Thus Dhṛtarāṣṭra’s joy would be frustrated, since Arjuna would be enlightened by Kṛṣṇa and would fight to the end.
