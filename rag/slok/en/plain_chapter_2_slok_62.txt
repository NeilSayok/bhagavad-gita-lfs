Speaker: Sri Bhagavan

Slok:
dhyāyato viṣayānpuṃsaḥ saṅgasteṣūpajāyate .
saṅgātsañjāyate kāmaḥ kāmātkrodho.abhijāyate

Life Application:
Catch your thoughts early when fixating on desires, preventing the chain reaction that leads to frustration and anger.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
These two verses explain the cause of faults such as attachment, so that they may be avoided. Delusion (sammoha) here means the inclination toward unrighteous action, as confirmed elsewhere where delusion is described as a leaning toward wrongdoing rooted in sinful deeds. "Failure of memory" means the loss of awareness of the scriptural prohibitions; "destruction of the intellect" means the complete loss of the faculty that discerns right from wrong; and "he perishes" means he meets with calamities such as hell. As it is said elsewhere: when one given to unrighteous desire loses remembrance of the scriptures, through blindness to right conduct and the deeds born of it, he falls into hell.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
This verse, together with the next, explains what immediately follows the teaching on the means to the highest human goal, by showing the condition of one who lacks steadfastness in that goal - a person devoid of discriminative wisdom who is heading toward great calamity. The point being made, confirmed by common experience, is that contemplation of sense-objects is the root of all misfortune. "Attachment" here refers to the ordinary fondness that arises when an exaggerated charm is projected onto objects; "craving" is an intensified degree of that same attachment; and "obstruction" is the thwarting or frustration of that craving.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
In a person who dwells on sense-objects, attachment to them arises; from attachment is born desire; from desire arises anger; from anger comes delusion; from delusion, loss of memory; from loss of memory, destruction of the intellect; and from destruction of the intellect, the person falls.

Explanation: When a person does not take refuge in God and does not think of Him, he inevitably thinks of the objects of the world instead, since the soul stands between God on one side and the world on the other - abandoning God's shelter, it can only dwell on the world. Through such repeated contemplation, attachment and fondness for those objects arises. This attachment leads to enjoyment of the objects, whether mental or physical, and the pleasure derived from that enjoyment deepens the fondness, which in turn leads to still more frequent contemplation of the object - so that attachment arises whether or not the object is actually enjoyed.

From attachment is born the desire (kama) to obtain those objects of enjoyment. When favourable circumstances keep supplying the desired objects, greed also grows; and when something obstructs the fulfilment of that desire, anger arises. Desire is such that any obstruction to it inevitably produces anger - this happens even in the pride one takes in one's caste, station, qualities or ability, since such pride carries within it a desire for respect and honour, and any hindrance to that desire brings anger. Desire is a quality of rajas, delusion a quality of tamas, and anger lies between the two.

From anger comes delusion (sammoha), a loss of discrimination. In fact, delusion can arise from desire, anger, greed, or attachment/possessiveness alike, but of these, anger is the most dangerous, since desire, greed, and possessiveness are all rooted in the pursuit of one's own pleasure and self-interest, whereas anger is driven by the urge to harm others - hence the Lord singles out anger here as the cause of delusion.

From delusion comes failure of memory (smriti-vibhrama) - the resolutions formed through scripture and right reasoning, about the disciplines to be practised and the need for one's own upliftment, are forgotten. From failure of memory comes destruction of the discriminating intellect, so that the capacity for fresh right judgement is lost. And from destruction of the intellect, the person falls from his spiritual footing.

Though it takes time to describe this chain - contemplation, attachment, desire, anger, delusion, loss of memory, loss of intellect, downfall - in actual experience these arise and bring about a person's ruin almost instantaneously, like an electric current.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Indeed, in respect of a person, whose attachment to sense-objects is expelled but whose mind is not focussed on Me, even though he controls the senses, contemplation on sense-objects is unavoidable on account of the impressions of sins from time immemorial. Again attachment increases fully in 'a man who thinks about sense-objects'. From attachment arises desire.' What is called 'desire' is the further stage of attachment. After reaching that stage, it is not possible for a man to stay without experiencing the sense-objects. 'From such desire arises anger.' When a desire exists without its object being nearby, anger arises against persons nearby under the following. 'Our desire is thwarted by these persons.'

'From anger there comes delusion'. Delusion is want of discrimination between what ought to be done and what ought not to be done. Not possessing that discrimination one does anything and everything. Then there follows the failure of memory, i.e., of the impressions of the earlier efforts of sense control, when one strives again to control the senses.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
These two verses, beginning with "dhyayatah," explain the cause of faults such as attachment so that they may be avoided, following on from the statement that one should be self-controlled and devoted to the Lord, and that avoidance of attachment and aversion is a further means to that end. Delusion (sammoha) is here explained as the desire for what is unrighteous, since the term especially concerns the constant craving directed toward the causes of sinful, unseen wrongdoing - as confirmed elsewhere, where delusion is defined as inclination toward unrighteousness. "Failure of memory" denotes the loss or unsettling of memory regarding the prohibitions of scripture. "Destruction of the intellect" is explained as the complete loss, in every respect, of the faculty that judges right and wrong; and "he perishes" means he meets with calamities such as hell, as is confirmed elsewhere: when a person devoted to unrighteous desire loses awareness of scripture, through blindness to right conduct and the deeds it produces, he attains hell. The underlying point is that the chain of attachment and aversion leads by succession to calamities such as hell; recognizing this, and recognizing that this chain arises from the succession of dwelling on sense-objects, one who wishes to avoid it should avoid that cause.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Having explained the fault that follows from lack of control over the external senses, the Lord now explains, in four verses, the fault that follows from lack of control over the mind - the loss of yogic wisdom - and thereby indicates what constitutes a person of steady wisdom. Of these four, the fault arising from lack of mental control is explained in the first two, beginning "dhyayatah vishayan": for one who reflects upon, that is, dwells upon, sense-objects, attachment arises; from attachment, longing for the object; from that, when thwarted, anger; from anger, loss of discrimination; from that, the lapse of the memory instilled by scripture and teachers; from that, the ruin of the resolute intellect; and so the person perishes - falling into utter forgetfulness, he becomes a mere creature of nature, remaining impure.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
An objection is raised: since the mind causes harm only by driving the external senses outward, once the senses are forcibly restrained - like a snake with its fangs pulled out - there should be no harm even if the mind itself remains unrestrained, because without external activity the purpose is already served; so the earlier injunction to be self-controlled would be pointless. In answer, these two verses show that even with the external senses restrained, if the mind is not mastered, every kind of ruin follows. For a person who, even with the senses withdrawn, keeps dwelling again and again in the mind on sense-objects such as sound, a fondness arises through the notion that "these are supremely conducive to my pleasure" - this is attachment. From that attachment, born of the conviction that objects are a source of pleasure, arises desire - an intense craving that "these should be mine." When that desire, directed toward some object, is thwarted, anger blazes up against whatever obstructs it. From anger comes delusion, the loss of discrimination between what should and should not be done. From delusion comes failure of memory - a lapse in recalling the teachings of scripture and teachers. From that failure of memory follows the destruction of the intellect - the faculty oriented toward the true Self is either prevented from arising or, having arisen, becomes unfit to bear fruit. And from destruction of the intellect, the person perishes - becomes unfit for any true human goal, and is, in effect, already dead while still living. Since even a person with the external senses restrained meets with such supreme calamity if the mind is not controlled, the mind must be restrained with the greatest effort - this is the intent behind the earlier injunction to be self-controlled, restraining all the senses.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Having stated the fault that arises from lack of control over the external senses, the Lord now states, in these two verses beginning "dhyayatah," the fault that arises from lack of control over the mind. For a person who dwells on sense-objects, regarding them as endowed with good qualities, attachment to them arises. From that attachment, an intensified craving for them arises. And from that craving, when thwarted by some cause, anger arises.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
It was said that the senses must be conquered through effort; here, by way of contrast, the fault that results when the mind instead habitually dwells on sense-objects is explained, beginning "dhyayatah." For a person who dwells on sense-objects, fondness for them arises; from that fondness, longing is born; from that longing, when thwarted by some cause, anger arises; from anger comes confusion about what ought and ought not to be done - an angry, deluded man will even rail against his own teacher; consequently there is a lapse of the memory formed through the instruction of scripture and teachers, so that when the occasion for memory to function arises, it fails to do so; from that follows the ruin of the intellect's capacity to discriminate between right and wrong action; and from that he perishes - though alive, he becomes as one dead, unfit for any true human goal. This is the sense of both verses; the underlying point is that dwelling on sense-objects is itself the seed of all misfortune.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
To the question "how, then, does one fall?", the answer is given here, beginning "dhyayatah": for a person who dwells on sense-objects, attachment to them arises. And to the further question of what results from attachment, it is said, beginning "sangat": from attachment desire is born, and from desire anger arises - blazing out on every side, that is, even toward things that do not deserve it.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
The scripture declares: "When the five senses, together with the mind, come to rest, and the intellect no longer wavers, that they call the highest state" - teaching that the supreme goal is attained through mastery of the senses, mind, and intellect. Here the question arises: for a yogi who has withdrawn his senses from external objects and rests in the mind alone, what happens if the mind itself remains unmastered? This is answered in these two verses, beginning "dhyayatah." For a person who dwells on, that is, thinks about, sense-objects such as sound, a bond of attachment toward those objects arises - meaning that even senses withdrawn from external objects, through a defect of the mind, once again seize upon those external objects. From that attachment, desire - a longing for that object - is born. And from desire, when thwarted by some cause, anger, whose very nature is to blaze up, arises.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them, and from such attachment lust develops, and from lust anger arises.

 One who is not Kṛṣṇa conscious is subjected to material desires while contemplating the objects of the senses. The senses require real engagements, and if they are not engaged in the transcendental loving service of the Lord, they will certainly seek engagement in the service of materialism. In the material world everyone, including Lord Śiva and Lord Brahmā – to say nothing of other demigods in the heavenly planets – is subjected to the influence of sense objects, and the only method to get out of this puzzle of material existence is to become Kṛṣṇa conscious. Lord Śiva was deep in meditation, but when Pārvatī agitated him for sense pleasure, he agreed to the proposal, and as a result Kārtikeya was born. When Haridāsa Ṭhākura was a young devotee of the Lord, he was similarly allured by the incarnation of Māyā-devī, but Haridāsa easily passed the test because of his unalloyed devotion to Lord Kṛṣṇa. As illustrated in the above-mentioned verse of Śrī Yāmunācārya, a sincere devotee of the Lord shuns all material sense enjoyment due to his higher taste for spiritual enjoyment in the association of the Lord. That is the secret of success. One who is not, therefore, in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, however powerful he may be in controlling the senses by artificial repression, is sure ultimately to fail, for the slightest thought of sense pleasure will agitate him to gratify his desires.
