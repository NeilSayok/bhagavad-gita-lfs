Life Application:
Acknowledge that action is constant in human life, and direct your inevitable activity toward wholesome and constructive goals.

Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Man is always under the influence of the three gunas of nature -- sattva, rajas and tamas -- and cannot remain completely inactive even for a moment. Complete inactivity is the nature of inert matter, not of a living being. Even when the body performs no action, the mind and intellect continue to function; thought-activity ceases only in deep sleep. As long as we remain under the sway of these gunas, we are compelled to act. Therefore, total renunciation of action is impossible, since it would go against the very law of nature. Even without physical action, a person who does not act still wastes the power of the mind in idle thoughts. Hence the Gita's teaching is that one should act through the body, but with a spirit of dedication and surrender -- this both prevents the waste of one's energy and helps the growth of one's personality. For a person who does not know the true nature of the Self, giving up one's duty is not proper. The Lord now explains this further.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
This verse takes up the question of why perfection is not attained merely by giving up action without knowledge. An objection could be raised: since even a person of knowledge is subject to the gunas, does this mean renunciation is impossible even for the wise? This is answered by clarifying that it is only the ignorant person -- not the knower of the Self -- who is driven to act by the desires arising from the gunas of sattva, rajas and tamas, since the knower of the Self is unmoved by the gunas (as stated later: 'one who is not shaken by the three gunas'). This corresponds to the distinction the Sankhyas draw between the path of knowledge and the path of action. Since even a person of knowledge retains some natural bodily movement, a doubt may arise whether karma yoga would still apply to them; this is resolved by recalling that such natural functioning is not the same as the compelled action driven by the gunas.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
No person, in any state, can remain without acting even for a single moment, because all beings, being subordinate to nature (prakriti), are made to act by the gunas born of it.

Explaining the first half of the verse: whether one follows karma yoga, jnana yoga or bhakti yoga, no seeker can remain without acting. The words 'kascit' (anyone), 'ksanam' (a moment) and 'jatu' (ever) are significant. 'Kascit' shows that no one -- wise or ignorant -- is free of action, for even the body of a wise man, though he has no real attachment to it, continues to function. 'Ksanam' shows that as long as one identifies with the body, not even a single moment passes without some action, even if one does not consciously feel 'I am always acting'. 'Jatu' shows that in every state -- waking, dream, deep sleep or unconsciousness -- action continues. The reason is given in the second half by the word 'avasah' (helpless): because of dependence on prakriti, action is unavoidable, since prakriti itself never ceases to change. The Gita counts not only bodily but also mental and verbal activity as karma (cf. 18.15), and only those activities with which a person identifies become binding. People often mistake only livelihood-related work as 'karma', while eating, sleeping, sitting and thinking are not thought of as karma -- this is a serious error, since bodily maintenance, subtle mental activities like sleep and thought, and even causal-body states like samadhi are all karma. As long as identification with the body persists, action continues in every state.

Explaining the second half: the gunas born of prakriti compel all dependent beings to act, since prakriti and its gunas are ceaselessly active (cf. 3.27, 13.29). Although the soul itself is inactive, unattached, imperishable and unchanging, as long as it identifies with a body -- gross, subtle or causal -- and seeks pleasure through it, it remains subordinate to prakriti (cf. 14.5); this subordination is what is meant by 'avasah' here. The same idea appears again at 8.19 and 9.8. One's nature (svabhava) is formed from tendencies, tendencies from the gunas, and the gunas from prakriti -- so whether one speaks of subjection to nature, to the gunas, or to prakriti, it amounts to the same dependence on the ever-changing products of prakriti, from which all other forms of dependence -- on time, on past karma, on circumstance, on people, wealth or possessions -- ultimately arise. Only when the soul realizes its true nature beyond the gunas, or attains the Supreme, does this dependence end and true freedom is attained.

A further point: prakriti has active (gross) and seemingly inactive (subtle) states, such as working versus sleeping; but even in apparent inactivity like sleep, subtle activity continues, as shown by the fact that a person woken before their sleep is complete feels they were woken too soon. Even in samadhi or cosmic dissolution, subtle activity continues, since prakriti, changing every instant, is never truly inactive. The soul has no real agency of its own, but by identifying with the body it comes to regard itself as the agent of these naturally occurring processes -- growth, digestion, breathing, perception -- thereby becoming bound. Prakriti is ever-changing, while the pure Self never changes; material objects have no independent existence, being only ceaselessly changing aggregates. As long as identification with such objects persists, one cannot avoid acting even for a moment. A seeker who truly realizes that all activity belongs to matter, and that they themselves have no real connection with it, becomes free from this dependence; the karma yogi removes this dependence by giving up desire, attachment and possessiveness toward the ever-changing objects of nature. The Lord repeats this same point at 18.11: no embodied being can entirely renounce action.

Having established that no one can remain inactive even for a moment, a doubt may arise as to whether one could forcibly restrain the senses and thereby appear inactive; the next verse addresses this doubt.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Since it was earlier said (3.4) that perfection is not attained merely by the stage of renouncing action without knowledge, this verse answers the natural question of the reason why, with the words 'na hi kascit...'. It does not merely assert that knowledge is essential, nor that rites such as yajna cannot be omitted -- rather it establishes that actions necessary for the maintenance of the body are simply unavoidable. This forecloses an alternative interpretation, based on the smriti text 'even so, a being is bound by action' (Mahabharata 12.241.7), that karma can never be given up in any sense; and it also prepares the ground for the restriction on the meaning of 'karma' that will be stated later at 3.9 ('for the sake of yajna...'). To preempt the question of why that later restriction is justified, this verse first establishes, in preparatory fashion, that the word 'karma' taken in its unrestricted sense -- meaning all action whatsoever -- cannot possibly be conceived by anyone as something that could be totally renounced.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Therefore, since everyone is helpless/dependent, action is inevitably brought about by the gunas born of prakriti.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Since purity born of right action is lacking, a person whose senses are unrestrained never remains, even for a moment, without engaging in some worldly or Vedic action -- rather stays engrossed in such activity; hence renunciation is not possible for an impure mind. Why does even an unenlightened person never remain inactive? Because every being lacking purity of mind, being helpless and dependent, is compelled to perform worldly or Vedic action by the gunas -- sattva, rajas, tamas -- born of nature, and expressed as attachment, aversion and the like. Since these natural gunas are the true agents driving action, and one acts under their compulsion at all times, an impure-minded person cannot achieve complete renunciation of all action; therefore steadfastness in knowledge founded on renunciation is not possible for such a person.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Renunciation of actions means giving up attachment to them, not literally ceasing to perform them, since that is not truly possible -- this is explained here. In any state, even for a moment, no one -- whether wise or ignorant -- remains without performing some action. The reason: everyone, being helpless and dependent, is driven to act by the gunas -- attachment, aversion and the like -- born of one's own nature.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Answering the question of the reason: no ignorant, impure-minded person ever remains, even for a moment, in any state, without performing some action; being wholly dependent, every unenlightened person is compelled to act by the gunas -- sattva, rajas, tamas -- born of prakriti. This shows that renunciation of action means only giving up attachment to it, not its literal non-performance, since that is impossible; the idea that this applies 'whether wise or ignorant' is rejected, since Shankaracharya has already refuted this view at length, and it would otherwise contradict the statement that the wise are 'unshaken by the gunas' (14.23), which applies only to the knower. Hence the ignorant person who merely abandons action is criticized (as at 3.6), since this would also contradict scripture.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
This verse is introduced to dispel a doubt about consistency with the previous one: for one who contemplates giving up action -- itself a mode of worshipping the Supreme Person -- steadfastness in knowledge alone is very difficult to accomplish. The words 'kascit' (anyone) and 'sarvah' (all) recall the diversity of qualified persons mentioned earlier (3.3), hence the phrase 'in this world'. 'Jatu' refers to a longer span of time such as a day, while 'ksana' refers to a very brief moment -- together conveying 'at any time whatsoever', i.e., at all times other than cosmic dissolution; even a sleeping person is performing the 'action' of sleep, which is why rules of time and place govern what is permitted or prohibited even during sleep. 'Akarmakrt' means 'one who does not act', not 'a doer of non-action'. The word 'sarvah' covers even one who has resolved 'I will do nothing', a point made explicit in the next verse (3.6). Since the gunas are described as 'born of prakriti', the specific gunas -- sattva, rajas, tamas -- are meant, as also stated in the Vishnu Purana (2.13.70), that these gunas are themselves governed by one's past karma; this rules out the idea that karma yoga's purpose is merely to weaken the gunas as a precondition for steadfastness in knowledge. The word 'one's own' (referring to the specific duty to be described later) points to the gunas as the true agents -- clarifying that 'avasah' (helpless) qualifies the verb 'is made to act', meaning one is impelled by another (the gunas), not that one impels others. The overall purport of this and the previous verse: since a person is entirely dependent on the gunas, steadfastness in knowledge is difficult to attain without first practicing karma yoga; through karma yoga, past sin is destroyed and the gunas come under control, favoring one's progress toward liberation -- this control of the gunas, meaning either the reduction of excess rajas and tamas or of the attachment and aversion they produce, is the 'purity' intended here.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Without knowledge, even an attempt to give up action does not amount to true renunciation -- whether knowingly or unknowingly, one continues to act regardless. No one, at any time, remains even for a moment without performing action, because everyone is driven to act by the gunas -- sattva and the rest -- born of prakriti. The reason for this is given by the word 'avasah' (helpless): meaning 'not under My control', that is, not yet My devotee. The implication is that only after recognizing the material nature of these processes, and by surrendering to Me, should one give up action -- not otherwise.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Elaborating the same point: a person of unrestrained mind, lacking the purity that comes from right action, never remains -- even in deep meditation or samadhi, even for a moment -- without performing some action, whether wayward thoughts or otherwise. This is because everyone in the world is driven to bodily, verbal or mental action by the gunas -- sattva, rajas, tamas -- born of their own nature, manifesting as attachment, aversion and the like.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Everyone is forced to act helplessly according to the qualities he has acquired from the modes of material nature; therefore no one can refrain from doing something, not even for a moment.

 It is not a question of embodied life, but it is the nature of the soul to be always active. Without the presence of the spirit soul, the material body cannot move. The body is only a dead vehicle to be worked by the spirit soul, which is always active and cannot stop even for a moment. As such, the spirit soul has to be engaged in the good work of Kṛṣṇa consciousness, otherwise it will be engaged in occupations dictated by the illusory energy. In contact with material energy, the spirit soul acquires material modes, and to purify the soul from such affinities it is necessary to engage in the prescribed duties enjoined in the śāstras. But if the soul is engaged in his natural function of Kṛṣṇa consciousness, whatever he is able to do is good for him. The Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam (1.5.17) affirms this: tyaktvā sva-dharmaṁ caraṇāmbujaṁ harer bhajann apakvo ’tha patet tato yadi yatra kva vābhadram abhūd amuṣya kiṁ ko vārtha āpto ’bhajatāṁ sva-dharmataḥ “If someone takes to Kṛṣṇa consciousness, even though he may not follow the prescribed duties in the śāstras or execute the devotional service properly, and even though he may fall down from the standard, there is no loss or evil for him. But if he carries out all the injunctions for purification in the śāstras, what does it avail him if he is not Kṛṣṇa conscious?” So the purificatory process is necessary for reaching this point of Kṛṣṇa consciousness. Therefore, sannyāsa, or any purificatory process, is to help reach the ultimate goal of becoming Kṛṣṇa conscious, without which everything is considered a failure.
