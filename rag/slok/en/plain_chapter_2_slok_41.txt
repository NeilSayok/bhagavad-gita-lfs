Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The supreme achievement of Self-realisation through the practice of Karma Yoga is possible only because the seeker practises it with firm resolve and a one-pointed mind. Those who act driven by countless desires for the fruits of action remain a scattered personality, and because of this they cannot work steadily and single-mindedly in any field -- the only result they get is disastrous failure. This verse briefly reveals the secret of success. When one acts with a resolute intellect, success is certainly attained in any field. But generally people harbour innumerable desires, which are often mutually contradictory, and naturally they exhaust the power of the mind trying to fulfil them all. This is called the play of resolve and irresolution, which robs a person of every opportunity for success.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
It was said, 'Listen to this understanding.' Since there are many kinds of intellect owing to differences of opinion, how then is one to become fixed in a single one? To this he replies with the words 'vyavasayatmika...' The meaning is that the views ascertained through proper reasoning are indeed only one.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
An objection is raised: besides the two kinds of intellect already spoken of (belonging to Sankhya and Yoga), there exist other kinds of intellect too, well known from the Vaisheshika school of Kanada and similar systems; so how can the Lord teach that only these two intellects exist? The reply is that this one intellect alone, being resolute (vyavasayatmika), is authoritative; other intellects, rooted in lack of discrimination, are declared invalid by the words 'bahushakha hi' (many-branched indeed). Compared to the resolute intellect that has taken to the path of the good, the other, contrary intellects -- born of invalid means of knowledge and self-imagined -- have countless branch-divisions that are the cause of worldly bondage, and the resolute intellect overcomes that very cause, because it arises from the flawless words of the Veda and so overcomes the cause of worldly existence both directly and through succession. The countless variations of these unauthoritative intellects, arising one after another through attachment, are their 'endlessness', meaning that without right knowledge they never come to rest; they are also without any real, substantial cause, being mere effects. Through inference and scripture, the discriminative intellect arrived at by refining the meaning of words sets aside the contrary intellects, and when these countless contrary intellects are thus set aside, worldly existence, having no support left, itself comes to an end. Just as a tree has many branches, each single intellect too has further particulars, which is why intellects are said to be innumerable and endless; and lest it be doubted that even men of true knowledge share in such division of intellect, that doubt too is answered.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O Kurunandana! In respect of attaining this equanimity, the resolute intellect (vyavasayatmika buddhi) is only one; the intellects of irresolute persons are endless and many-branched.

Explanation -- 'vyavasayatmika buddhirekeha kurunandana': The goal that the seeker of Karma Yoga wishes to attain is equanimity (samata), and that equanimity is itself the nature of the Supreme. To attain that equanimity which is of the nature of God, evenness of the inner faculties is the means, and attachment to the world is the obstacle to that evenness. The single resolve, either to remove that attachment or to attain the Supreme Reality, is called the resolute intellect. Why is this resolute intellect only one? Because in it there is renunciation of desire for worldly objects, and this renunciation is of one kind only, whether one renounces the desire for wealth or the desire for honour. But in acquiring things there are many kinds, because each single thing itself comes in many varieties -- just as one and the same sweet comes in many forms. Hence the desires for such things are also many and endless. In the Gita, the resolute intellect is described in the context of Karma Yoga (this verse) and Bhakti Yoga (9.30), but not in the context of Jnana Yoga, because in Jnana Yoga realisation of one's true nature comes first and the intellect becomes resolute as its natural result, whereas in Karma Yoga and Bhakti Yoga the resolve of the intellect comes first and realisation follows. Hence in Jnana Yoga knowledge is primary, while in Karma Yoga and Bhakti Yoga the single resolve is primary. 'bahushakha hyanantashcha buddhayo'vyavasayinam': The irresolute are those who harbour selfish desire within, who are attached to enjoyment and accumulation. Because of desire, the intellects of such people are endless, and each of these intellects itself has endless branches. For example, 'I must obtain a son' is one resolve, and taking some medicine, chanting some mantra, performing some ritual, seeking a saint's blessing, and so on, for that purpose, are the endless branches of that one resolve. Similarly, 'I must acquire wealth' is one resolve, and doing business, taking employment, stealing, plundering, cheating or defrauding for that purpose are its endless branches. In the intellect of such people there is no resolve for attaining God. The next three verses explain why the intellects of irresolute people are endless.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Here, i.e., in every ritual sanctioned by the scriptures, the Buddhi or disposition of mind marked by resolution, is single. The Buddhi marked by resolution is the Buddhi concerned with acts which must be performed by one desirous of release (and not any kind of work). The term 'Vyavasaya' menas unshakable conviction:  this Buddhi (disposition of mind) comes out of prior determination about the true nature of the self. But the Buddhi concerning the performance of rituals of fulfill certain desires, is marked by irresolution; because here only this much knowledge of the self is sufficient - 'the self (as an entity) exists differently from the body.' Such a general understanding is sufficient to alify for performing acts giving fulfilment of certain desires. It does not reire any definite knowledge about the true nature of the self. For, even if there is no such knowledge, desires for heaven etc., can arise, the means for their attainment can be adopted, and the experience of those fruits can take place. For this reason there is no contradiction in the teaching of the scriptures. [The contradiction negated here is how can the same scriptural acts produce different results - fulfilment of desires and liberation. The difference in the disposition of the mind accouts of it.]

The Buddhi (mental disposition) marked by resolution has a single aim, because it relates to the attainment of a single fruit. For, as far as one desiring release is concerned, all acts are enjoined only for the accomplishment of that single fruit. Therefore, since the purpose of the scriptures here is one only (i.e., liberation), the Buddhi regarding all rituals taught in the scriptures too is only one, as far as liberation-seekers are concerned. For example, the set of six sacrifices, beginning with Agneya with all their subsidiary processes (though enjoined in different passages) forms the subject of a single injunction, as they are all for the attainment of a single fruit. Conseently the Buddhi concerning these is one only. The meaning of the verse under discussion must be construed in the same manner. But the Buddhi of the irresolute ones who are engaged in rituals for winning such fruits as heaven, sons, cattle, food etc., are endless, frutis being endless. In rituals like Darsapurnamasa (new moon and full moon sacrifice), even though attainment of a single fruit (heaven) is enjoined, there accrues to these the character of having many branches on account of the mention of many secondary fruits as evidenced by such passages as, 'He desires a long life.' Therefore the Buddhi of irresolute ones has many branches and are endless.

The purport is:  In performing obligatory and occasional rituals, all fruits, primary and secondary, promised in the scriptures, should be abandoned, with the idea that release or salvation is the only purpose of all scripture-ordained rituals. These rituals should be performed without any thought of selfish gains. In addition, acts motivated by desires (Kamya-karmas) also should be performed according to one's own capacity, after abandoning all desire for fruits and with the conviction that they also, when performed in that way, form the means for attainment of release. They should be looked upon as eal to obligatory and occasional rites suited to one's own station and stage in life.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Kuru-nandana, O scion of the Kuru dynasty; iha, is this path to Liberation; there is only eka, a single; vyavasayatmika, one-pointed; buddhih, conviction, which has been spoken of in the Yoga of Knowledge and which has the characteristics going to be spoken of in (Karma-) yoga. It is resolute by nature and annuls the numerous branches of the other opposite thoughts, since it originates from the right source of knowledge. [The right source of knowledge, viz the Vedic texts, which are above criticism.] Those again, which are the other buddhayah, thoughts; they are bahu-sakhah, possessed of numerous branches, i.e. possessed of numerous variations. Owing to the influence of their many branches the worldly state becomes endless, limitless, unceasing, ever-growing and extensive. [Endless, because it does not cease till the rixe of full enlightenment; limitless, because the worldly state, which is an effect, springs from an unreal source.] But even the worldly state ceases with the cessation of the infinite branches of thoughts, under the influence of discriminating wisdom arising from the valid source of knowledge. (And those thoughts are) hi, indeed; anantah, innumerable under every branch. Whose thoughts? Avyavasayinam, of the irresolute ones, i.e. of those who are devoid of discriminating wisdom arising from the right source of knowledg.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Objection: by the verse beginning 'vyavasayatmika', the Yoga that was promised is now being taught; but this is not merely praise, like 'buddhya yuktah' (2.39) and similar words -- so what then is the purpose of this verse? The reply: listen to this teaching concerning the means to knowledge that is now to be spoken; the sense being that, having heard it, one should become firmly established in it, since mere hearing without a definite conclusion is not possible. Many contrary and mutually opposed teachings exist concerning the means to knowledge -- how then, amid such difference of opinion, with followers of one view teaching one means and followers of another refuting it and teaching something else, and with nothing to decide which alone is true, can firm faith be placed in any one teaching alone? One might object that every teaching is itself resolute regarding its own subject, since none of the teachers is in doubt about their own view -- so how can it be said that the resolute intellect belongs only to the wise while the intellects of the irresolute are many? The answer is given by the word 'samyak' (properly): here 'yukti' means a valid means of knowledge in general, and being ascertained by a valid means of knowledge is the true sense of the word 'vyavasaya', not mere certainty as such. Only the view established by a valid means of knowledge is to be accepted, and regarding it there is never any real dispute -- it is only one's own want of firm faith in it that creates difficulty; the disagreement present among unauthoritative teachings cannot affect it.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
In answer to the question 'why', it is said: in this Yoga the intellect is one, being resolute. The intellect that determines that some particular action -- whether fruitless or dedicated to the Lord -- must be performed is one, because its object is one. In Sankhya, however, the mental modification is free of the triad of knower, knowing and known. A further distinction is drawn from action performed for desired results: the intellects of the irresolute, those attached to desire-driven action, are, as is well known, many-branched and endless. This is also stated later, in the section on the demoniac nature: 'I shall sacrifice, I shall give, I shall rejoice' (16.15) and so on.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
To establish this, it is shown that statements such as 'tam etam' convey a single meaning. O joy of the Kurus, on this path of the good, the resolute intellect -- the intellect that ascertains the truth of the Self -- is one only, to be accomplished by all four stages of life, since the use of the instrumental case in such phrases shows that each means is independently sufficient; were the meanings different, it would amount to a mere aggregation, and there is no proof here of such an aggregation as there is elsewhere. Since both the intellect concerned with Sankhya and that concerned with Yoga lead to the same result, it is one and resolute, and it overcomes all contrary intellects because it arises from the flawless words of the Veda; the other intellects, belonging to the irresolute, are to be overcome -- this, according to the commentator, is the meaning. Others explain that here, in Karma Yoga, the resolute, single-pointed intellect is the firm resolve, 'I shall cross over worldly existence only through worship of the Supreme Lord.' In every way this accords with the teaching that even a little of this righteousness saves one from great fear. In the sphere of ritual action, however, the intellects of the irresolute are many-branched, because desires are of many kinds, and endless, because of the further divisions arising from the fruits and qualities of action. Hence the great difference between action performed for desire and action performed for purification.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
In answer to the question 'why', the difference between the two is explained: in this Karma Yoga, characterised as worship of the Lord, the resolute intellect -- the firm resolve, 'I shall surely cross over through devotion to the Supreme Lord alone' -- is one and single-pointed. But for the irresolute, those of many inclinations given to desire, the intellects are endless because desires are endless, and further many-branched owing to differences of action, quality and fruit. For, in action performed for the worship of the Lord, even the daily and occasional rites, though deficient in some subsidiary part, are not lost, since one is enjoined to perform them as best one can and the deficiency ceases the moment the act is dedicated to the Lord. It is not so, however, with action performed for desire, such as offering the Agnihotra for the sake of heaven, or offering with curd for the sake of the senses -- hence the great difference between the two.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
On this path to liberation, the resolute intellect -- the firm resolve regarding the means and the end -- is one, and being born of valid knowledge, it overcomes the branching divisions of the contrary intellect described earlier. Those who explain the resolute intellect as the firm resolve, 'I shall cross over through devotion to the Supreme Lord alone,' are describing the very nature of that intellect, since the commentators too, in saying it arises from valid knowledge, have called it firm -- hence there is no contradiction. The intellects of the irresolute, lacking the discriminative intellect born of valid knowledge, being desire-ridden, are many-branched, giving rise to unceasing worldly existence branch upon branch, and endless too, because desires themselves are endless. As to the doubt that, if Sankhya and Yoga are equally means of protection from great fear, what then distinguishes the two, since Sankhyas have no fear of falling while Yogis have such fear so long as bodiless liberation is not attained -- this is worth further examination, since it would be inappropriate to introduce a criticism of Yoga within a passage devoted to praising it; the earlier criticism beginning 'kamatmanah' clearly applies to the man of desire, not to Yoga itself. Hence, addressing Arjuna, born in a noble line, for whom the resolute intellect alone is fitting, the Lord says 'kurunandana', O joy of the Kurus.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Having thus, under the guise of extolling its greatness, shown Karma Yoga's superiority over all other action, the Lord now, as promised, teaches the intellect proper to it, distinguishing it from the intellect of desire-prompted action, both in cause and in result. 'Here' is explained as covering every scriptural action, since worldly action that is neither enjoined nor prohibited yields varying results and admits of no such unity; alternatively, the word gathers together obligatory, occasional and optional rites, so as not to be restricted to the present war alone. If it be asked how there can be unity amid the diversity of intellects concerned with diverse actions, the intellect meant is qualified by its object -- namely, that of one desirous of liberation. By the word 'resolve', the false notion that it means mere resolve to perform an act is set aside; what is thereby excluded is desire-prompted action. If it be asked how, without knowledge of a self distinct from the body and destined for another world, the great effort of rites such as sacrifice -- whose fruit is enjoyed in another body -- could be undertaken, the answer is that qualification through desire presupposes only the notion 'this action is meant for me, since it is the means to what I desire,' requiring merely that the self be stable enough to take up another body, not full knowledge of its true, eternal, self-luminous, blissful nature. It may be asked whether both heaven and liberation, being experienced after the body, alike require only knowledge of the self's mere existence -- whence, then, this distinction? The answer is that in desire-prompted action, certainty regarding the self's true nature is not required, since desire for what is established as pleasurable arises spontaneously, one naturally undertakes the means suited to that end while certain of one's own continuance until the fruit matures (just as a farmer tills the soil though the harvest is yet to come), and the fruit of a correctly performed act arises independently and is naturally experienced without any prior need for knowledge of the self's true nature -- just as with any worldly enjoyment. Elsewhere too, the self does not become the material cause of the body produced by sacrifice, nor does it enter the act merely as a residual impression, since no injunction to know the self in truth occurs in the context of desire-prompted action, Vedantic knowledge being established as aiming at a higher fruit and not remaining a mere auxiliary to ritual. Even the Upanishads teach the fruit of heaven to those unacquainted with the truth of the self, as in the text: 'These rafts, indeed, unsteady, in the form of the eighteen kinds of sacrifice -- the fools who delight in this as the highest good' (Mundaka Upanishad 1.2.7) and similar scriptures.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Moreover: for those who perform action for the sake of its fruit, the intellect goes in many directions; but lest one mistakenly suppose that for those who act knowing it to be the Lord's command, the intellect too becomes one only out of mere confusion, it is said: O Kurunandana, born of a noble family, here on the path of devotion, the resolute intellect is only one, of the form 'I shall act only by the command of the Lord.' The intellects of the irresolute, the outward-turned, those of unsettled heart, are endless and, being fruit-oriented, many-branched. For such people, lacking wholehearted engagement, obstacles and transgressions can indeed arise; but for devotees, because of their wholehearted engagement, there is never any possibility of failure or transgression. Hence the Lord's own assurance: 'If there should be a lapse of time for those performing My work, the three crores of great sages continue that work on his behalf.'

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
If it be asked, since Sankhya and Yoga are equally means of protection from the great fear of worldly existence, what then is the difference between the two, the answer is that Sankhyas have no fear of falling, whereas Yogis have such fear so long as bodiless liberation is not attained. 'Resolve' means firm ascertainment of the truth, and the intellect of that nature is the modification of the inner faculty taking the form of that truth -- the modification in the form of Brahman arising from the sentence 'I am Brahman,' known as knowledge of Brahman, which rightly arises by overriding every other modification, is one and one alone, for scripture says, 'This world of Brahman shines forth but once.' Indeed, once Brahman is known, nothing further remains to be known or done, since the knower of Brahman has thereby accomplished all that is to be accomplished, and hence for him there is no fear of falling. But for the irresolute, the ignorant, the intellects are endless, and each of them is many-branched, since certainty as to 'this alone is my good' is hard to attain, and sometimes, even when what is not good is mistaken for good, the fear of falling remains -- this is the great distinction between the two.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Those who are on this path are resolute in purpose, and their aim is one. O beloved child of the Kurus, the intelligence of those who are irresolute is many-branched.

 A strong faith that by Kṛṣṇa consciousness one will be elevated to the highest perfection of life is called vyavasāyātmikā intelligence. The Caitanya-caritāmṛta ( Madhya 22.62) states: ‘śraddhā’-śabde – viśvāsa kahe sudṛḍha niścaya kṛṣṇe bhakti kaile sarva-karma kṛta haya Faith means unflinching trust in something sublime. When one is engaged in the duties of Kṛṣṇa consciousness, he need not act in relationship to the material world with obligations to family traditions, humanity or nationality. Fruitive activities are the engagements of one’s reactions from past good or bad deeds. When one is awake in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, he need no longer endeavor for good results in his activities. When one is situated in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, all activities are on the absolute plane, for they are no longer subject to dualities like good and bad. The highest perfection of Kṛṣṇa consciousness is renunciation of the material conception of life. This state is automatically achieved by progressive Kṛṣṇa consciousness. The resolute purpose of a person in Kṛṣṇa consciousness is based on knowledge. Vāsudevaḥ sarvam iti sa mahātmā su-durlabhaḥ: a person in Kṛṣṇa consciousness is the rare good soul who knows perfectly that Vāsudeva, or Kṛṣṇa, is the root of all manifested causes. As by watering the root of a tree one automatically distributes water to the leaves and branches, so by acting in Kṛṣṇa consciousness one can render the highest service to everyone – namely self, family, society, country, humanity, etc. If Kṛṣṇa is satisfied by one’s actions, then everyone will be satisfied. Service in Kṛṣṇa consciousness is, however, best practiced under the able guidance of a spiritual master who is a bona fide representative of Kṛṣṇa, who knows the nature of the student and who can guide him to act in Kṛṣṇa consciousness. As such, to be well versed in Kṛṣṇa consciousness one has to act firmly and obey the representative of Kṛṣṇa, and one should accept the instruction of the bona fide spiritual master as one’s mission in life. Śrīla Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura instructs us, in his famous prayers for the spiritual master, as follows: yasya prasādād bhagavat-prasādo yasyāprasādān na gatiḥ kuto ’pi dhyāyan stuvaṁs tasya yaśas tri-sandhyaṁ vande guroḥ śrī-caraṇāravindam “By satisfaction of the spiritual master, the Supreme Personality of Godhead becomes satisfied. And by not satisfying the spiritual master, there is no chance of being promoted to the plane of Kṛṣṇa consciousness. I should, therefore, meditate and pray for his mercy three times a day, and offer my respectful obeisances unto him, my spiritual master.” The whole process, however, depends on perfect knowledge of the soul beyond the conception of the body – not theoretically but practically, when there is no longer a chance for sense gratification manifested in fruitive activities. One who is not firmly fixed in mind is diverted by various types of fruitive acts.
