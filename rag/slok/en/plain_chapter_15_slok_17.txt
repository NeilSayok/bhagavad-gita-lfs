Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:
But higher than all am I, the Supreme God, the Absolute Self, the Eternal Lord, Who pervades the worlds and upholds them all.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
But distinct is the Supreme Person, who is designated as the Supreme Self (Paramatma), the imperishable Lord, who pervades the three worlds and sustains them.

Commentary: Swami Chinmayananda explains that a single individual can be addressed by different names depending on various roles, relations, and offices; yet, if these relative viewpoints are removed, that person does not become non-existent but remains as a simple, unqualified individual. Similarly, the Supreme Reality, which when viewed as the ever-changing world of matter is called 'Kshara Purusha', and as the knower of Kshara is called 'Akshara Purusha', is in Himself distinct from both. He is described here as the Supreme Person (Uttama Purusha), the Supreme Self (Paramatma), and the Imperishable Lord (Avyaya Ishvara). By transcending the Kshara, the Akshara also ceases to be as a relative term, and what remains is the absolute substratum of both, the Paramatma. This Paramatma, the imperishable Lord, enters the three worlds and sustains them. In Sanskrit, 'Loka' means that which is perceived or experienced. Thus, 'Loka' can refer to the physical worlds (heaven, earth, etc.) or our three states of experience: waking, dream, and deep sleep. The same consciousness illuminates all three. We must not think of these three Purushas as three distinct entities. Only the Uttama Purusha is the absolute reality, appearing as Kshara and Akshara due to two different limiting adjuncts. In the absence of adjuncts, He remains in His ever-pure, unconditioned nature. For example, the single all-pervading space, conditioned by a pot or a room, appears as pot-space and room-space. It is clear that there are no three distinct spaces. If we ignore the pot and the room, there is only one space. Similarly, the Uttama Purusha alone is called Kshara and Akshara as the experienced and the experiencer. Free from all conditioning, He is the Paramatma. In the next verse, Lord Krishna explains the etymology of the word 'Purushottama' to show how He is the Supreme Brahman.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
Sri Madhvacharya explains that the Supreme Being (Paramatma) is entirely distinct from the perishable Jivas (Brahma down to all bound souls) and the imperishable Prakriti (represented by Sri or Kutastha). The Sarkaraksha Sruti declares: "All living beings from Prajapati downwards are Kshara; the imperishable Purusha is Pradhana (Prakriti). But the Supreme Person is spoken of as distinct from both, who is the support of all."

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Sri Anandgiri explains that after describing the two categories of cause and effect (Kshara and Akshara), the Lord presents a third category distinct from both, namely the Paramatma. His distinction means He is untouched by the defects and qualities of these two limiting adjuncts, being eternally pure, conscious, and free. 'Uttamah' and 'Anyah' indicate that He is entirely free from mutable and immutable characteristics. He sustains the entire universe (referred to as the three worlds) not through physical effort, but by His mere presence and existence as pure consciousness, and since there is no other sustainer of Him, He is called imperishable (Avyayah) and Lord (Ishvarah).

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
But distinct is the Supreme Person who is spoken of as the Supreme Self (Paramatma). He is the indestructible Lord who, entering the three worlds, sustains all.

Commentary: Swami Ramsukhdas explains that after describing the perishable and the imperishable in the previous verse, the Lord now shows that the Supreme Person is entirely different and distinct from both. The word 'anyah' (other) is used here not to show that the Supreme Self is completely separate from the Jiva (which is His own fragment), but to emphasize His extraordinary distinction and superiority. Jiva, despite being a part of God, has oriented itself towards the perishable world, whereas the Lord is free from any such limitation. The term 'Paramatma' represents the Supreme Self, which stands for the unconditioned, attribute-free Reality, the Soul of all souls. The words 'Paramatma' and 'Ishvara' both appear in this verse, signifying that the formless and the personal aspects are both the one Purushottama. The Lord is present everywhere equally and sustains all beings, though the ignorant soul erroneously assumes the burden of maintaining its family and relations, thus suffering needlessly. The Lord is called 'Avyaya' (indestructible/undecaying) because even while sustaining the entire creation, He suffers no depletion or change. The word 'Ishvara' represents the Supreme Ruler. Just as a child does not know how its parents sustain and raise it, the ignorant soul does not realize that the Lord alone maintains all. The surrendered devotee, however, knows this truth. In this maintenance, the Lord is completely impartial, sustaining the devotee and the non-devotee, the saint and the sinner, the believer and the atheist alike, as seen in the impartial distribution of sunlight, air, and the earth's support.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
But there is the 'Supreme Person who is other than the bound and liberated selves' expressed by the terms, the 'perishable' and the 'imperishable'. He forms a completely different category. All Srutis call Him the Supreme Self. But by that very designation as the Supreme Self, it may be known that the Supreme Person is a category distinct from the bound and the liberated selves. How?  'Entering the threefold world,' supports it. 'Loka' (world) is that which is perceived. There are three such perceivable worlds, He enters the 'three worlds' which can be understood from the authority of the Srutis. These are the world of unconscient matter, the world of conscient selves conjoined with matter, and the world of liberated selves. As understandable from the Srutis, He enters into these three categories as their Atman and supports them. Thus, He is an entity different from the triad which He pervades and maintains. Further He is different, as He is imperishable and as He is the Lord. Being imperishable, He is different from the bound non-conscient matter whose nature is subject to decay. He is different from the bound conscient selves as the latter is subject to Prakrti and follows its laws. He is also distinguished from the liberated selves, because in their previous condition they were connected with matter and mixed with it. Similarly, He is the Lord of these 'three worlds,' a category distinct from those which have to be ruled.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Tu, but; anyah, different, entirely contrary in characteristics from these; is the uttamah, supreme, most excellent; purusah, Person, who is different in characteristics from these-the mutable and the immutable-, untouched by the mutable and the immutable limiting adjuncts, and is by nature eternal, pure, conscious and free; udahrtah, spoken of in the Upanisads; iti, as; the paramatma, supreme Self; He is paramah, supreme, as compared with the selves like body etc. created by ignorance, and is the atma, Self, the inmost Consciousness of all beings. Hence He is the supreme Self. He Himself is being specially described: yah, who, by dint of His own active power inhering in the energy that is Maya; [Caitanya, consciousness, itself is the bala (energy); the sakti (active power) therein is Maya. Through that He upholds.] avisya, permeating; loka-trayam, the three worlds-called Bhuh (Earth), Bhuvah, (Intermediate Space) and Svah (Heaven); bibharti, upholds (them) by merely being present in His own nature. (And He) is the avyayah, imperishable; isvarah, God, the Omniscient One called Narayana, who is the Lord by nature.
This name-the supreme Person-of God as described is well known. Showing that the name is apt by virtue of its etymological significance, the Lord reveals Himself saying, 'I am the unsurpassable God':

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Sri Jayatirtha, expanding on Madhvacharya's view, notes that the Supreme Self (Paramatma) is designated as 'Uttama Purusha' (Supreme Person) to distinguish Him from both the changing Jivas and the unchanging Prakriti. Jivas are called Kshara because of their changeable states of enjoyment, and Prakriti is called Akshara because it is the mother of all changes yet remains itself unmanifest. The Supreme Lord is the controller of both, who pervades and sustains the entire universe by entering into it.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Sri Vallabhacharya explains that the word 'tu' (but) differentiates the Supreme Person from the Kshara and Akshara. The Purushottama is the supreme, limitless, eternal embodiment of existence, consciousness, and bliss (Satchidananda), who is the cause of all causes. Entering the three worlds, which are composed of the three Gunas, He rules as the Inner Self (Antaryamin) and sustains all. He is called Paramatma because He is the Supreme Ruler and the support of all. As the Bhagavata states, bondage and liberation are under His control, and He is free from all contradictions.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Sri Madhusudan Saraswati explains that the Uttama Purusha is entirely distinct from the perishable and imperishable limiting adjuncts. He is untouched by their defects and is by nature eternally pure, conscious, and liberated. He is called Paramatma because He is supreme compared to the five imaginary selves (Annamaya, Pranamaya, Manomaya, Vijnanamaya, and Anandamaya) created by ignorance, and is the inmost consciousness (Pratyak Cetana) of all beings. Permeating the three worlds by His Maya-Sakti, He sustains them by providing existence and consciousness. He is the changeless Lord (Ishvara), the Inner Ruler of all, Narayana.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Sri Sridhara Swami explains that the purpose of defining the mutable and the immutable was to reveal the Supreme Person (Uttama Purusha), who is distinct from both. He is called Paramatma by the Upanisads because He is the Self (distinct from the insentient Kshara) and Supreme (Param, distinct from the conscious individual Jiva who is the enjoyer). He is the Lord who, being changeless, enters and sustains the entire universe.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Sri Dhanpati explains that after showing the dual aspects of cause and effect (Kshara and Akshara), the Lord presents the Supreme Self (Paramatma), who is contrary to them, untouched by their defects, and eternally pure, conscious, and free. He is called Paramatma in the Upanisads because He is superior to the physical body and the individual soul, and is the inner controller of all. By His Maya-Sakti, He enters the three worlds and sustains, governs, and illumines the universe merely by His presence. Being changeless, He is the imperishable God (Narayana).

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha interprets the term 'Paramatma' (Supreme Self) as indicating that the Supreme Person is entirely different from the bound and liberated souls. He enters the three categories (insentient matter, bound sentient souls, and liberated souls) as their inner controller (Antaryamin) and supports them, which proves He is distinct from what He pervades. He is 'Avyaya' (imperishable) because He does not share the decay of matter or the limitations of bound and liberated souls. He is 'Ishvara' because He is the ruler, which sets Him apart from the ruled creation.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Sri Purushottamji explains that the previous two Purushas were described to facilitate the knowledge of the Purushottama (Supreme Person). The word 'tu' excludes any equality between the Supreme Person and the other two. The Supreme Person is 'anyah' (different), meaning He is unknown to all and distinct from all. He is called 'Paramatma' because He is the highest, unmodified Self of all. He enters the three worlds, supports them, and sustains them to enable the experience of various Rasas (divine tastes). He is 'Avyaya' (changeless) and 'Ishvara' (capable of doing, undoing, or otherwise acting), showing His absolute control.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Sri Neelkanth explains that the Supreme Person is the unconditioned Reality, entirely distinct from the limiting adjuncts of cause and effect (Kshara and Akshara), and is referred to as the Paramatma in the scriptures. He enters and pervades the three worlds (constituting the highest, middle, and lowest grades of bodies) by His Maya, acting as Ishvara to sustain them. Despite this, He is 'Avyaya' (indestructible/changeless), meaning He is not affected by the attributes of the Jiva (such as limited knowledge) or those of the cosmic ruler, remaining completely free from growth or decay.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Besides these two, there is the greatest living personality, the Supreme Soul, the imperishable Lord Himself, who has entered the three worlds and is maintaining them.

 The idea of this verse is very nicely expressed in the Kaṭha Upaniṣad (2.2.13) and Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad (6.13). It is clearly stated there that above the innumerable living entities, some of whom are conditioned and some of whom are liberated, there is the Supreme Personality, who is Paramātmā. The Upaniṣadic verse runs as follows: nityo nityānāṁ cetanaś cetanānām. The purport is that amongst all the living entities, both conditioned and liberated, there is one supreme living personality, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, who maintains them and gives them all the facility of enjoyment according to different work. That Supreme Personality of Godhead is situated in everyone’s heart as Paramātmā. A wise man who can understand Him is eligible to attain perfect peace, not others.
