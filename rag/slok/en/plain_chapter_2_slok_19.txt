Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The Self, being eternal and unchanging, is neither killed nor does it kill anyone. Those who, on the destruction of the body, consider the Self to be dead, or consider it to be the killer, both fail to know the real nature of the Self, and engage in a futile dispute. 'It is the body that dies, and I am the killer' -- this notion belongs to the ego-bound individual (jiva). The conscious Self, which illumines both body and ego, is distinct from both. In short, the meaning is that the Self is neither the agent of any action nor the object of any action -- that is, no action whatsoever can be performed upon it.

How is the Self unchanging? The answer is given in the next verse.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
(In connection with 'avinashi tu tad viddhi') some say that the first half supports the meaning of the word 'tat,' while the second half serves to refute either atheism or the doctrine of transformation, and to show that the appearance of birth and the like in the Self is merely figurative -- hence the statement 'antavantah...' This too may be one valid approach. To refute the notion that the meaning of the Gita-scripture stated earlier rests on mere conjecture, the Lord cites two mantras (Upanishadic sayings); the connection of the two verses is shown, in 'shoka-moha-adi...' The connection of the first mantra is stated, in 'yat tu...' The objection is raised that, since this cognition rests on direct perception, its falsity is untenable, in 'katham...' The resolution given is that direct perception itself, being born of ignorance, is a mere semblance, and hence the cognition arising from it is not valid knowledge, in 'ya enam...'

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
The person who considers this indestructible embodied one to be the slayer, and the person who considers it slain, neither of them truly knows it; for it neither slays nor is slain.

Explanation -- 'Ya enam vetti hantaram' -- he who considers this embodied one to be the slayer does not know rightly. Because there is no agency in the embodied one. Just as no craftsman, however skilled, can accomplish anything without a tool, so too this embodied one can do nothing whatsoever by itself, without the body. Hence, in the thirteenth chapter, the Lord says that one who understands that all actions whatsoever are performed by Prakriti alone experiences the embodied one's non-agency (13.29). The sense is that there is no agency in the body, but the embodied one, through identifying with the body and forming a bond with it, comes to consider itself the agent of the actions performed through the body. If it does not form this bond with the body, it is the agent of no action whatsoever.

'Yash chainam manyate hatam' -- he who considers this to be slain, he too does not know rightly. Just as this embodied one is not the slayer, so too it is not the slain; because no change of any kind ever comes upon it. Only that which undergoes change, transformation -- that is, which is subject to origination and destruction -- can be slain.

'Ubhau tau na vijanito nayam hanti na hanyate' -- neither of the two truly knows, that is, he who considers this embodied one to be the slayer does not know rightly, and he who considers it to be the slain also does not know rightly.

Here the question arises: does the one who considers this embodied one both the slayer and the slain know rightly? The answer is that he too does not know rightly. Because this embodied one is in truth not so. It neither destroys nor is destroyed. It remains, without any modification, eternally and continuously exactly as it is. Hence one should not grieve on account of this embodied one.

Since the topic of war confronts Arjuna, the embodied one is here described as free of the actions of dying and killing; in truth, it is free of all actions whatsoever.

Connection -- why this embodied one does not die is answered in the following verse.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
With regard to "This" viz., the self, whose nature has been described above, he who thinks of It as the slayer, i.e., as the cause of slaying, and he who thinks 'This' (self) as slain by some cause or other - both of them do not know. As this self is eternal for the reasons mentioned above, no possible cause of destruction can slay It and for the same reason, It cannot be slain. Though the root 'han' (to slay) has the self for its object, it signifies causing the separation of the body from the self and not destruction of the self. Scriptural texts like 'You shall not cause injury to beings' and 'The Brahmana shall not be killed'?  (K. Sm. 8.2) indicate unsanctioned actions, causing separation of the body from the self. [In the above otes, slaughter in an ethical sense is referred to, while the text refers to killing or separating the self from the body in a metaphsyical sense. This is made explicit in the following verse].

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
'Ya enam...' Whoever views 'this,' that is, the Self and the body, as slayer and slain, has ignorance in him. That is why he is in bondage. The Lord clarifies this same point further:

'Antavantah...' These bodies, at an inexpressible time, meet their gross destruction, and this would be impossible unless they were subject to destruction at every moment, that is, unless they took on a different state at every instant. As it is said: 'by observing the withered state at the end, the loss of newness at every moment is inferred.' The sage too has said: 'the mutual difference among subtle time-parts, each serving a different purpose, occurs at every moment in all things, but owing to its subtlety this is not clearly grasped' (Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Moksha-dharma, ch. 308, v. 121). 'Having different purposes' means: because they perform different acts, each serving its own distinct purpose. The bodies have an end and are perishable; but the Self is eternal, because it is incomprehensible. Only what is comprehensible, that is, insentient, undergoes transformation; not what is non-insentient, of the nature of consciousness alone, since it cannot acquire an altogether different nature. Thus, the bodies always meet their end, and hence are unfit for grief; the Self always remains indestructible, and hence it too is unfit for grief. Thus, in the word 'ashochyan,' a single krtya-suffix has been employed by the sage to convey both senses simultaneously.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
'Ya enam...' Why is the Self's eternality stated yet again? Because the purpose here is not merely to establish the Self's eternality; rather, if the Self's eternality is settled, what then becomes of the ordinary practice of speaking of its slaying? -- anticipating this doubt, the delusive nature of that practice is stated here, in 'vyavaharas tu...' Here 'vyavahara' (practice) means cognition. Still, one might object that 'this neither slays nor is slain' is mere repetition; he answers: no, because the validity of cognitions is negated only by some sublating factor. By what sublating factor is this shown to be deluded? -- anticipating this, he says that the very reasons already stated in support of one's own position also serve, in their application, to sublate this notion, in 'kutah...'

One might object: here there is but a single act of slaying, so how can there be 'both'? And how can it be that 'this neither slays nor is slain'? The answer is that, just as the Self's eternality, its lack of independence too has already been stated earlier. As stated in the Tatparya-nirnaya: there, just as the practice of speaking of slaying is shown to be deluded, so too is the practice of speaking of independence -- thus 'both' and the rest is accounted for.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
He censures one who considers the Self to be the agent or object of the act of slaying, in 'ya enam...' The root 'han' (to slay) denotes only the causing of separation of the composite body; hence this Self neither slays nor is slain. The scriptural statement 'do not do violence to any being' too is fully accounted for from the standpoint of the body alone.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
One might object: though grief arising from separation from kinsmen like Bhishma has been removed by 'ashochyan anvashochas tvam...,' there is still no remedy for the sin arising from being the agent of their slaying. For it is not a rule that 'where there is no grief, there is no sin' -- otherwise, in the slaying of a hated Brahmin, where grief would not even be appropriate, the absence of sin would follow. Hence, since both 'I am the doer' and 'you are the instigator' would incur the sin caused by violence, the statement 'therefore fight, O Bharata' would be improper -- anticipating this doubt, the Lord resolves it with a verse recited in the Kathaka Upanishad. This embodied one, possessed of qualities such as invisibility, whoever considers to be the agent of the act of slaying -- thinking 'I am its slayer' -- and whoever else considers it the object of the act of slaying -- thinking 'by the destruction of the body, I am slain' -- both of these, owing to identification with the body, do not know, through scripture or through discernment, this unmodified Self, whose nature is to be no agent at all. Why? Because it neither slays nor is slain -- that is, it is neither agent nor object.

Here, though it would have sufficed to say merely 'he who knows this as slayer' and 'as slain,' the repetition of terms serves as an ornament of the sentence. Or, he who considers this the slayer is the logician and the like, who accepts the Self's agency; and he who considers it the slain is the Charvaka and the like, who accepts the Self's destructibility.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Thus the grief occasioned by the death of Bhishma and the rest has been dispelled. And the sorrow that was expressed on account of the Self's being the slayer -- in 'I do not wish to slay these...' and the like -- is, in the same way, likewise groundless, he says, in 'ya enam...' 'Enam' means the Self. Neither the status of object nor of agent in the act of slaying belongs to the Self -- this is the sense. He states the reason for this, in 'na ayam...'

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
To dispel Arjuna's false notion that 'I am their slayer, they will be slain by me,' the Lord cites a verse (a saying from the Kathaka Upanishad), in 'ya...' The doubt is this: though grief arising from separation from kinsmen has been dispelled by 'ashochyan...,' there remains no remedy for the sin arising from being their slayer, since sin persists in the slaying of a hated Brahmin even where there is no grief. Hence both I, the agent, and you, the instigator, would incur the sin caused by violence -- so the statement 'tasmat...' would be improper; this doubt is dispelled by this verse recited in the Kathaka Upanishad. This should be considered. He who knows this Self to be the agent of the act of slaying, and he who knows it to be the object of that act, both are deluded, not knowing, owing to their identification of the Self with the body. For this Self, being distinct from the body, being unmodified, is neither the agent nor the object of the act of slaying -- this is the sense.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Now, that which was stated in the verse 'avinashi tu...' (2.17), and which will be elaborated in 'nainam chindanti...' (2.23) -- that both weapons and the like, and the Self, are unfit to be, respectively, the slayer and the slain -- this is here confirmed through censure of one who holds the contrary view, in 'ya enam...' The word 'hantaram' is formed from the root 'han' by the suffix 'trich,' and hence its being masculine is accounted for precisely by the rule prohibiting the genitive with such derived agent-nouns. In saying 'hanana-hetum,' the intent of the suffix here is merely to indicate the cause.

Here the doubt arises: is not the object of the root 'han' the Self itself, as is clear from usages like 'wishes to kill me'? -- hence violence pertains only when the conscious Self is taken as the object of the root 'han.' If so, the statement 'this neither slays nor is slain' would itself become incoherent. To this he answers: the root 'han' does indeed take the Self as its grammatical object, but this does not signify the Self's departure from its own nature; rather, it signifies only 'slaying,' that is, separation from the body and the like, as is seen in its usage both in ordinary speech and in the Vedas. Hence the scriptural injunction 'do not do violence to any being' too is fully accounted for as a prohibition of causing separation from the body; in sanctioned separation from the body (as in sacrificial animals or the slaying of an enemy), there remains no element of violence at all.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
One might ask: though the individual soul is indeed a part of the Lord, how then does its slaying occur? He answers, in 'ya enam...': he who considers this to be the slayer, and he who considers it slain, both do not know. This neither slays nor is slain; everything occurs by My will alone -- this is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
One might object: by the maxim 'the unreal has no being,' since the unreal sense-organs and the like are false and hence have no true nature at all, agency in them is impossible. Hence agency, and the status of being subject to bondage and liberation, must be attributed to the real (the Self) alone. Otherwise, bondage would reside in the inner faculty and liberation in the Self -- and thus the two would rest on different substrates. And by the statement 'by which all this is pervaded,' the real (sat) alone has been declared the material cause of the body and the rest. Thus, with regard to the act of slaying, the Self itself would come to have both agency and objecthood, which is contradictory, since an action cannot be performed upon oneself -- fire does not burn fire; this would not be proper -- anticipating this doubt, he says, in 'ya enam...': whoever, like the logicians, considers this Self to be the agent of the act of slaying, and whoever, like the Charvakas, considers it the object of that act, both do not know the true nature of the Self -- this is the remaining sense. Because this neither slays nor is slain. That which is the agent is not the Self, nor is the body the Self, since both have already been determined to be not-Self. The sense is this: just as in a lump of iron, the capacity to burn comes only through its contact with fire, not of itself, so too agency, arising together with the sense-organs and the like, is a property of the sense-organs and the like alone, not of the Self. In the Self, the appearance of agency arises only through its connection with the sense-organs and the like. Hence bondage belongs only to the Self as qualified by the sense-organs and the rest (that is, conditioned by limiting adjuncts), not to the Self alone.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Neither he who thinks the living entity the slayer nor he who thinks it slain is in knowledge, for the self slays not nor is slain.

 When an embodied living entity is hurt by fatal weapons, it is to be known that the living entity within the body is not killed. The spirit soul is so small that it is impossible to kill him by any material weapon, as will be evident from subsequent verses. Nor is the living entity killable, because of his spiritual constitution. What is killed, or is supposed to be killed, is the body only. This, however, does not at all encourage killing of the body. The Vedic injunction is mā hiṁsyāt sarvā bhūtāni: never commit violence to anyone. Nor does understanding that the living entity is not killed encourage animal slaughter. Killing the body of anyone without authority is abominable and is punishable by the law of the state as well as by the law of the Lord. Arjuna, however, is being engaged in killing for the principle of religion, and not whimsically.
