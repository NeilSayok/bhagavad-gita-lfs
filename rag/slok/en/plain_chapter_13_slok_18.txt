Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Brahman alone is that one Consciousness-light, by which all intellectual knowledge, intuition, and experience are illumined. Because of It alone do we become aware of our various knowledges and experiences; therefore, It is compared to light or illumination. Just because an object is before our eyes, we cannot see it unless it is also illuminated by some external light. Taking this worldly experience as an analogy, we must also accept that there must be some inner light to illuminate our internal feelings and thoughts; otherwise, we could not have any awareness of these mental modifications. This illuminator of the modifications of the mind is called the self-luminous Atman, or the Light of the Self. To call this Consciousness 'light' is the tradition of spiritual science.
In the early period of Vedanta study, being unacquainted with the technical language of the scriptures, seeking aspirants understand the word 'light' to mean only physical light. But this concept is not correct, because physical light belongs to the category of the seen (object), whereas the Atman is the Seer of all (subject). Therefore, the seen light cannot be the Atman, nor can the Atman be like this light. Hence, it becomes necessary for the Guru to clarify the real significance of words like 'Light of the Self' or 'Atman-light'.
The Light of lights: To indicate the Seer, one must first negate the entire category of the seen; that is, one must prove that they are not the Atman. Among the sources of light known to us—sun, moon, stars, electricity, and fire—none has the capacity to illuminate the Atman. Before It, all these become lustreless. Therefore, Lord Krishna calls that Atman the 'Light of lights', which illumines even all the physical, visible lights. We would not even be aware of this sun, which is said to be self-luminous, if the Consciousness-element were not illumining it. We are affected by the pleasures and pains of the world only when we are aware of them. And this awareness is possible only through the light of Consciousness. Therefore, Consciousness is said to be the illuminator of the entire category of the seen.
It is beyond darkness: Even after explaining so much, because the impression of physical light is extremely strong in the disciple's intellect, he again grasps it only as a relative concept of light. We know and understand external light only as the opposite of darkness. The word 'light' has no meaning for the sun, because the sun does not know darkness at all. Therefore, to indicate the absolute Consciousness-nature of the Atman, it is said here that It is beyond even the concept of darkness.
This light of Consciousness is so subtle that it illumines both light and darkness. It has no opposition with anything. One meaning of the Lord's statement can also be that the Atman is that light which is the illuminator of both the modifications of our mind—knowledge (light) and ignorance (darkness)—yet It itself remains untouched by both.
In the second line of this verse, the Atman or Brahman alone is indicated by three words: Knowledge, the Knowable, and the Goal of Knowledge (attainable by knowledge). That Brahman is Knowledge, i.e., of the nature of Consciousness. Brahman alone is the knowable object (jneyam), because only by Its knowledge can the world of transmigration be terminated. This Brahman is 'attainable by knowledge' (jnanagamyam), meaning it is accessible to experience through a pure mind endowed with virtues like humility (amanitvan).
It is seated in the hearts of all: If there is such an infinite Consciousness-element which is the illuminator of all, and without which life has no existence at all, then it is certainly worth knowing. To attain It alone can be the goal of our life. Where should we search for It? Which pilgrimage should we go on? Are we capable of such an adventurous journey? Usually, people ask such questions, which shows that they think of the Atman as something different from themselves, which they assume is to be attained in some other place or time. To remove all such contrary notions, a clear and bold declaration is made here that the infinite Supreme Self is situated in the hearts of all.
Philosophically, the word 'heart' refers to the pure mind, which is considered the seat of all noble and holy emotions. In this atmosphere of inner purity, when the intellect meditates on that supreme Atman, which is transcendent yet all-pervasive, it itself becomes of the nature of the Atman. This is self-realization. Therefore, the heart is considered the dwelling place of the Atman. The experience of the Atman situated in one's own heart is the experience of the infinite Brahman, because the Atman is Brahman.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
That Supreme Divine is said to be the Light of all lights and entirely beyond ignorance. He is of the nature of knowledge, the knowable, attainable through knowledge (the set of means), and is seated in the hearts of all.

Explanation: -- jyotiṣāmapi tajjyotiḥ -- Jyoti is the name of light (knowledge), that is, all those from which light is obtained or knowledge is gained, are lights. Physical objects are seen in the light of the sun, moon, constellations, stars, fire, electricity, etc.; hence the light (illuminator) of physical objects is the sun, moon, etc. The knowledge of literal and acoustic words is attained through the ear; hence the ear is the light (illuminator) of sound. The knowledge of the touch of cold-hot, soft-hard, etc., is attained through the skin; hence the skin is the light (illuminator) of touch. The knowledge of forms like white, blue, yellow, etc., is attained through the eyes; hence the eye is the light (illuminator) of form. The knowledge of tastes like sour, sweet, salty, etc., is attained through the tongue; hence the tongue is the light (illuminator) of taste. The knowledge of fragrance-foul smell is attained through the nose; hence the nose is the light (illuminator) of smell. The knowledge of the five objects like sound etc., through these five senses happens only when the mind is present with those senses. If the mind is not with them, then no object is known. Therefore, the mind is the light (illuminator) of the senses. Even when there is knowledge of objects through the mind, unless the intellect is applied to it and stays with the mind, clear and permanent knowledge of that object does not arise. Only by the association of the intellect does clear and permanent knowledge of that object arise. Therefore, the intellect is the light (illuminator) of the mind. Even after gaining the knowledge of duty-non-duty, real-unreal, eternal-temporary from the intellect, if the self (doer) does not assimilate it, then it remains merely intellectual knowledge; that knowledge does not manifest in life and conduct. That truth is not established in the self. The truth which is established in the self never leaves. Therefore, the self is the light (illuminator) of the intellect. The self also is a part of the Supreme Self, and the Supreme Self is the whole. In the self, knowledge and light come from the Supreme Self alone. Therefore, the Supreme Self is the light (illuminator) of the self. No one can illumine that self-luminous Supreme Self. The implication is that the light (knowledge) of the Supreme Self comes into the self. The light of the self comes into the intellect, the light of the intellect into the mind, the light of the mind into the senses, and the light of the senses into the objects. In the root, light in all of these comes from the Supreme Self alone. Therefore, the Supreme Self alone is the Light of all these lights, the Illuminator of all illuminators (Footnote p. 692). Just as examinees sitting one behind another can see those sitting in front of them, but not those sitting behind them; in the same way, the ego, intellect, mind, senses, etc., can see (know) what is in front of them, but not what is behind them. Just as the examinee sitting at the very back can see all the examinees sitting in front of him; in the same way, the supreme illuminator, the Supreme Self, sees and illumines the ego, intellect, mind, senses, etc., but no one can illumine Him. That Supreme Self is the equal and absolute illuminator of the entire moving and non-moving world -- yasya bhāsā sarvamidaṁ vibhāti sacarācaram (Srimad Bhagavatam 10.13.55). There, the triad of illuminator, illumination, and the illuminated does not exist.

tamasaḥ paramucyate -- That Supreme Self is completely beyond ignorance, i.e., entirely unrelated and detached. In the senses, mind, intellect, and ego, both knowledge and ignorance come and go, but in that Supreme Self, who is the supreme illuminator of all, ignorance never enters, can never enter, and its entry is not even possible. Just as darkness never enters the sun, in the same way, ignorance never enters that Supreme Self. Therefore, that Supreme Self is said to be entirely beyond ignorance.

jñānaṁ jñeyaṁ jñānagamyam -- Ignorance never enters that Supreme Self. He Himself is of the nature of knowledge, and everyone receives light from Him alone. Therefore, that Supreme Self is called 'Jnanam', i.e., of the nature of knowledge. Through the senses, mind, intellect, etc., the objects (which come to be known) are also known, but they are not the ones that must be known, because even after knowing them, something remains to be known, knowledge is not complete. In reality, the only one worth knowing is the Supreme Self -- avasi dekhiahiṁ dekhana jogū (Ramacharitamanas 1.229.3). After knowing that Supreme Self, nothing else remains to be known. In the fifteenth chapter, the Lord has said about Himself that 'I alone am to be known by all the Vedas' (15.15) and 'he who knows Me becomes the knower of all' (15.19). Therefore, the Supreme Self is called 'Jneyam' (the Knowable). In this chapter, from the seventh to the eleventh verses, the means like 'amanitvam' (humility) etc., which have been described under the name of knowledge—when the unreal is abandoned through that knowledge, the Supreme Self can be known in truth. Therefore, that Supreme Self is called 'Jnanagamyam' (attainable by knowledge).

hṛdi sarvasya viṣṭhitam -- That Supreme Self is eternally and constantly seated in the hearts of all. The meaning is that although that Supreme Self is fully pervasive in all space, time, object, person, event, circumstance, state, etc., yet His place of realization is indeed the heart. The way to experience that Supreme Self in one's own heart is: -- (1) When a person knows any object, then in that knowledge both the real (sat) and the unreal (asat) are present. To separate these two, the seeker should experience that although my states of waking, dreaming, deep sleep, and childhood, youth, old age, etc., were different, I remained one. Pleasurable-painful, favorable-unfavorable circumstances came and went, but in them I remained the same one. The union and separation of space, time, object, person, etc. occurred, but in them also I remained the same one. The meaning is that although states, circumstances, union-separation were different (of various kinds), yet the one who has remained the same in all of them, and has not changed, should be experienced (by separating from all of them). By doing so, one will experience Him who is seated in the hearts of all, because this self is identical with the Supreme Self. (2) Just as an extremely hungry person cannot live without food and an extremely thirsty person cannot live without water, in the same way, one cannot live without that Supreme Self and becomes restless. Without Him, one should feel neither hunger nor thirst nor get sleep. The mental modification should go nowhere except to that Supreme Self. If one becomes restless to attain the Supreme Self in this manner, then one will experience that Supreme Self in one's own heart. In this way, once the Supreme Self is experienced in the heart, the seeker experiences that the Supreme Self alone is everywhere. This is the real experience.

Connection: -- The description of the field (Kshetra), knowledge, and the knowable that has taken place from the first to the seventeenth verses, is now summarized along with its fruit in the next verse.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
'jyotiṣām...' -- The spiritual light, which is the source of all illuminating conscious entities, is the illuminator. That is why it is said: 'The pure essence of the conscious self is like a jewel on His neck' (Srimad Bhagavatam 3.28.28). 'Beyond darkness' (tamasah param) means beyond Prakriti, as is also stated in the Sruti: 'There was darkness; in the beginning all this was covered by darkness' (Rigveda 10.129.3). He is the Indweller (Antaryamin), which is clear from the statement: 'seated in the heart of all' (hrdi sarvasya dhisthitam). That by which anything is known is 'knowledge' (jnanam), which refers to consciousness or the means of knowledge like humility (amanitva) etc. That same entity is the knowable (jneyam), attainable by knowledge (jnanagamyam), and is seated in the heart of all.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Objection: If it is present everywhere but not perceived, then it must be inert (jada).
Answer: No, because even though it is self-luminous, it is not graspable by the senses due to being devoid of form, etc. Thus, the Lord says 'jyotiṣāmapi...'. That knowable Brahman is the light that illumines even the illuminators, such as the sun etc. externally, and the intellect etc. internally. This is because the light of consciousness is capable of illumining inert lights, and also from Srutis like: 'Illumined by whose light the sun shines', 'By its light all this shines variously', and as the Lord will say in 'That light in the sun...' (15.12).
Lest it be thought that though not inert itself, it might be associated with the inert, the Lord says: 'beyond darkness' (tamasah param). It is beyond the inert category of darkness (Tamas); it is the absolute Brahman untouched by ignorance (Avidya) and its effects, which are unreal, because there can be no connection between the real and the unreal. This is stated by the Sruti: 'higher than the high Imperishable' and by the knowers of Brahman. As is said: 'A real connection of the Self, which is unattached, with the non-self, which is attached, and of the immutable with the mutable, is not possible', and from the Sruti: 'I know that Supreme Person of the effulgence of the sun, beyond darkness'. 'Of the effulgence of the sun' (aditya-varnam) means that it does not depend on another light for its own illumination; it is the illuminator of all.
Because it is self-luminous and untouched by the inert, it is 'knowledge' (jnanam)—the consciousness expressed through the modifications of the mind produced by valid means of knowledge. Therefore, it alone is 'the knowable' (jneyam), fit to be known, because only the unknown can be known; whereas the inert, being devoid of unknownness, is unfit to be known.
If so, why is it not known by all? To this the Lord says: 'attainable by knowledge' (jnanagamyam). It is to be reached or attained through the set of means described earlier beginning with humility (amanitva) and ending with reflection on the goal of knowledge of truth, which are called 'knowledge' because they lead to knowledge; meaning it cannot be attained without them.
Objection: If it is to be attained by means, is it separated by space?
Answer: No. 'seated in the heart of all' (hrdi sarvasya dhisthitam). It is seated in the heart, i.e., in the intellect of all living beings. Though present everywhere generally, it is specially present there in an manifested state as the individual soul (jiva) and the indweller (antaryamin), just as the solar light is reflected in a mirror or sun-stone. It is really unseparated from oneself, but appears separated due to delusion, and is attained, as it were, by the cessation of ignorance, which is the cause of all delusion.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Furthermore, 'jyotiṣām...' -- That is the Light which illumines even the lights like the moon, sun, etc. Because from Srutis like: 'Illumined by whose light the sun shines', 'There the sun does not shine, nor the moon and stars, nor do these lightnings shine, much less this fire; when He shines, everything shines after Him, by His light all this is illumined'. For this very reason, He is said to be beyond darkness (Tamas), untouched by ignorance, as in the Sruti: 'I know that Supreme Person of the effulgence of the sun, beyond darkness'. He is 'knowledge' (jnanam)—the consciousness expressed through the modifications of the intellect; He is 'the knowable' (jneyam) in the form of forms etc. (when objectified); and He is 'attainable by knowledge' (jnanagamyam)—meaning He is to be attained by the previously described means of knowledge characterized by humility, modesty, etc. He qualifies 'attainable by knowledge' by saying 'seated in the heart of all' (hrdi visthitam)—meaning He is present in the heart of all creatures, specially as the Inner Ruler (Niyantr) without deviating from His true nature. If the reading is 'dhisthitam', it means presiding over (adhisthaya) and remaining there.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
To remove the delusion that if the Knowable is present everywhere but not perceived, then it must be darkness, the Lord says 'jyotiṣām...'. It is the Light of lights, namely of the sun etc. and also of the intellect etc., because they shine by being enkindled by the light of consciousness of the Self. This is known from Srutis and Smritis like: 'Illumined by whose light the sun shines', 'There the sun does not shine, nor the moon and stars, nor do these lightnings shine, much less this fire; when He shines, everything shines after Him, by His light all this is illumined', and 'That light in the sun which illumines the whole universe, and that which is in the moon and in the fire, know that light to be Mine' (Gita 15.12). Even though the Knowable is of the nature of Light, to ward off the delusion of its being touched by darkness, the Lord says: 'beyond darkness' (tamasah param). It is spoken of as untouched by ignorance, as declared by Srutis like 'I know that Supreme Person of the effulgence of the sun, beyond darkness'.
Furthermore, that by which things are known is 'knowledge' (jnanam), namely humility (amanitva) etc. The Knowable which was spoken of in 'I shall speak of that which is to be known' (13.12) is referred to as 'attainable by knowledge' (jnanagamyam), because after being known, it is the result of knowledge. But when it is an object to be known, it is called 'the knowable' (jneyam). Thus, there is no tautology between the words 'jneyam' and 'jnanagamyam'. These three—Knowledge, Knowable, and Attainable by Knowledge—specially exist (visthitam) in the heart, i.e., in the intellect, of all creatures. The reading 'dhisthitam' is rejected by the Acharyas (Shankaracharya) and is a corrupt reading. This statement 'seated in the heart' (hrdi visthitam) is spoken by the Lord to console Arjuna, who might be despondent thinking that knowledge etc. is difficult to acquire, or to reveal the presence of knowledge etc., because they are experienced in the intellect itself.
If it is asked: 'Why did the Acharya (Shankaracharya) not explain that the consciousness expressed through the modifications of the mind is knowledge, and when objectified in the form of forms etc. it is the knowable, which exists in the intellect of all creatures generally and specially as the individual soul and Inner Controller?' We reply: because the meaning explained by us is easily understandable and has a greater flavor, and also because that meaning is already included in statements like 'without and within all beings' (13.16). The manner of inclusion is to be understood thus: external to the elements it is the external forms, internal to beings it exists in the heart as the manifested individual soul and Inner Controller, and it is the Light of lights which illumines the intellect etc. in the form of consciousness expressed through mental modifications.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Objection: How can the self, which is of the nature of self-illumination, be the illuminator of gems, lamps, sun, etc.?
Answer: To this, the Lord says 'dipadityadinam api...'. Just as lamps, sun, etc., illuminate through their radiating light (prabha) which contacts the objects, and not directly by their own form; similarly, the self also illuminates through its attribute of knowledge, which stands in the position of radiating light. This is the sense. The force of the word 'api' (even) in 'even of other illuminators' indicates that the self is the locus of knowledge that illumines these, just as it does for the illuminated objects like pots etc. By the term 'conjunction of senses with objects', the collection of materials or the contact between them is intended, or the light arising from it is indicated. For deep darkness (santamasa) is not an obstacle to sensory contact like a wall; otherwise, a person standing in deep darkness would not be able to perceive objects placed in a light that is separated by deep darkness, just as one cannot see objects behind a wall. By this, it is indicated that the illumination of lamps etc. is limited only to visible physical objects and is not all-pervasive like knowledge. 'By that much only'—meaning not by directly producing consciousness.
To discard the idea that the word 'tamas' (darkness) here refers to physical darkness, which is discarded because the self's being beyond physical darkness is already obtained by a fortiori logic (since it is the illuminator of all lights), and because the self's transcendence of Prakriti must be spoken here, the commentator says: 'the word Tamas...'. This is due to the abundance of scriptural and Smriti usages of the word 'Tamas' in the sense of primordial nature (Mula-Prakriti), such as: 'He whose body is Tamas' (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.7.13), 'Darkness was there, all this was covered by darkness in the beginning' (Rigveda 10.129.3), 'When there is no darkness' (Svetasvatara Upanishad 4.18), 'Tamas becomes one in the Supreme Lord' (Subala Upanishad 2), and 'This universe was in the form of darkness' (Manusmriti 1.5). 'param' means other or different. Or it means the primary one as the enjoyer. Indeed, even earlier, the two natures (Prakriti and Purusha) were divided as higher and lower. 'spoken of'—namely in statements like 'He is without attributes, beyond Prakriti'. By this, the distinction of the self from the inert is intended, which is why the commentator says 'therefore...'. To enable the investigation of its difference as before, the word 'jneyam' (knowable) is used; otherwise, it would be a repetition. Therefore, the commentator says: 'that whose sole form is knowledge is jneyam'. In 'jnanagamyam' (attainable by knowledge), since merely stating that it is the object of common knowledge is useless and would repeat the sense of 'jneyam', and since the word 'gamya' is well-known as a synonym of 'attainable', the commentator speaks of what is connected with Prakriti, saying 'by humility (amanitva) etc.'. Just like 'this is knowledge' (13.11), here too the instrumental derivation of the word is shown by the words 'by the stated means of knowledge'. 'of man etc.'—meaning of the physical body. Remaining in the form of the enjoyer (bhoktr) is the 'special remaining'. Or, remaining in the heart in its essential form, and in other limbs through its attribute of knowledge, is the special feature. Since the word 'sthiti' (existence/remaining) cannot be taken in its primary sense here, it is explained as mere presence. Just as in the statement 'this is the same rice in everyone's house', the singular number 'in the heart of all' is used to denote the generic unity of the class (jati-aikya).

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Furthermore, 'jyotiṣām...' -- That same Light is the illuminator of even the lights like the sun, moon, etc., which are otherwise seen as illuminating. The idea here is: as the Sruti 'There the sun does not shine...' (Kathopanishad 5.15, Svetasvatara Upanishad 6.14, Mundaka Upanishad 2.2.10) speaks of the non-shining of these lights there, this would render their creation useless; therefore, He illumines them and thereby causes their beauty etc. Otherwise, their capacity to illuminate all other things would not exist. If so, then will the primary darkness itself be the object of illumination for all? To this the Lord says: 'beyond darkness' (tamasah param). It is spoken of as higher or superior to even the primary darkness. That is why the Sruti also says: 'Darkness was covered by darkness in the beginning' (Rigveda 10.129.3). If He is self-luminous and exists in manifold forms in all, why is He not known by all? To this the Lord says: 'knowledge' (jnanam). He is the consciousness expressed through the modifications of the intellect. Therefore, wherever He wishes to reveal Himself, He manifests in that form. Similarly, 'jneyam' (the knowable) means He manifests in the form of the knowable. Nevertheless, He is attainable only through the home of the Supreme Person (Purushottama); hence the Lord says: 'attainable by knowledge' (jnanagamyam). He is to be reached or attained through knowledge, i.e., by the previously described path of knowledge; by this, His nature as the Imperishable (Akshara) is indicated. If His nature as knowledge was previously said to make Him inaccessible to all, how is He attainable by knowledge? To this the Lord says: 'seated in the heart of all' (hrdi dhisthitam)—meaning presiding over (adhisthitam) the heart of all living entities. He remains there as the Inner Controller of all; hence where He so wills, He manifests in the form of knowledge, and where He does not wish to reveal Himself, He acts as a cover (veiling power). This is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Having thus spoken of the accidental (accidental/associated) characteristics of the Knowable, the Lord now states its essential characteristic, saying 'jyotiṣām...'. That knowable Brahman is the light that illumines even the lights, namely the external ones like the sun etc. and the internal ones like the intellect etc. which otherwise illumine other things. This is because the light of consciousness is capable of illumining inert lights. And so say the Srutis: 'Illumined by whose light the sun shines', 'By His light all this is illumined', and the Lord will say 'That light in the sun...' (15.12). It is said to be 'beyond darkness' (tamasah param), meaning far away from ignorance, which is the cause of the creation and dissolution of creatures.
Objection: Just as in astronomy it is well-known that the illuminator of the moon's light is the solar light, which is of the same class (luminous); similarly, is there some extraordinary light of the same class that illumines the solar and other lights?
Answer: Anticipating this, the Lord says: 'knowledge' (jnanam). That light which is of the nature of pure consciousness alone, and not physical, is the knowable entity, which is the most desired object to be attained by knowledge because it is veiled. From where does that knowledge arise? To this the Lord says: 'attainable by knowledge' (jnanagamyam). Because it is to be reached or attained through the means of knowledge like humility (amanitva) etc.
Is it then separated by space like another village, or separated by time like another state of childhood, youth, etc.? To this the Lord says: 'seated in the heart of all' (hrdi sarvasya visthitam). The meaning is that it is one's own Self, which shines perfectly for those whose vision is turned inward.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
He is the source of light in all luminous objects. He is beyond the darkness of matter and is unmanifested. He is knowledge, He is the object of knowledge, and He is the goal of knowledge. He is situated in everyone’s heart.

 The Supersoul, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, is the source of light in all luminous objects like the sun, moon and stars. In the Vedic literature we find that in the spiritual kingdom there is no need of sun or moon, because the effulgence of the Supreme Lord is there. In the material world that brahma-jyotir, the Lord’s spiritual effulgence, is covered by the mahat-tattva, the material elements; therefore in this material world we require the assistance of sun, moon, electricity, etc., for light. But in the spiritual world there is no need of such things. It is clearly stated in the Vedic literature that because of His luminous effulgence, everything is illuminated. It is clear, therefore, that His situation is not in the material world. He is situated in the spiritual world, which is far, far away in the spiritual sky. That is also confirmed in the Vedic literature. Āditya-varṇaṁ tamasaḥ parastāt ( Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad 3.8). He is just like the sun, eternally luminous, but He is far, far beyond the darkness of this material world. His knowledge is transcendental. The Vedic literature confirms that Brahman is concentrated transcendental knowledge. To one who is anxious to be transferred to that spiritual world, knowledge is given by the Supreme Lord, who is situated in everyone’s heart. One Vedic mantra ( Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad 6.18) says, taṁ ha devam ātma-buddhi-prakāśaṁ mumukṣur vai śaraṇam ahaṁ prapadye. One must surrender unto the Supreme Personality of Godhead if he at all wants liberation. As far as the goal of ultimate knowledge is concerned, it is also confirmed in Vedic literature: tam eva viditvāti mṛtyum eti. “Only by knowing Him can one surpass the boundary of birth and death.” ( Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad 3.8) He is situated in everyone’s heart as the supreme controller. The Supreme has legs and hands distributed everywhere, and this cannot be said of the individual soul. Therefore that there are two knowers of the field of activity – the individual soul and the Supersoul – must be admitted. One’s hands and legs are distributed locally, but Kṛṣṇa’s hands and legs are distributed everywhere. This is confirmed in the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad (3.17): sarvasya prabhum īśānaṁ sarvasya śaraṇaṁ bṛhat. That Supreme Personality of Godhead, Supersoul, is the prabhu, or master, of all living entities; therefore He is the ultimate shelter of all living entities. So there is no denying the fact that the Supreme Supersoul and the individual soul are always different.
