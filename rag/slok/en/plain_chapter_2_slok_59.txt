Life Application:
Cultivate higher spiritual fulfillment so that unhealthy habits and cravings naturally drop away without forced struggle.

Commentary by Swami Tejomayananda:
For an embodied being who abstains from taking in sense-objects, the objects do withdraw, but the attachment (liking) for them does not. Only when this person perceives the Supreme Reality does that attachment too cease.

Commentary by Swami Sivananda:
The objects of the senses turn away from the abstinent man leaving the longing (behind); but his longing also turns away on seeing the Supreme.

Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:
The objects of sense turn from him who is abstemious. Even the relish for them is lost in him who has seen the Truth.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Without the inner capacity of withdrawal (pratyahara), a person may sometimes give up enjoyment of objects because of illness, a passing wave of grief, or a vow such as fasting. At such times it looks as if dispassion or aversion toward objects has arisen, but the underlying attachment (raga) in the mind merely remains dormant for a while. Arjuna's doubt here is whether a yogi's sense-restraint might likewise be only temporary, liable to break down under favourable or tempting circumstances; this verse removes that doubt.

The principle can be seen clearly by watching how goods move from shops to customers: an item reaches only the home of someone who actively desires and strives for it. A wine-shop empties only because drinkers who crave liquor carry the bottles away; a plough made by a blacksmith goes only to a farmer's house, never to an artist's, a poet's, a physician's or a lawyer's. In the same way, objects of enjoyment come only to those who long for them, and stay away from one who has renounced such longing.

By abstention (niraharatva) the objects themselves go away, but the taste or liking (rasa) born of past experience does not thereby cease. The Lord assures here that when direct realisation of the Supreme Self dawns, this taste too disappears - objects become as powerless over the mind as roasted seeds are incapable of sprouting.

This is not hard to understand, since we know that what causes distress in one state of experience need not persist as such in another: a kingdom enjoyed in a dream does not relieve my poverty in the waking state, yet my waking poverty cannot deprive me of enjoying that dream-kingdom. Across the waking, dream and deep-sleep states the ego accumulates countless desires for objects; but when the ego itself dissolves upon recognising the pure consciousness that transcends all three states, on whom would these latent impressions then exert their influence?

A seeker who wishes to attain Self-knowledge must first bring the senses under control, for otherwise...

Commentary by Swami Adidevananda:
The objects of senses, excepting relish for the objects, turn away from the abstinent dweller in the body. Even the relish turns aswy from him when what is supreme over the senses i.e., the self, is seen.

Commentary by Swami Gambirananda:
The objects recede from an abstinent man, with the exception of the taste (for them). Even the taste of this person falls away after realization the Absolute.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
The Lord states in the verses that follow that this mark of steady wisdom does not come about without effort. Mere abstention (niraharatva) only removes the capacity to enjoy objects, or the desire for other objects - it does not remove the underlying craving (rasa/raga); that craving is removed only by direct realisation. This is confirmed by the Bhagavata (11.8.20): 'The wise quickly conquer their senses through abstention, except the sense of taste (rasa), for that alone still grows toward its object.' Here the word 'rasa' denotes attachment (raga).

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
A doubt is raised: even when the senses turn away from their objects, if the attachment (raga) to those objects still lingers, how can steady wisdom be attained? The verse explains the means of removing that attachment. Mere non-engagement with objects does not by itself remove the residual attachment. 'Niraharasya' is explained as 'of one who does not take in objects' - even such a person, if devoid of true knowledge and merely engaged in harsh austerity, may have the senses withdrawn from objects while attachment still remains; only knowledge of the ultimate Reality (tattva-jnana) uproots it. The word 'rasa' here does not mean the ordinary flavours (sweetness, etc.) but attachment itself, as shown by usages like 'svarasena' (by one's own inclination) and 'rasika' (one who follows his own taste). This attachment ceases through the realisation 'I am indeed That' - whereupon knowledge of objects becomes seedless, incapable of sprouting again. One might object that without direct realisation attachment cannot be removed, and without removing attachment realisation cannot arise - creating a vicious circle; but this is denied, because discrimination first removes the gross subjection of the senses to objects, and the resulting true knowledge then removes even the subtle attachment entirely, so no circularity is involved. Since the firmness of wisdom bears fruit only in this way, the conclusion is stated accordingly.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Even for one who withdraws the senses from objects (niraharasya), the objects do turn away, but the taste (rasa) for them does not. For the person of steady wisdom, however, even that taste turns away upon experiencing the Supreme Reality.

A person becomes 'without intake' in two ways: (1) by voluntarily giving up food, or through illness; and (2) by renouncing all sense-objects and sitting in solitude, i.e. withdrawing the senses from objects. Here the word 'niraharasya' refers specifically to the seeker who withdraws the senses from objects.

A sick person thinks, 'I cannot enjoy things now because of illness - that is a compulsion; but once I recover and regain strength, I will indulge again.' In this way the taste-mentality (rasa-buddhi) remains within him. Similarly, when the senses are withdrawn from objects, the objects themselves go away, but the seeker's inner sense of taste or pleasure in those objects does not quickly disappear.

For those who are naturally free of attachment to objects and possess intense dispassion, this taste-mentality ceases even during the stage of practice itself. But for those practising thoughtfully without such intense dispassion, it is said that even after giving up the objects, their taste-mentality does not cease.

'Raso'pyasya param drstva nivartate' - the taste-mentality of this person of steady wisdom ceases upon experiencing the Supreme Self. It is not a rule that the mere cessation of taste-mentality makes one a person of steady wisdom, but it is a rule that becoming a person of steady wisdom means the taste-mentality no longer remains.

The phrase 'raso'pyasya' implies that taste-mentality resides in the seeker's very sense of 'I'; this same taste-mentality later takes the gross form of attachment. Hence the seeker should root taste out of his own ego-sense, thinking, 'I am desireless; attachment and craving are not my concern.' With this desireless attitude - or with desirelessness as the goal - taste-mentality no longer remains, and upon experiencing the Supreme Reality, taste ceases altogether.

Connection: What is the harm if taste does not cease? This is explained in the next verse.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
The sense objects are the food of the senses. From the abstinent embodied being, i.e., from one who has withdrawn his senses from objects, these sense-objects, being rejected by him, turn away, but not the relish for them. Relish means hankering. The meaning is that the hankering for the sense-objects does not go away by abstinence alone. But even this hankering will go away, when one sees that the essential nature of the self is superior to the sense-objects and that the realisation of this self gives greater happiness than the enjoyment of sense-objects.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Although visayah, the objects, (i.e.) the organs, figuratively implied and expressed by the word 'objects', or, the objects themselves; vinivartante, recede; niraharasya dehinah, from an abstinent man, from an embodied being, even from a fool who engages in painful austerity and abstains from objects; (still, they do so) rasavarjam, with the exception of the taste (for them), with the exception of the hankering that one has for objects. The word rasa is well known as referring to the sense of taste (hankering), as in such expressions as, 'sva-rasena pravrttah, induced by his own taste (i.e. willingly)', 'rasikah, a man of tastes', 'rasajnah, a connoisseur (of tastes)', etc. Api, even that; rasah, taste of the nature of subtle attachment; asya, of this person, of the sannyasin; nivartate, falls away, i.e. his objective perception becomes seedless; when drstva, after attaining; param, the Absolute, the Reality which is the supreme Goal, Brahman, he continues in life with the realization, 'I verily am That (Brahman).'
In the absence of full realization there can be no eradication of the 'hankering'. The idea conveyed is that, one should therefore stabilize one's wisdom which is characterized by full realization. [If it be held that attachment cannot be eliminated without the knowledge of Brahman, and at the same time that the knowledge of Brahman cannot arise until attachment is eradicated, then we get involved in a vicious circle. In answer it is said that gross attachments are eliminated through discrimination which restrains the senses from being overpowered by objects. And the full Knowledge arising thereof eliminates the subtle inclinations as well. Hence there is no vicious circle involved.]

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Since the phrase 'his wisdom is established' occurs at the end of this set of three verses (2.61), one might mistakenly think this verse merely restates the defining mark of steady wisdom directly; to dispel that, it is clarified that this mark does not arise without great effort. If wisdom of this kind arose so easily, everyone would achieve it simply to escape the troubles caused by desire - but that is not observed, so it must not be a mark attainable without effort.

The word 'niraharatvena' is explained as 'by not taking in objects,' which explains the phrase 'rasavarjam': for one who does not take in objects, the objects themselves - here referring to the senses' capacity to enjoy them - withdraw, except for 'rasa,' i.e. attachment; leaving that aside, the mental craving for objects does not cease by mere non-partaking. An alternative reading takes 'akanksha' (longing) to include the capacities for enjoyment as well, so that 'rasavarjam' means 'excepting the longing for taste, etc.' On this reading: by non-partaking, objects other than taste - along with the capacities and longings to enjoy them - withdraw, but the mind's longing for the taste of objects, and the tongue's capacity to enjoy taste, do not withdraw.

'Raso'pi' is explained accordingly: on the first reading, 'rasa' is the attachment that has all objects as its scope; on the second, it is the capacity to enjoy, together with the longing for, such taste. This is confirmed by the Bhagavata verse cited: the wise swiftly conquer the senses through non-partaking, except for taste, which grows toward its object even then - showing that 'rasa' means attachment. Since taste is spoken of figuratively as though sentient, it is proper to say that 'rasa withdraws upon seeing the Supreme.'

The overall meaning: when the external senses contact objects, the mind proceeds toward them through that channel, and when the mind so proceeds, attachment arises, which agitates the Self - this is non-conquest of the senses, for one is said to be 'conquered' by whatever agitates him. But when the external senses, even in the presence of objects, do not make contact with them, and even if slight contact occurs the mind is not drawn toward them, and even if the mind is drawn no attachment to the objects arises - then there is no agitation of the Self; this is true conquest of the senses. Such conquest is of two kinds: through exhaustion of the external senses' capacity, and through exhaustion of the mind's attachment, achieved respectively through mere non-partaking and through direct realisation of Brahman. Hence, conquest of the senses is attainable only through great effort.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Objects withdraw only for one whose senses are truly withdrawn from objects through abstention - not for anyone else who merely abstains outwardly; and even then, sense-objects withdraw except for the 'taste.' The taste, i.e. the attachment, does not withdraw, because that faculty is the strongest of all and most compelling for the aspirant. The Lord then states the means of removing this taste: even the taste of this perfected, steady-minded person ceases upon experiencing the bliss of the Supreme Self, by the rule that no one continues to pursue a lesser pleasure once a greater bliss has been tasted. What follows describes the posture, speech, contemplation and vows characteristic of one established in the Self.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
An objection: even a fool may withdraw the senses from objects owing to illness, so how can steady wisdom be said to belong only to the wise? This verse answers: for an embodied, ego-identified fool too - whether sick, or a harsh ascetic - who does not draw objects in through the senses, the objects (sound etc.) do withdraw, but excepting 'rasa,' i.e. craving; that craving does not withdraw for the ignorant person - meaning the objects withdraw for the fool, but attachment to them does not. For the person of steady wisdom, however, having directly realised the Supreme Goal as 'I am That,' even the 'rasa' - attachment even to trivial pleasures - withdraws; the word 'api' (even) implies the objects too. This is explained similarly elsewhere. Thus the withdrawal of objects together with their attachment is the defining mark of the person of steady wisdom, and this does not wrongly apply to a mere fool. Since, without true realisation of the Supreme Self, attachment-laden objects cannot be uprooted, one should therefore strive with great effort to make firm the wisdom born of true realisation, which alone uproots such attachment.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
An objection: mere non-engagement of the senses with objects cannot be the defining mark of the person of steady wisdom, since the insentient, the sick, and those who are fasting show the very same non-engagement, without any real distinction. This verse answers: 'ahara' means the taking in of objects through the senses. For an embodied, ego-identified, ignorant person who does not take in objects through the senses, the objects - that is, the experience of them - do withdraw. But 'rasa,' meaning craving or longing, is excepted: the longing does not withdraw. For the person of steady wisdom, however, upon seeing the Supreme Self, even the 'rasa,' the attachment, withdraws on its own, that is, is destroyed. Alternatively: for one intent on fasting, objects mostly withdraw since, tormented by hunger, he has no interest in sound, touch and the like; but 'rasavarjam' means that the longing for taste specifically does not withdraw. The rest is as before.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
An objection: even a sick person or one under a vow of abstinence has the senses withdraw from objects, so this defining mark would wrongly apply even to fools. This is answered: 'objects' here figuratively means the senses, or else the objects such as sound themselves; these withdraw - except for 'rasa,' attachment - even for a foolish person engaged in harsh austerity, abstaining from food and not taking in objects; that is, the objects withdraw but the attachment does not. But upon directly realising the Supreme Self as 'I am Brahman,' even this subtle, colouring 'rasa' (attachment) of the ascetic withdraws, and his knowledge of objects becomes seedless, incapable of sprouting again. Therefore the wisdom born of true realisation, which uproots this attachment, should be made firm through great effort.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
An objection: since, in the absence of food, the senses do not engage with objects, why should such a fasting person too not be considered a person of steady wisdom? This verse answers: it is true that for the embodied being who abstains, objects do withdraw. But 'rasavarjam' means 'excepting rasa' - rasa here being the longing for the experience of those objects, and that longing remains. The word 'dehinah' (the embodied one) indicates that even such a person's identification with the body does not cease. But for the person of steady wisdom, even that very longing (rasa) withdraws upon seeing the supreme, most excellent divine relish. This is the distinguishing difference between an ordinary abstainer and the person of steady wisdom.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
An objection: if withdrawal of the senses from objects were the cause of steady wisdom, it would exist even in sleep, fainting, trance or possession, making everyone a person of steady wisdom. This is answered: it is true that for a foolish, ego-identified embodied being who, in sleep and the like, does not take in objects through the senses, the objects do indeed withdraw - except for 'rasa,' attachment, which does not withdraw; even then attachment persists subtly, since the root cause of attachment, ignorance of the Self, has not been burnt away, and so such a person is not one of steady wisdom. But for this same person, upon seeing the Supreme and directly realising the Self, even the 'rasa' - attachment to objects like sound - withdraws, because the root ignorance is burnt away. This is the great distinction between one in sleep and the like, and one established in samadhi. Other, earlier commentators explain it differently: for even a fool - a sick person or a harsh ascetic - who does not take in objects, the objects withdraw except for rasa; and for that same person, upon seeing the Supreme while so established, even the rasa withdraws.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Though the embodied soul may be restricted from sense enjoyment, the taste for sense objects remains. But, ceasing such engagements by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in consciousness.

 Unless one is transcendentally situated, it is not possible to cease from sense enjoyment. The process of restriction from sense enjoyment by rules and regulations is something like restricting a diseased person from certain types of eatables. The patient, however, neither likes such restrictions nor loses his taste for eatables. Similarly, sense restriction by some spiritual process like aṣṭāṅga-yoga, in the matter of yama, niyama, āsana, prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra, dhāraṇā, dhyāna, etc., is recommended for less intelligent persons who have no better knowledge. But one who has tasted the beauty of the Supreme Lord Kṛṣṇa, in the course of his advancement in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, no longer has a taste for dead, material things. Therefore, restrictions are there for the less intelligent neophytes in the spiritual advancement of life, but such restrictions are only good until one actually has a taste for Kṛṣṇa consciousness. When one is actually Kṛṣṇa conscious, he automatically loses his taste for pale things.
