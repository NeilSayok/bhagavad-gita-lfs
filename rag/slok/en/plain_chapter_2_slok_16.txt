Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
In Vedantic scripture, the discrimination of sat (the real) and asat (the unreal) has been made in an extremely scientific manner. Our philosophy gives clear definitions of both. The unreal is that which had no existence in the past, and will have none in the future, but which merely appears to exist in the present. In the language of the Mandukya Karika, whatever has no existence at its beginning and its end is also unreal in the present -- the things we perceive, though false, are nonetheless taken to be real.

The truly real, by its very nature, is that which remains eternal and unchanging across all three times -- past, present and future. In ordinary usage, if a person mistakes a post for a ghost, then from the post's standpoint the ghost is called unreal -- because the ghost is impermanent, and once the post is recognized, the ghost no longer remains there. Similarly, on waking from a dream, we feel no anxiety for the children seen in the dream, because on waking we realize the falsity of the dream. Even though it appeared real, the dream is false. Hence, only that which is unnegated across all three times is called real.

Our life, tied to the limiting adjuncts of body, mind and intellect, is itself limited, because the external objects, feelings and thoughts experienced through them are momentary. All three are undergoing constant change. The destruction of one state is the birth of another. By this very definition, all these are unreal.

Is there some real substance underlying them? There is no doubt that some single unchanging substratum is needed for the changes occurring in things. To hold together, in a single thread, the countless experiences occurring at the level of body, mind and intellect, and to give rise to the experience of one complete life, a substratum of some eternal, unchanging, real substance is certainly required.

Like a thread that holds beads together, there is something in us that, while remaining amid all changes, binds together our various experiences. On subtle reflection, we come to know that this something is our own conscious Self. Of the countless experiences that arise and shine forth, none is itself the Self. Life, which is a stream of experiences, is possible only because of this consciousness. It is this same consciousness that illumines the experiences occurring in childhood, youth and old age. Experiences come and go. The consciousness by which I have known everything, without which I myself would not exist -- that conscious Self is the eternal, real substance, free of birth and destruction.

The seers of Truth recognize the essential nature of both of these -- sat and asat, Self and not-Self. From the mysterious union of these two, this wondrous world arises.

Then what is that eternal, real substance? Listen...

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
It has been said that the Self is eternal. Is the Self alone eternal, or is something else eternal too? He says: 'na asatah...' Of the unmanifest cause (Prakriti) and of Being (Brahman), of neither is there non-existence. 'Both Prakriti and Purusha are eternal, and Time too is possessed of existence' -- this is the statement of the Vishnu Purana. The separate mention is for the sake of emphasis. That the unmanifest is itself a cause is also stated: 'possessed of the form of both sat and asat, made of the gunas, yet itself beyond the gunas, all-pervading' -- from the Bhagavata; and 'from asat, sat was born' is likewise said of the unmanifest (Prakriti). This is established also by tradition, he says: 'ubhayor api...' 'Anta' (finality) means determination.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
To further establish endurance (titikshutva) as a fitting qualification of one entitled to this teaching, by way of a second reason, he brings down the next verse. Cold, heat and the like, the causes of grief, being not-Self, have no real existence, whereas the Self, being unmodified and uniform, alone is real -- hence endurance is a fitting qualification for the seeker of liberation; this is the purpose of the verse. One might object that, though the effect (cold, heat, etc.) is unreal, its cause being real would prevent their utter non-existence -- he clarifies that what is denied here is the unreal 'together with its cause.'

This disputed duality (cold, heat and the like) is, like the illusory snake in a rope, inexpressible, being untrue and unauthoritative -- since perception and other means of valid knowledge can establish the mere existence of a substrate, but cannot determine its true nature, the object itself being difficult to ascertain with certainty. This duality, being subject to coming and going, is false, as is generally agreed. Examining whether the effect is different from or identical with its cause, both alternatives are shown to be flawed. In the end, it is established that the Self alone, by virtue of its unchanging, uniform nature, is real, while the dualities of cold, heat and the rest are false -- and it is precisely on the ground of this knowledge of the truth that patient endurance is shown to be proper.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
(Note p. 55) Of the unreal there is no being (existence), and of the real there is no non-being; the knowers of Truth have seen the finality, that is, the essential truth, of both these.

Explanation -- 'Na asato vidyate bhavah' -- the body did not exist before its origination, will not remain after death, and even in the present it is ceasing to exist moment by moment. The meaning is that this body never remains in a state of being, in past, future, or present -- hence it is unreal. In the same way, this world too has no being; it too is unreal. This body is but a small sample of the world, and so, by the body's transformation, one comes to experience the transformation of the entire world -- that this world had no being before, will have none after, and even now is in the process of non-being.

The whole world is continuously burning, like wood, in the fire of Time. When wood burns, at least charcoal and ash remain; but Time burns the world in such an extraordinary way that neither charcoal nor ash remains at all -- it renders the world an utter non-being. Hence it is said that the unreal has no being.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
'The unreal,' that is, the body, can never come into being. 'The real,' that is, the self, can never cease to be. The finale about these, the body and the self, which can be experienced, has been realised correctly by the seers of the Truth. As analyis ends in conclusion, the term 'finale' is here used. The meaning is this:  Non-existence (i.e., perishableness) is the real nature of the body which is in itself insentient. Existence (i.e., imperishableness) is the real nature of the self, which is sentient. [What follows is the justification of describing the body as 'unreal' and as having 'never come into being.']

Non-existence has, indeed, the nature of perishableness, and existence has the nature of imperishableness, as Bhagavan Parasara has said:  'O Brahmana, apart from conscious entity there does not exist any group of things anywhere and at any time. Thus have I taught you what is real existence - how conscious entity is real, and all else is unreal' (V. P., 2.12.43 - 45). 'The Supreme Reality is considered as imperishable by the wise. There is no doubt that what can be obtained from a perishable substance is also perishable' (Ibid., 2.14.24). 'That entity which even by a change in time cannot come to possess a difference through modification etc., is real. What is that entity, O King?  (It is the self who retains Its knowledge)' (Ibid., 2.13.100).

It is said here also:  'These bodies ৷৷. are said to have an end' (2.18) and 'Know That (the Atman) to be indestructible' (2.17). It is seen from this that this (i.e., perishableness of the body and imperishableness of the self) is the reason for the designating the Atman as 'existence' (Sattva) and body as 'non-existence' (Asvattva). This verse has no reference to the doctrine of Satkaryavada (i.e., the theory that effects are present in the cause), as such a theory has no relevance here. Arjuna is deluded about the true nature of the body and the self; so what ought to be taught to him in order to remove his delusion, is discrimination between these two - what is alified by perishablenss and what, by imperishableness. This (declaration) is introduced in the following way:  'For the dead, or for the living' (2.11).

Again this poin is made clear immediately (by the words), 'Know that to be indestructible ৷৷.' (2.17) and 'These bodies ৷৷. are said to have an end' (2.18).

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Since 'the unreal has no being,' etc., for this reason too it is proper to bear cold, heat and the rest without grief or delusion. Of the unreal, that is, of cold, heat and the rest along with their causes, there is no being -- because when examined by means of proof, cold, heat and the rest, and their causes, do not stand established as any real substance -- because they are all modifications, and whatever is a modification is by nature changeful. Just as the shape of a pot, when examined by the eye, yields nothing apart from clay and is thus unreal, so too every modification, being unobtainable apart from its cause, is unreal -- since none of them is found to exist prior to their arising nor after their destruction.

Objection: since clay and the like, as causes, and their own causes in turn, are likewise not found apart from their own causes, their non-existence too would be established, and so the non-existence of everything whatsoever would follow.

Answer: this is not correct, since everywhere two distinct cognitions -- the cognition of the real and the cognition of the unreal -- are found. That object regarding which the cognition never varies is real, and that regarding which the cognition varies is unreal. Thus the division between the real and the unreal rests upon cognition itself.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
In 'na tv eva aham...' (2.12), Lord Krishna, who placed even Himself among those to whom that teaching applied, now displays the tendency to raise an objection to that very point, in the word 'nitya' (eternal). Birthlessness alone had thus far been inferred as the reason. 'Well then, what follows?' he asks, in 'kim...' 'Beginningless' too must be included. On the first alternative, no illustration is available and hence the pervasion cannot be grasped; the second alternative, being unauthoritative, cannot even be put forward -- this is the opponent's intent. The proponent, understanding this intent, and holding that whatever is possible through direct concomitance need not proceed through the indirect route of negative concomitance, adopts the second alternative, in 'anyad api...' With the stated intent in view, he asks, in 'tat...' Since the first half is of difficult meaning, he explains it: 'asatah...' -- 'cause' means Prakriti. 'Abhava' (non-existence) means both prior non-existence and subsequent destruction. Whence is this established? -- for this the commentator gives scriptural proof, in 'prakritih...' 'Purusha' means the Supreme Self, also referred to by the word 'Brahman.' Even where beginninglessness is at issue, this scripture is to be understood in the same sense.

One might ask: since a single sentence, 'there is no non-existence of the unreal and the real,' could have denied non-existence of both at once, why is each denied separately? He answers, in 'prithak...' The taking of two separate sentences -- 'the unreal has no being' and 'the real has no non-being' -- denying each separately, is for the sake of emphasis, is to be understood. This emphasis serves to establish the pervasion required for the inference. For this very reason, many illustrations are shown.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Refuting the notion of existence itself as a modification, he declares its not-to-be-grievability, in 'nasata...': since existence itself arises only after origination, it too is a modification -- this is the sense. Just as, in the scriptural statement 'this was, in the beginning, the unreal itself; from that the real was born' (Taittiriya Upanishad 2.7), the doctrine of an unreal origin is refuted on account of the logical contradiction 'how could the real be born from the unreal?' (Chandogya Upanishad 6.2.2), so too here the Lord refutes the doctrine of the unreal. Of the unreal -- such as a sky-flower, non-existent in all three times -- there is no being, that is, no existence. And of the real -- the Self, existent in all three times -- there is no non-being: it is never perceived that the Self did not exist before, does not exist now, or will not exist hereafter. Since the unreal has no being, the being of the body and the rest cannot be asserted either, since they are perishable; nor can their non-being be asserted, since they do arise. Since the coming-into-being of the unreal, that is, its origination, is itself untenable, the true nature of this phenomenal world of body and the rest must be described as a compound of the real and the unreal.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
If one should object: 'granted the oneness of the Self, still that real Self's world of insentient objects perceived by it is itself equally real; and since cold, heat and the rest are the cause of pleasure and pain, their experience is unavoidable, and since a truly real thing cannot be destroyed merely by knowledge, why then endurance, or how could such a person become fit for immortality?' -- he answers: the entire manifold of duality is merely superimposed upon the Self, and hence its destruction through knowledge is entirely tenable, just as silver superimposed on mother-of-pearl is destroyed by the knowledge of the shell.

The Lord says that whatever is limited by time, place, or substance is unreal -- as a pot and the like, subject to origination and destruction, are said to be limited by time, since they are correlative to prior non-existence and subsequent destruction. Likewise limitation by place is also a mark of unreality. Difference of the same kind, difference of another kind, and internal difference -- these three kinds of difference are themselves the limitation of a thing. But the Person, known through the scripture 'this Person is self-luminous,' being self-luminous, and being complete by 'lying in the city (of the body),' finds pleasure and pain equal for him, since they appear merely as attributes not belonging to the Self. By the scripture 'this is the eternal glory of the knower of Brahman -- it neither increases through action, nor diminishes,' increase and decrease in the form of pleasure and pain are denied of him. 'Dhira' (steadfast) means the impeller of the intellect, the witness of the intellect. These pleasure-and-pain-giving sense-contacts do not, in truth, modify him, since, being the illuminator of all modifications, he is himself beyond modification -- just as the sun, though the eye of the entire world, is untouched by external faults, so too, per the scripture, 'the one inner Self of all beings is not touched by the world's sorrow, since it is external to it.' Hence that person, through knowledge of his own oneness with Brahman, by removing the root ignorance that is the source of all sorrow, becomes fit for immortality, that is, liberation -- untouched by the whole of duality, self-luminous, of the nature of supreme bliss.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
If one should ask, 'even so, how is cold, heat and the like, being utterly unbearable, to be endured, since enduring them fully might even, at times, lead to the body's destruction,' he answers, with the intent that everything can be endured through discernment of the truth, in 'na...': of the unreal, that is, of cold, heat and the like, which are not attributes of the Self and hence do not truly exist, there is no being, that is, no reality, in the Self. Likewise, of the real, that is, of the Self, whose very nature is being, there is no non-being, that is, no destruction. Thus the determination of both the real and the unreal has been seen. By whom? By the seers of truth, that is, by those who know the true nature of things. Endure with this kind of discernment -- this is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Although cold, heat and the like do appear, still, being sublated upon reflection, they are false, whereas the Self, by contrast, is real -- hence, without grief or delusion, it is proper simply to endure cold, heat and the rest. Since the unreal is sublated upon reflection, cold and the like, previously non-existent, even when examined by perception and other means of valid knowledge, have no ultimate reality. From this it follows that the cold and the rest in question, like the mirage-silver, are untrue, since the substrate-grasping means of knowledge (perception and the like) does not convey true validity regarding it.

If it be said that perception and the like sometimes do convey true validity, the answer is: the very changeability of the cold-heat duality, being subject to coming and going, establishes its unreality. The argument is this: 'the object in question is unreal, since it comes and goes, like the snake in the rope.' Since the inner faculty is impermanent and an object of perception, and distinct from you, the sense-contacts that produce pleasure and the rest are likewise impermanent -- endure them, knowing with discernment that they can do you no harm. Do not, through false identification of the Self with the sorrowful inner faculty, consider yourself sorrowful.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
He introduces the verse 'na asatah...' as a fresh beginning, after an intervening digression, to establish the topic properly at hand. That the changeable nature is inevitably subject to modification is not itself the cause of grief, but rather is opposed to the cause of grief and is itself the cause of the absence of grief -- this is the sense. Clarifying the sense of the words sat, asat, bhava, abhava and the like as suited to the present context, he explains: 'asatah' -- the unreal and the real are to be construed as the body and the Self respectively. The reference to 'these two' indicates both that are subject to experience.

The word 'tu' in 'true vision' conveys that this vision does not exceed the bounds of experience -- hence it is said 'as experienced, by the seers of Truth.' By this, Shankara's construal, 'that Brahman, that being, that truth,' is also refuted. Since the destruction of the eternal, denoted by the word 'sat,' is impossible, the word 'anta' (finality) is used in the sense of determination.

One might object that to say 'the body has no being' is improper, since this contradicts perception and the like; and to say 'the Self has no non-being' is likewise improper, since in the state of total negation described in the Chandogya Upanishad (6.2.1) -- 'this was, in the beginning, the unreal itself' -- even the Self is spoken of by the word 'asat'...

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
If one should object, 'enduring pain and the like would only result in the body's destruction, and liberation is not, in any case, contingent upon the destruction of the body, so what benefit is there in enduring suffering?' he answers, in 'nasata...': of the unreal, that is, of the mundane, there is no transcendent being; of the real, that is, of the transcendent, which consists of the Lord's own existence, there is no non-being, that is, no destruction -- this is the sense. The illustration here is this: one group of gopis remained within the house, while another group went to the Lord's Rasa-lila dance; yet of both, it is said: 'ubhayor api...' The word 'tu' serves to bar you from that vision. The finality of both of these has been seen by the seers of truth, that is, by devotees of the Lord fit to behold the Lord -- that is, its fruit has been seen. If you too desire likewise, then endure pleasure, pain and the rest; by this, the body and the rest, fit for the Lord's service, will not be destroyed -- this is the sense.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
'...does not see. For there is no loss of the seer's vision, since it is indestructible; nor is there any second, distinct thing there for it to see separately.' If the knower and the rest were truly real, then the non-perception of the real thing in deep sleep and the like would have to be attributed either to its absence, or to a loss of the seer's own vision. Neither holds: not the former, since it is improper to posit the existence, elsewhere, of something whose very nature was seen to arise and perish in one place; not the latter, since it is precisely denied by the very scripture cited above. Hence sameness, whether of reality or unreality, between the two is difficult to maintain.

One might object: space and the like, being real, are never absent anywhere or at any time; yet even a real atom is seen to be absent in some other location, and the coming-into-being (origination) of a previously non-existent pot and the like is also observed -- so how can it be said that 'the unreal has no being, the real has no non-being'? This doubt is dispelled by the experience of the learned, in 'ubhayor api...' 'Anta' means the true nature of things. Just as, in a dream, sky, pot, the snake mistaken for a rope and the like, though appearing definite with attributes of permanence-impermanence, reality-unreality and so forth, are sublated upon waking, so too what is seen even in the waking state is sublated by knowledge of the truth.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Those who are seers of the truth have concluded that of the nonexistent [the material body] there is no endurance and of the eternal [the soul] there is no change. This they have concluded by studying the nature of both.

 There is no endurance of the changing body. That the body is changing every moment by the actions and reactions of the different cells is admitted by modern medical science; and thus growth and old age are taking place in the body. But the spirit soul exists permanently, remaining the same despite all changes of the body and the mind. That is the difference between matter and spirit. By nature, the body is ever changing, and the soul is eternal. This conclusion is established by all classes of seers of the truth, both impersonalist and personalist. In the Viṣṇu Purāṇa (2.12.38) it is stated that Viṣṇu and His abodes all have self-illuminated spiritual existence ( jyotīṁṣi viṣṇur bhuvanāni viṣṇuḥ ). The words existent and nonexistent refer only to spirit and matter. That is the version of all seers of truth. This is the beginning of the instruction by the Lord to the living entities who are bewildered by the influence of ignorance. Removal of ignorance involves the reestablishment of the eternal relationship between the worshiper and the worshipable and the consequent understanding of the difference between the part-and-parcel living entities and the Supreme Personality of Godhead. One can understand the nature of the Supreme by thorough study of oneself, the difference between oneself and the Supreme being understood as the relationship between the part and the whole. In the Vedānta-sūtras, as well as in the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam , the Supreme has been accepted as the origin of all emanations. Such emanations are experienced by superior and inferior natural sequences. The living entities belong to the superior nature, as it will be revealed in the Seventh Chapter. Although there is no difference between the energy and the energetic, the energetic is accepted as the Supreme, and the energy or nature is accepted as the subordinate. The living entities, therefore, are always subordinate to the Supreme Lord, as in the case of the master and the servant, or the teacher and the taught. Such clear knowledge is impossible to understand under the spell of ignorance, and to drive away such ignorance the Lord teaches the Bhagavad-gītā for the enlightenment of all living entities for all time.
