Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
In these one-and-a-half verses we find the description of the entry into the battlefield of Arjuna, the hero of the Mahabharata war. The exact moment and manner of his entry are also recorded here. Arrow-warfare had not yet begun, but that moment was not far off. It was the most tense moment of the war. The crisis had reached its peak. At such a time, Arjuna of the monkey-banner requested Lord Krishna to drive his chariot between the two armies.

In ancient times, on the battlefield, every great warrior had his own distinctive, well-known emblazoned banner. When the banner fluttered, the warrior seated in the chariot could be identified by the enemy. According to the rules of that time, an ordinary soldier could not shoot arrows at a commander. Every warrior fought only with a warrior of his own rank. The custom of identifying a person by a distinctive emblem is prevalent on battlefields even today; a special mark identifying a senior officer is inscribed on his vehicle and uniform. The emblem on Arjuna's banner was the monkey (Hanuman).

From Sanjaya's description it appears that Arjuna was impatient to begin the righteous war. He had already raised his bow, which signals his readiness for battle.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Having shown that fear had come upon Duryodhana and the other sons of Dhritarashtra, he now cites, in 'Atha...', the very opposite condition of Partha and the other Pandavas. Even though the moment of fear had arrived and flight was possible, seeing the enemy standing firm and unmoving -- quite the reverse of himself -- Arjuna, having taken up the great monkey Hanuman as his banner-emblem, spoke to the Lord. What did he say? This forthcoming reasoned statement, called 'this word.' In what state did he speak it? -- 'When the discharge of weapons had begun,' meaning when the mass release of arrows, spears and the like was imminent, about to be launched. Having done what did he address the Lord? -- 'Having raised his bow.' By the word 'lord of the earth,' the all-seeing Sanjaya addresses King Dhritarashtra.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O lord of the earth, Dhritarashtra! Just as weapons were about to be set in motion, seeing the kings who unjustly held the kingdom and their allies standing arrayed before him, monkey-bannered Arjuna, son of Pandu, raised his Gandiva bow and spoke these words to the indwelling Lord Sri Krishna.

Explanation: 'Atha' -- this word conveys that Sanjaya now begins the Bhagavad Gita, the dialogue between Lord Sri Krishna and Arjuna. This dialogue ends with the word 'iti' in verse 74 of chapter 18. Likewise, the teaching of the Bhagavad Gita begins from verse 11 of chapter 2 and ends at verse 66 of chapter 18.

'Pravritte shastrasampate' -- although Grandsire Bhishma had not blown his conch to declare the start of battle, but only to cheer Duryodhana, still both the Kaurava and Pandava armies took it as the declaration of battle and readied themselves, weapons in hand. Seeing the armies with weapons raised, Arjuna too, filled with valor, raised his Gandiva bow.

'Vyavasthitan dhartarashtran drishtva' -- by these words Sanjaya conveys that when your son Duryodhana saw the Pandava army, he ran straight to Dronacharya; but when Arjuna saw the Kaurava army, his hand went straight to his Gandiva bow -- 'dhanurudyamya.' This shows that Duryodhana was inwardly afraid, while Arjuna was inwardly fearless, enthusiastic, and valiant.

'Kapidhvajah' -- by giving Arjuna the epithet 'monkey-bannered,' Sanjaya reminds Dhritarashtra of Hanuman seated on Arjuna's banner. Once, while the Pandavas lived in the forest, the wind suddenly brought a divine thousand-petaled lotus and dropped it before Draupadi. Delighted, she asked Bhimasena to bring her many such lotuses. Setting out to fulfill her wish, Bhimasena reached the banana grove, where he met Hanuman. After much conversation, Hanuman insisted Bhimasena ask a boon, and Bhimasena asked only for his continued grace. Hanuman then said: 'O son of the wind, when you roar like a lion, plunging into the enemy army stricken by arrows and weapons, I shall amplify that roar with my own; moreover, seated on Arjuna's banner, I shall roar so terribly that it will strike fear into the enemy's hearts, enabling you all to slay them with ease.' Thus, whoever has Hanuman seated on his chariot-banner is assured of victory.

'Pandavah' -- Dhritarashtra had used the word 'Pandavah' (sons of Pandu) in his own question. So, to repeatedly remind Dhritarashtra of the Pandavas, Sanjaya uses the word 'Pandavah' (as in 1.14 and here).

'Hrishikesham tada vakyamidam aha mahipate' -- upon seeing the Pandava army, Duryodhana ran to his teacher Dronacharya and spoke cunning words; but Arjuna, upon seeing the Kaurava army, spoke to Lord Sri Krishna -- the world-teacher, the indwelling Lord, the impeller of mind and intellect -- words full of valor, enthusiasm, and devotion to duty, which are related in what follows.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Having shown that fear had come upon Dhritarashtra's sons, he now cites the opposite condition of the Pandavas, in 'Atha...'. Even though the moment of fear had arrived and flight was possible, seeing the enemy standing firm in readiness for battle -- quite the reverse -- at the very moment weapons were about to be discharged, Arjuna, son of Pandu, graced by having the great hero Hanuman on his banner, utterly free of fear, raised his Gandiva bow and spoke this forthcoming statement to Hrishikesha, Sri Krishna, who as impeller of the senses knows the workings of every inner faculty. He did not act rashly on his own without deliberation -- unlike the enemy, whose lack of deliberation is contrasted with skill in policy and dharma; by implication, the address 'lord of the earth' conveys that 'you seized the kingdom rashly, without deliberation, and hence have no policy or dharma, and thus no victory awaits you.' He then introduces Arjuna's actual words: 'Station my chariot between the two armies' -- 'the two armies' meaning one's own side and the opposing side, both present -- 'station,' i.e., make it firm. Thus Arjuna directs the Lord of all as though commanding Him; for what is impossible for devotees, when even the Lord Himself carries out their commands? Hence the Pandavas' victory is certain. Anticipating the Lord's possible objection -- 'while I station the chariot thus, will not these enemies dislodge me and the chariot?' -- Arjuna says 'Achyuta' (the infallible one): who indeed could dislodge You, who are infallible in place, time, and substance? By this, any anger arising from being commanded is also forestalled, since You are ever unchanging.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Venkatanatha explains the passage from 'atha vyavasthitan' up to 'Kurun' (1.25): the whole is a single sentence resolved in three parts. The first sentence explains the sense of 'priyachikirshavah' (those wishing to please). Regarding 'vyavasthitan' -- the word 'vi' signals a special nuance -- namely 'eager for battle,' as is likewise stated shortly after, 'standing ready, wishing to fight.' Regarding 'kapidhvajah' -- to preclude any diminishment implied by merely calling him 'monkey-bannered,' the epithet recalls the boon Hanuman granted at the lotus expedition; and to convey that the mere sight of Hanuman would strike terror into the enemy just as into demons, he is elsewhere called 'the monkey-bannered one whose banner burned Lanka.' The word 'Achyuta' (infallible), establishing the Lord's unfailing nature, is explained through the terms 'jnana' etc.; the term 'Hrishikesha' is explained via 'para' and 'apara' (transcendent and immanent) -- or, the creation and so forth, characterized by His powers, is itself the very meaning of the word 'Hrishikesha.' As stated in the Ahirbudhnya Samhita: 'Being the Lord, He delights manifestly through the sport of creation; His being Hrishikesha, His lordship, and His divinity are thereby made clear. Being endowed, through His own unchanging nature, with the form of vigor called the senses (hrishika), the Lord, ever through independence, engages eternally in the act of creation and the rest; this state of possessing lordship and vigor is called 'Hrishikeshatva.'' As for the word 'Achyuta' meaning 'one who does not let go of those who take refuge in Him, and hence has no falling away' -- he shows this etymology through the phrase 'out of affection for His devotees,' etc. 'Seated as My charioteer' -- the sense being: for one who, as Hrishikesha, governs all the senses in every way, what great matter is it to govern the mere reins of a chariot? Regarding 'nirikshe' (let me look upon) -- the prefix conveys the sense of 'thoroughly.' The word 'yavat' (as long as) here conveys either completeness, or the time-limit of the looking, or, per grammar, the future tense marker in the sense of continuous looking. The verse says 'those with whom I must fight, let me look upon them' -- not simply 'let me observe those with whom I must fight' -- hence the rise of the following verse 'yotsyamanan' (those about to fight). Also, since 'dhartarashtrasya durbuddheh' (of the ill-counseled son of Dhritarashtra) is meant to expose Duryodhana's specific fault, there is no redundancy. Alternatively: since 'between the two armies' has already been mentioned, and since the coming verse says 'he saw them standing in both armies,' this earlier verse concerns those of one's own side and their allies standing in one's own army -- meaning, with whom, standing alongside, must I fight the enemy; the next verse concerns the Kaurava allies standing in the opposing army -- this distinction is clear. Even though he already knew all this beforehand...

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Thus, having described the battle-enthusiasm arisen in both armies for the meeting of Krishna and Arjuna, he now tells, in four verses beginning with 'Atha...', the reasoned prompting Krishna gave to Arjuna -- who, having entered the midst of battle at Krishna's direction, was about to feel detachment at the sight of his kinsmen's destruction. First he shows the occasion for that prompting. 'Atha' (now/then) -- seeing, in reverse order, without any fear, Dhritarashtra's people standing arrayed -- their position clearly discerned -- monkey-bannered Arjuna (the epithet 'monkey-bannered' conveys the lightness of his weapon-handling), as the discharge of weapons began, raised his bow -- Pandu's son, desiring to regain his own kingdom, spoke this forthcoming statement to Hrishikesha, the impeller of the senses, at that very moment. The address 'lord of the earth' is meant to convey that kings, too, should act according to dharma. He then gives Arjuna's actual words, beginning with 'senayoh...': 'O Achyuta, station my chariot between the two armies.'

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
At that time Arjuna, the son of Pāṇḍu, seated in the chariot bearing the flag marked with Hanumān, took up his bow and prepared to shoot his arrows. O King, after looking at the sons of Dhṛtarāṣṭra drawn in military array, Arjuna then spoke to Lord Kṛṣṇa these words.

 The battle was just about to begin. It is understood from the above statement that the sons of Dhṛtarāṣṭra were more or less disheartened by the unexpected arrangement of military force by the Pāṇḍavas, who were guided by the direct instructions of Lord Kṛṣṇa on the battlefield. The emblem of Hanumān on the flag of Arjuna is another sign of victory because Hanumān cooperated with Lord Rāma in the battle between Rāma and Rāvaṇa, and Lord Rāma emerged victorious. Now both Rāma and Hanumān were present on the chariot of Arjuna to help him. Lord Kṛṣṇa is Rāma Himself, and wherever Lord Rāma is, His eternal servitor Hanumān and His eternal consort Sītā, the goddess of fortune, are present. Therefore, Arjuna had no cause to fear any enemies whatsoever. And above all, the Lord of the senses, Lord Kṛṣṇa, was personally present to give him direction. Thus, all good counsel was available to Arjuna in the matter of executing the battle. In such auspicious conditions, arranged by the Lord for His eternal devotee, lay the signs of assured victory.
