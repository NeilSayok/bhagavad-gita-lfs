Life Application:

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The senses are the root cause of the fall of the Self, the sovereign of the spiritual kingdom. Arjuna is here cautioned to remain constantly vigilant against the uncontrolled, unbridled wandering of the senses among sense-objects if he wishes to attain perfection. Modern psychology may frown upon this teaching of the Gita, since, according to Freud, desires are man's natural instincts, and to restrain them is seen as unnatural suppression. In the West, restraint is equated with repression, and no one favours repression for the sake of mental health. But Vedic philosophy nowhere teaches repression. It stresses instead the maturing of the intellect, through which a person's personality blossoms and the craving for the inferior naturally falls away upon attaining the superior. It speaks not of suppressing desires but of rising above them.

Lord Krishna beautifully clarifies this Vedic principle here, illuminating both the positive (what should be done) and the negative (what should be given up) aspects of the practice of self-development. Enjoyments and actions unfavourable to self-development should be renounced while favourable practices are pursued. In the positive teaching, the Lord instructs the disciple to become mat-parah - one who regards Me, the Supreme Self, as the highest goal of life. In this half-verse, 'yukta asita mat-parah,' the complete discipline of self-development is stated. The immoral and sensuous tendencies that drag a person to the animal level are the outcome of enjoyments and accumulated desires from countless past lives; it is never possible to destroy them all, or to rise above them, in a single lifetime - this is also the cause of despair among moral reformers, ideal teachers, and spiritual seekers.

The ancient rishis discovered, through their own experience, the means to end these sensory tendencies: the endeavour, in the calm atmosphere of meditation, to steady the mind in its pure, complete nature. Through this practice, one whose senses have thereby come under control is regarded as a person of steady wisdom. The deeper meaning of this verse now becomes clear: forcible restraint of the senses through starvation is only momentary, and one should not expect the blossoming of spiritual beauty from it. The person established in Self-realization, whose senses remain naturally under control, is the one of steady wisdom - he neither destroys the senses nor stops using them. The perfected sage is one whose senses and mind, being under control, remain ever ready to serve him. The Lord next explains the cause of the downfall of one who fails.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
If it be objected that the senses cannot possibly be controlled, the verse 'tāni' answers this: they can be controlled through great effort, and so one should make that effort. 'Yukta' means one whose mind is joined with Me in yoga. 'Matparah' means one for whom I alone am supreme, superior to everything else. The phrase 'vaśe hi' then states the result of such mastery.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Having explained the attainment of mastery over the senses, the verse 'tāni' now states what should follow. What becomes of one who thus sits is stated by 'vaśe hi.' In answer to how one who is composed and free from distraction should be seated, the word 'matparah' is used. Dispelling any doubt about a difference between the higher and lower self, the phrase 'I am none other' clarifies what this manner of sitting means. The latter half of the verse is explained by 'evam.' The force of the word 'hi' is brought out through 'practice' - meaning that through sustained, long, uninterrupted practice of reflecting 'I am not different from the Supreme Self,' or through habitually recognizing the faults inherent in sense-objects, the senses become fully restrained.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
The karma-yogi seeker, having brought all these senses under control, should sit devoted to Me; for one whose senses are under control has firm wisdom.

Explanation: The senses forcibly drag the mind toward objects; the seeker must vigilantly keep them from ever wavering toward sense-objects, and become wholly devoted to Me. This implies that when a seeker controls his senses, pride can arise - the sense that 'I have brought my senses under control by my own strength.' This pride prevents further progress and turns the seeker away from God. Therefore the seeker should never take pride in his own strength in restraining the senses, nor consider his own effort the cause, but should regard any success in sense-control as due solely to God's grace. In this way, by being devoted to God alone, the practice reaches fulfilment.

Here 'mat-parah' implies that obtaining a human body, developing interest in spiritual practice, engaging in it, and its fulfilment - all of these depend on God's grace alone; but due to ego, a person's attention rarely turns to this truth. Karma-yogis especially tend to emphasize action itself and regard it as their own effort; hence, as a special grace, God advises even the karma-yogi seeker to become devoted to Him. Being devoted to God means holding importance for God alone - 'God is mine and I am God's; the world is not mine, nor am I the world's' - because God is always with me, whereas the world is not. Thus the seeker's sense of self should rest only in God. Although this passage occurs in the context of karma-yoga, one might expect a karma-yoga-specific method here; yet a study of the Gita shows that devotion to God alone (bhagavat-parayanata) is the true cause of success in any practice - hence the Gita glorifies such devotion greatly, as in the statement that among all yogis, the one who worships Him with faith and love, becoming devoted to Him, is the best (6.47).

Regarding 'vaśe hi yasyendriyāṇi tasya prajñā pratiṣṭhitā': earlier, in verse 59, it was said that even when the senses are withdrawn from their objects, steadiness of wisdom does not necessarily follow, since an inner relish (rasa-buddhi) for objects may still remain. Here, by contrast, it is said that one whose senses are under control is a person of steady wisdom - meaning that for such a person, this inner relish has itself ceased. Hence it is not a fixed rule that withdrawing the senses from objects makes one a person of steady wisdom, since the inner relish may persist; but it is a fixed rule that becoming a person of steady wisdom results in the senses being under control.

Connection: Being devoted to God results in the senses coming under control and the inner relish ceasing; what happens when one is not devoted to God is described in the next two verses.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
With a desire to overcome this mutual dependence between the subduing of the senses and vision of the self, one has to coner the senses which are difficult to subdue on account of their attachment to sense-objects. So, focussing the mind on Me who am the only auspicious object for meditation, let him remain steadfast. When the mind is focussed on Me  as its object, then such a mind, purified by the burning away of all impurities and devoid of attachment to the senses, is able to control the senses. Then the mind with the senses under control will be able to experience the self. As said in Visnu Purana, 'As the leaping fire fanned by the wind burns away a forest of dry trees, so Visnu, who is in the hearts of all the Yogins, destroys all the sins.' Sri Krsna teaches the same here:  'He whose senses are under control, his knowledge is firmly set.'

Sri Krsna says:  'One who endeavours to subdue the senses, depending on one's own exertions, and does not focus the mind on Me in this way, becomes lost.'

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
This addresses a possible doubt: since the topic under discussion was the goal to be attained, why does the verse speak of restraining the senses? This clarifies an intervening doubt - if the senses cannot be conquered even by ordinary discriminative knowledge, one might think mastery over them is altogether unachievable, and that since liberating knowledge is said to depend on sense-conquest, a mutual-dependency difficulty would arise, undermining sense-conquest as a means to knowledge. In answer to the doubt of whether sense-restraint is possible at all, the phrase 'restrain them all and remain seated' shows that it is achievable through great and sustained effort - great effort here referring to an alternative, intensified practice, even where an easier method exists. The word 'yukta' does not mean mere physical yoking but absorption (samadhi). 'Matparah' is clarified, on the basis of the later word 'mayi' (in Me), as referring to the Lord Himself; contrary to those who interpret 'matparah' as knowledge of non-duality, it is explained in its natural sense - 'I alone am supreme, superior to all' - and continuous fixing of the mind upon the Lord alone, known thus, is the supreme means for conquering the senses. Mention of matters such as abstention from food is only to indicate an actual state of affairs. The latter half of the verse, 'vaśe hi,' states the result: mastery over the senses yields firm wisdom. Since such wisdom is the fruit of sense-conquest achieved only through great effort, those who shrink from such effort fail to attain it - not because the definition of steady wisdom is otherwise mistaken. This is the combined purport of this verse together with the following two verses.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
To firmly establish the duty first taught regarding the senses, these two verses describe how one should sit, along with the reasoning behind it. Even for one who strives, practising mastery over each sense individually is the better course, since the senses agitate the mind. Therefore, one who first restrains all the senses through the intellect, remains united in yoga, and is devoted to Me alone (matparah) - his wisdom alone, it should be understood, is firmly established.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
If this is so, what then is the remedy? The verse answers: one should restrain all these senses - the instruments of knowledge and action - bring them under control, and remain seated, absorbed, with the mind subdued, free from outward activity. If it be asked how the turbulent senses can be brought under control, the answer is 'matparah' - one for whom I, Vasudeva, the Self of all, alone am supreme and to be sought, meaning one who is exclusively devoted to Me. As it is said, 'No evil ever befalls the devotees of Vasudeva.' Just as in the world thieves are restrained by taking refuge with a powerful king, and knowing themselves to be under the king's protection they themselves come under control, so too, by taking refuge in the Lord who dwells within all as the inner controller, the wayward senses are restrained through His power alone; and further, thinking 'I am under the Lord's protection,' the senses themselves come under His control. The great glory of devotion to the Lord will be explained in detail later. The result of controlling the senses is stated by 'vaśe hi' - it is self-evident. This also answers the earlier question of how one should sit: one should sit with the senses brought under control.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Since this is so, the verse 'tāni' states: the yogi, having restrained all these senses and being united in yoga, should remain seated devoted to Me alone (matparah) - one whose senses are under his control. This answers the question of how one should sit: one should sit with the senses brought under control.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Therefore, having restrained all the senses and brought them under one's own control, one for whom I alone am supreme (matparah), being thus united, should remain absorbed in Me. Thus, one who is devoted to Me in this way has his wisdom firmly established. This means that one whose wisdom is firmly established has his senses under control, and not otherwise, since the senses are inherently turbulent. This is why, earlier, even the wise were said to be incapable of this by mere effort alone.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Even though the senses are difficult to conquer, they must still be restrained, since otherwise steadiness of wisdom itself cannot be established. Hence the verse 'tāni' states: having restrained, that is, brought under control, and being united, that is, prepared, one should remain seated as 'matparah' - one for whom I alone, the inner Self of all, am supreme and dearest, more excellent than all things external such as women and the like, and than all things internal such as the body and senses. 'Hi' means 'because'; 'vaśe' means 'under command.' The rest is clear.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
One who restrains his senses, keeping them under full control, and fixes his consciousness upon Me, is known as a man of steady intelligence.

 That the highest conception of yoga perfection is Kṛṣṇa consciousness is clearly explained in this verse. And unless one is Kṛṣṇa conscious it is not at all possible to control the senses. As cited above, the great sage Durvāsā Muni picked a quarrel with Mahārāja Ambarīṣa, and Durvāsā Muni unnecessarily became angry out of pride and therefore could not check his senses. On the other hand, the king, although not as powerful a yogī as the sage, but a devotee of the Lord, silently tolerated all the sage’s injustices and thereby emerged victorious. The king was able to control his senses because of the following qualifications, as mentioned in the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam (9.4.18–20): sa vai manaḥ kṛṣṇa-padāravindayor vacāṁsi vaikuṇṭha-guṇānuvarṇane karau harer mandira-mārjanādiṣu śrutiṁ cakārācyuta-sat-kathodaye mukunda-liṅgālaya-darśane dṛśau tad-bhṛtya-gātra-sparśe ’ṅga-saṅgamam ghrāṇaṁ ca tat-pāda-saroja-saurabhe śrīmat-tulasyā rasanāṁ tad-arpite pādau hareḥ kṣetra-padānusarpaṇe śiro hṛṣīkeśa-padābhivandane kāmaṁ ca dāsye na tu kāma-kāmyayā yathottama-śloka-janāśrayā ratiḥ “King Ambarīṣa fixed his mind on the lotus feet of Lord Kṛṣṇa, engaged his words in describing the abode of the Lord, his hands in cleansing the temple of the Lord, his ears in hearing the pastimes of the Lord, his eyes in seeing the form of the Lord, his body in touching the body of the devotee, his nostrils in smelling the flavor of the flowers offered to the lotus feet of the Lord, his tongue in tasting the tulasī leaves offered to Him, his legs in traveling to the holy place where His temple is situated, his head in offering obeisances unto the Lord, and his desires in fulfilling the desires of the Lord … and all these qualifications made him fit to become a mat-para devotee of the Lord.” The word mat-para is most significant in this connection. How one can become mat-para is described in the life of Mahārāja Ambarīṣa. Śrīla Baladeva Vidyābhūṣaṇa, a great scholar and ācārya in the line of the mat-para, remarks, mad-bhakti-prabhāvena sarvendriya-vijaya-pūrvikā svātma-dṛṣṭiḥ su-labheti bhāvaḥ. “The senses can be completely controlled only by the strength of devotional service to Kṛṣṇa.” Also, the example of fire is sometimes given: “As a blazing fire burns everything within a room, Lord Viṣṇu, situated in the heart of the yogī, burns up all kinds of impurities.” The Yoga-sūtra also prescribes meditation on Viṣṇu, and not meditation on the void. The so-called yogīs who meditate on something other than the Viṣṇu form simply waste their time in a vain search after some phantasmagoria. We have to be Kṛṣṇa conscious – devoted to the Personality of Godhead. This is the aim of the real yoga.
