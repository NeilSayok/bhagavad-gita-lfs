Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
That reasoned discrimination through which knowledge of the ultimate Reality is gained is called Sankhya, which the Lord has already expounded; gaining this knowledge brings about the complete cessation of the world of grief and delusion. Now Sri Krishna promises Arjuna an exposition of Karma-yoga, or Buddhi-yoga.

Many people mistake the law of karma for fatalism, but it is a foundational principle of Hindu thought, essential for anyone studying the Hindu way of life to understand correctly. If, for a crime committed a year ago by one Raman Rao in Madras, I am today punished by a court in Delhi, it implies that there must be some real continuity between that criminal Raman Rao and the present-day monk Chinmaya - the long arm of the law, recognising that the offender Raman Rao is indeed Chinmaya, reaches Delhi to punish him. In the same way, Nature's justice is inexorable and complete. Hence the Hindu sages accepted that the sufferings we undergo in the present have their cause in wrongs committed in some other place and body in the past, which proves that the sinner of the past and the sufferer of the present must be one and the same - called in the scriptures the jiva, the mind-and-intellect.

Every willed action leaves upon the doer's mind an impression matching the doer's intention; these impressions are called vasanas, and to work them out each jiva is born in a particular place, time and circumstance. According to accumulated past actions, all beings must undergo pleasure and pain. The scars left by sin on the mind can be washed away only by tears shed in moments filled with genuine remorse. Circumstances do not make a person weep; rather, one's own sinful tendencies are the true cause of sorrow. For one whose inner being is purified, sorrow no longer finds any basis.

Merely possessing a recording of music does not let us hear it; only when the record is placed on the player and touched by the needle does the music sound. Likewise, vasanas alone in the mind do not produce experience of pleasure or pain; only when, touched by the needle of egoism, they express themselves in the outer world as action does the varied fruit come to be experienced.

One established in the evenness described in the previous verse rises above the pairs of opposites - pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat - and abides in the bliss of the Self. To the extent that identification with body, mind and intellect diminishes, to that same extent the ego of doership also perishes; and finally, when the ego is absent, for whom would the fruit of action remain? That is, both action and its fruit come to an end.

This truth taught by Sri Krishna in the Gita was nothing new or original; five thousand years before the birth of Christ He merely revived an ancient teaching and thereby restored to life a dying faith - a teaching that even today, after thousands of years, still stands offering us its message of joy.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
Sankhya is knowledge. As is stated in the Vyasa-smriti quoting the Lord's own words, 'the true realization of the pure nature of the Self is called Sankhya.' Yoga is the means, as in the usage of the Bhagavata, 'the yogas that are seen and applied for the attainment of good for men.' Sankhya and Yoga are not intended here as ends valued in themselves apart from devotion, since elsewhere they are combined, as in the term 'Karma-yoga'; and because, taken apart from the Veda's single purpose, they are censured as differing doctrines in texts on liberation - though, praised together with the Pancharatra, no real conflict remains, since the Vedas and the Pancharatra serve one and the same end, their apparent separateness being relative to the standing of systems such as Sankhya. This is confirmed in the very scripture of the sages called Chitrashikhandins, the source of the Pancharatra, which declares the unity of the Vedas. Thus everywhere the words Sankhya and Yoga are to be understood as denoting the means to that same end. Since it follows that jnana, knowledge of the individual soul, has already been declared, and the means (upaya) is yet to be told: buddhi is that by which one understands; hence, the meaning is, 'that speech by which the subject matter of Sankhya is understood has been declared to you.'

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Objection: since the removal of sorrow and delusion had already been effectively accomplished through the logical argument of verses such as 'having regard also to your own duty,' why, after that, is the exposition of the highest Reality now being brought to its conclusion? Reply: those earlier verses only exhibited the well-known worldly principle that heaven and the like are attained by one who performs the duties of caste and life-stage, and not otherwise - a rule shown merely in passing, not as the real intent. What was really intended is what is conveyed by the teaching beginning with 'never was there a time when I did not exist,' established by reasoning such as 'It is not born, nor does it ever die.' The purpose of this conclusion is to exhibit, through their common subject matter, the two distinct paths (of knowledge and of action) each assigned to its own province - hence this conclusion of the teaching on the highest Reality. Since this division of subject matter could equally well have been shown later, at the point where the two paths are actually taken up, it is shown here at the outset for the convenience both of the further exposition and of the listener's grasp of it. Having thus indicated the value of this conclusion, the Lord now brings the teaching to a close, gathering up the knowledge of the highest Reality already imparted in the words 'but for Yoga,' extolling that very wisdom for the sake of its distinguished fruit in the words 'endowed with which wisdom.' The word buddhi is here shown not to denote a mere object of the inner faculty but knowledge (jnana) itself, whose distinguishing fruit, independent of any auxiliary means, is stated to be the direct removal of sorrow and delusion - that is, attachment and aversion, and the misconceived sense of agency and enjoyership, all born of ignorance of one's own true nature - since it is knowledge alone, ascertained by the method of agreement and difference, that removes such ignorance without need of anything further. Explaining 'but now hear this in respect of Yoga,' the word Yoga is here shown not to denote the mere restraint of the mind's modifications, but Knowledge itself, useful for liberation, indicated by the word 'this.' The very means for the arising of that knowledge is disclosed by the word 'through detachment' - freedom from any desire for fruit. The reason for praising this wisdom is stated to be the creation of eagerness: praised wisdom becomes an object worthy of faith and thereby makes the hearer fit to practise it faithfully, so that such praise serves a real purpose. Since, however, mere wisdom concerning the performance of action cannot by itself remove the bondage of action - for action cannot be uprooted at its very root without knowledge of Truth - this possible doubt is answered by the words indicating that this comes about through the grace of the Lord.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O Partha, this same evenness of mind (sama-buddhi) that was earlier explained to you in the context of Sankhya-yoga, now hear it explained in the context of Karma-yoga; endowed with that same evenness you shall cast off the bondage of action.

Here the word 'tu' (but) marks a shift of context - the topic of Sankhya having been concluded, the topic of Yoga now begins. The word 'esha' (this) refers to the very evenness of mind described in the previous verse, which has already been thoroughly explained in the Sankhya-yoga section (verses 11 to 30): once the true discrimination between the body and its possessor, the embodied Self, arises, one experiences one's own ever-established state of equanimity - since it is attachment to the body alone that gives rise to inequality of mind. Thus evenness has already been dealt with under Sankhya-yoga; now hear this same evenness explained with reference to Karma-yoga. The word 'imam' indicates that this same evenness is now to be explained in the context of Karma-yoga - how it is attained there, what its nature is, and what its glory is; for these reasons the Lord asks Arjuna to hear of this wisdom in respect of Yoga.

'Endowed with this wisdom, O Partha, you shall cast off the bondage of action' - Arjuna feared that sin would attach to him through fighting (1.36, 45); but in the Lord's view, sin attaches to action only through unevenness of mind, that is, through attachment and aversion, and never through evenness of mind. Just as countless virtuous and sinful deeds go on happening in the world without their merit or demerit attaching to us, because we remain even-minded and without partiality toward them, so too, if you remain endowed with this evenness of mind, these actions of yours will likewise not bind you.

Earlier, in the seventh verse of this chapter, Arjuna had asked about his own true welfare; hence the Lord now describes the principal means to that welfare. First He taught the discipline of Sankhya-yoga and laid great stress on the performance of one's prescribed duty, saying that for a kshatriya there is no better means to good than a righteous war (2.31); then He said that fighting with an even mind incurs no sin (2.38); and now He speaks of that same even-mindedness in relation to Karma-yoga.

The Karma-yogi performs all action for the welfare and guidance of the world (loka-sangraha), as will be said later, 'having regard also to the welfare of the world you should act' (3.20). Acting selflessly to uphold the moral order of society, turning people from the wrong path onto the right one, makes the attainment of evenness of mind easy; and through this attainment, the Karma-yogi is easily freed from the bondage of action.

This thirty-ninth verse properly belongs right after the thirtieth verse, and should logically have come there, since it speaks of two distinct grounds of steadfastness (nishtha): first, steadfastness through Sankhya-yoga (evenness of mind), described from verse 11 to 30, and now, steadfastness through Karma-yoga. Hence the insertion here of the eight verses from 31 to 38 might seem out of place. Yet the reason these eight verses are given here is that, before speaking of evenness of mind in Karma-yoga, it was first necessary to clarify what is Arjuna's duty and what is not his duty - namely, that fighting is his duty and refusal to fight is not. Therefore the Lord spoke these eight verses (2.31-38) purely to establish duty and non-duty, and only then takes up the theme of evenness of mind again. In sum: verses 11 to 30 established evenness of mind by describing the real (sat) and the unreal (asat) - that the real is ever real and the unreal ever unreal, and neither can be altered by anyone; verses 31 to 38 then established what is duty and what is not; and from verse 39 onward, having renounced what is not his duty and taken up what is, the Lord goes on to describe evenness of mind in success and failure, and in the attainment or non-attainment of the fruit of action.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
As a summing-up of the topic already concluded, and as a preparatory statement connecting it with the topic about to begin, the words 'This has been declared to you' are used. To dispel the impression that the words Sankhya and Yoga here refer to the systems of the sage Kapila and of Patanjali, it is explained that Sankhya means the truth about the Self as ascertained through discriminative understanding. These other two systems are not intended here, because, though accepted in a limited, qualified sense in certain scriptural contexts (as in expressions like 'endowed with that understanding'), they are not intended in their entirety; for if a system could be authoritative merely because some part of it is accepted, even a Buddhist doctrine could claim authority on that basis, which is untenable. Also, Yoga does not here mean the Patanjala system, since the compound 'Karma-yoga' specifically distinguishes this Yoga, a term not found as such in Patanjali's text. Moreover, these two systems are censured elsewhere as differing in doctrine from the Pancharatra and the Veda-Aranyaka - as at Mahabharata 12.349.64, which lists Sankhya, Yoga, Pashupata and Veda-Aranyaka as distinct and separate teachings; while the Pancharatra alone is extolled there as surpassing all these doctrines (12.349.68), a praise which, being of one system among several rivals, implies censure of the rest. And the Veda-Aranyaka is not thereby condemned, since it shares a single purport with the Pancharatra - the text's apparent distinction of the two is only relative, for the Chitrashikhandin sages, being of one mind, are said to have composed a single supreme scripture in harmony with the four Vedas on Mount Meru, thereby affirming their essential unity. Hence, wherever the words Sankhya and Yoga occur, as in 'through the Yoga of knowledge for the Sankhyas' (3.3) or 'the ignorant speak of Sankhya and Yoga as different' (5.4), they are likewise to be understood as denoting the means to knowledge. Since buddhi (understanding), being itself produced by such knowledge, is what is actually declared and heard about - and not something separately taught - the word 'buddhi,' though speech would be the more natural term for what is declared, is deliberately used in the locative 'sankhye' to indicate the topic concerned, so as to convey not mere words but the very understanding meant to arise in you.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Thus, though Sankhya, aimed at the removal of sorrow, has already been taught, seeing that Partha's inclination is not confined to that alone, the Lord now introduces the teaching of Atma-yoga (Karma-yoga) for the settling of his mind, saying 'This has been declared to you.' Sankhya means the discriminative reckoning of the truth of the Self and the not-Self; its subject matter was taught, as a thing to be accepted, from 'It is not that I did not exist' (2.12) through 'therefore you should not grieve for any being' (2.30); in between, the performance of one's own duty was also established; and again, toward the end, in 'having made pleasure and pain alike' (2.38) and what follows, the teaching of Sankhya has been given together with an element of Yoga. Now, in respect of Yoga - the discipline whose purpose is restraint of the mind leading to steadiness resting upon the Lord - hear this wisdom, favouring the observance of one's own duty, that is now to be declared. The word 'tu' marks this distinction. Endowed with this wisdom, you shall cast off the bondage of action, comprising both merit and demerit together.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Objection: granted there is no sin in fighting when done with the understanding that it is one's own duty, still, is it fitting for You to instruct me to fight, when for the one who knows, as declared in 'he who knows this as the slayer... whom does he cause to be slain, whom does he slay?', all action is set aside altogether? For one who is by nature a non-doer and non-enjoyer, pure in essence, the notion 'having fought I shall enjoy its fruit' cannot arise together with true knowledge, since knowledge and action, like light and darkness, cannot combine - this being Arjuna's own unspoken thought, to become explicit later at 'if you deem knowledge superior.' Hence it would seem that instructing the same person in both knowledge and action is improper. Reply: this is not so, because instruction in knowledge or in action is each appropriate depending on whether the person is wise or unwise. Accordingly, in these twenty-one verses beginning 'never was there a time when I did not exist,' the Lord has declared to you the wisdom of Sankhya - that is, knowledge of the Purusha taught in the Upanishads, realized as free from every limiting condition; this knowledge, the sole cause of removing every evil, has been imparted to you. For one who possesses such knowledge, as will be said later, 'no duty whatsoever exists' - no action is prescribed at all. But if, even after this teaching, such wisdom has not arisen in you because of some defect of mind, then, for removing that defect and thereby directly realizing the Self, you must practise Karma-yoga. In that Yoga, hear at length the wisdom already indicated in 'having made pleasure and pain equal' - the wisdom marked by the relinquishment of attachment to the fruit of action - which is now to be explained to you. The word 'tu' indicates a distinction from the wisdom concerning pure Self-knowledge given earlier, showing that this present wisdom concerns Yoga; hence there is no contradiction from any implied combining of the two, since instruction in knowledge is meant for the pure of mind and instruction in action for the impure. This wisdom relating to Yoga is then praised through its fruit: endowed with this resolute understanding, engaged in action, you shall progressively and utterly cast off the obstruction to knowledge caused by impurity of intention that arises from action - such that the obstruction will not recur. The underlying idea: the obstruction to knowledge caused by action can be removed only by righteous action itself, as scripture says, 'through righteousness one wards off sin'; mere inquiry such as hearing the scriptures can remove only the visible obstacles of doubt and the like for one already free from action-born impurity, but by itself cannot remove the bondage of action. Since your mind, therefore, is still exceedingly impure, only the external means - action - is fit for you to practise now; you are not yet even fit for hearing the scriptures, still less for knowledge itself - as will be said later, 'your right is to action alone.' By this, the objection that the Lord ought rather to have prescribed hearing and the like, since that is the more direct means to Sankhya's wisdom, instead of action, stands refuted.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:

Sankhya is that by which the truth of things is rightly and fully known; the reality of the Self shining forth through such right knowledge is Sankhya, and the wisdom fit to be practised with regard to it has been declared to you. But even when this wisdom concerning Sankhya has thus been declared, if direct realization of the truth of the Self does not yet arise for you, then, for the sake of directly realizing the truth of the Self through the purification of the inner faculty, hear now this wisdom in respect of Karma-yoga. Endowed with this wisdom - performing Karma-yoga as an offering to the Supreme Lord, thereby becoming pure of mind, and attaining, through His grace, direct knowledge - you shall utterly cast off the bondage consisting of action.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
This teaching arose incidentally in the context of the knowledge of the Self that removes sorrow and delusion, illustrating, by the well-known worldly principle that those who know their duty perform it once sorrow and delusion, which obstruct it, are removed, that you too, having regard to your own duty, ought not to waver, overcome by sorrow and delusion. The eight verses beginning 'having regard also to your own duty' (2.31-38) were spoken only incidentally in this connection, not with the intent of prescribing a combination of knowledge and action, since it is the exposition of the highest Reality that is properly the subject here. Concluding that exposition of the highest Reality, the Lord now proposes to speak of steadfastness in Yoga, the means to it, for the purification of the mind, and declares: This wisdom concerning the discrimination of the highest Reality - the direct cause of removing sorrow, delusion and the other faults that are the root of transmigratory existence - has been declared to you. Now, in respect of Yoga - namely, the performance of action for the worship of the Lord through detachment and abandonment of the pairs of opposites, and also the Yoga of meditative absorption, both being the means to attain that same knowledge - hear this wisdom, next to be declared. This wisdom is then praised: endowed with this wisdom relating to Yoga, you shall utterly cast off the bondage of action, that is, action itself under the names of merit and demerit. Though such wisdom concerning Yoga alone cannot by itself remove the bondage of action without knowledge of Truth, since action cannot be uprooted at its very root without such knowledge, still this comes about through the attainment of knowledge granted by the Lord's grace; the mention of this intermediate step serves to praise its means, Karma-yoga. The address 'Partha' hints that one endowed with this wisdom shall not again enter a mother's womb. As for the alternative view, that the wisdom of Yoga merely removes the impurity obstructing knowledge that is caused by action, while inquiry (shravana) removes only doubt-related obstacles and by itself cannot remove the bondage of action - this is untenable, since there is no ground for restricting the word 'karma' here only to actions productive of heaven and hell, nor for taking 'bandha' to mean a mere obstruction rather than actual bondage. The removal of the bondage called merit and demerit through this wisdom is possible indirectly, through the attainment of knowledge, as already explained; and even the doubts removed by inquiry ultimately stem from the impurity of mind caused by sin. Hence the earlier explanation - that you shall cast off the bondage of action, that is, transmigratory existence, through the attainment of knowledge granted by the Lord's grace - stands, free from the fault of gratuitously supplying words or of rendering the word 'action' meaningless, just as the word 'birth' in the phrase 'freed from the bondage of birth' meaningfully conveys the nature of that bondage.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Continuing the theme of the removal of sorrow discussed in the preceding section, the Lord now connects it, through its principal purport, with the topic of the section that follows, using the word 'thus.' By this it is shown why Karma-yoga is spoken of before Jnana-yoga: although, immediately after teaching the truth of the Self, it would seem fitting to speak next of Jnana-yoga, sustained reflection on that truth, still, since Jnana-yoga is attainable only through Karma-yoga, Karma-yoga is expounded first, and Jnana-yoga is described afterward, at 'having renounced all desires' (2.55), as its fruit. 'To state' means to expound at length what has so far been mentioned only in brief. To dispel both the notion that the term 'Sankhya-yoga' here denotes a doctrine opposed to the Veda, and the notion that the word 'Sankhya' here refers to the Jnana-yoga to be described later at 'through the Yoga of knowledge for the Sankhyas' (3.3), it is explained that, according to the lexicons, 'sankhya' means intellect, understanding, wisdom, or realization; and just as the word 'Sankhya' is used of the Supreme Self in Upanishadic usage ('the qualityless Purusha is Sankhya'), it applies equally well to the individual self. There is no inconsistency of meaning across passages such as 3.3, because the term 'buddhi-yoga' is used throughout in connection with it, and even where some variation appears, it is reconciled according to context in each place. The singular number is used to indicate that the term is meant generically. The phrase 'the truth of the Self' shows that its immediate fruit is right ascertainment alone; and the word 'buddhi' here denotes the very determinate understanding, imbued with reflection on right reasoning, that results from such ascertainment - so there is no conflict between what is to be accomplished and its means. Or else, 'buddhi' here may mean the conclusion to be established by this scripture, while the phrase 'for the knowledge of that' points toward direct realization. Delimiting the portion in which the truth of the Self was taught, the Lord says 'never indeed' - meaning that from this point onward, in passages such as 'having regard also to your own duty,' confusion between right and wrong and attachment born of affection are being addressed. The word 'tu' here is shown, from the context, to mark these very special topics. The phrase 'this, to be declared' shows that its exposition will follow without delay, distinguishing the wisdom already implicit from the wisdom now to be explicitly declared, and also showing that the very term Karma-yoga is rooted in this particular application of understanding. To dispel any confusion, arising from the separate case-endings of 'buddhya' and 'yaya,' that these might belong to two different actions, it is glossed as 'endowed with which wisdom.' The compound 'karma-bandha' is explained as an instrumental compound meaning 'bondage caused by action,' the word 'bandha' here denoting not its ordinary sense but transmigratory existence itself - thereby also removing the misconception that action performed would otherwise leave no result whatsoever.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having thus taught Sankhya, the knowledge of the Self, the Lord now brings it to a close: 'This' - the wisdom already declared to you for the sake of Sankhya, the illumination of the Self and the not-Self, has been given precisely as a means to that end. Since Sankhya, being in the nature of the pain of separation from the Lord, cannot by itself bring true peace through mere knowledge of the Self apart from its identity with the Lord, this wisdom of Self-knowledge has been declared only for the sake of leading to that further knowledge. But if, even after hearing this, knowledge has still not arisen in you, then your delusion shall be removed through Karma-yoga - hence the Lord says: now hear this wisdom in respect of Yoga. Endowed with this wisdom, O Partha, best among My devotees, you shall utterly cast off the bondage of action, that is, the sin of the actions performed - 'cast off' meaning its complete removal, such that it does not arise again.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Thus, having removed both the delusions earlier troubling Arjuna - namely, first, that agency and the causing of killing are impossible for the Self, as taught in 'he who thinks of this one as the slayer... whom does he slay, whom does he cause to be slain?'; and then the injunction 'therefore arise for battle,' which might seem contradictory, since one who is a non-doer and all-pervading like space cannot properly be enjoined to act - the Lord now resolves this apparent conflict by assigning the two teachings to two different kinds of aspirants. 'This has been declared to you' refers to what was taught earlier, from 'you should not grieve for those beyond grief' through 'having regard also to your own duty' - namely, the knowledge in respect of Sankhya, the Upanishadic teaching by which the truth of things is fully known: that is, the knowledge of the Self taught in the Upanishads, the cause of the cessation of transmigratory existence, is what has been declared to you regarding Sankhya. 'But now, in respect of Yoga' - the topic to be defined later as 'evenness of mind in success and failure is called Yoga' - the word 'tu' here signals a distinction from what preceded. Accordingly, the Lord will later declare that knowledge and action belong to different types of aspirants: 'In this world a twofold path was taught by Me of old, O sinless one - the path of knowledge for the Sankhyas, and the path of action for the Yogis' (3.3) - thereby also dispelling any notion that knowledge and action are meant to be combined. 'This,' already touched upon briefly in the immediately preceding verses such as 'having regard also to your own duty,' is now to be explained at length - hear it. The Lord then goes on, in the next verse and a half beginning 'endowed with wisdom,' to praise this very wisdom.

Objection: it is heard elsewhere that the bondage of action is destroyed by knowledge of the Self alone - 'having known the Self through austerity, he is not tainted by sinful action'; whereas Karma-yoga would seem, if anything, to strengthen the bondage of action further - how then can it be said that one shall cast off the bondage of action through it? We answer: on the strength of another scripture, namely the Isha Upanishad - 'All this, whatever moves in this moving world, should be covered by the Lord; enjoy through that renunciation, and do not covet anyone's wealth. Even while performing actions here, one may wish to live a hundred years; thus for you there is no other way; action does not cling to a man' - which teaches that, since everything is sustained by the Lord, no one can act by his own independent will; therefore, being free of any sense of ownership everywhere, and having renounced the pride of doership and enjoyership, one should both enjoy experience and perform action - and action so performed leaves the doer untainted, there being no means to this end other than that. Hence, just as gold combined with a certain agent, such as iron, can be made to lose its impurity, so action itself, when endowed with a particular quality - namely, performance with equanimity and non-attachment - becomes the very cause of destroying its own kind, further bondage; and it is therefore rightly said that even through Karma-yoga one shall cast off the bondage of action.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Thus far I have described this knowledge to you through analytical study. Now listen as I explain it in terms of working without fruitive results. O son of Pṛthā, when you act in such knowledge you can free yourself from the bondage of works.

 According to the Nirukti, or the Vedic dictionary, saṅkhyā means that which describes things in detail, and sāṅkhya refers to that philosophy which describes the real nature of the soul. And yoga involves controlling the senses. Arjuna’s proposal not to fight was based on sense gratification. Forgetting his prime duty, he wanted to cease fighting, because he thought that by not killing his relatives and kinsmen he would be happier than by enjoying the kingdom after conquering his cousins and brothers, the sons of Dhṛtarāṣṭra. In both ways, the basic principles were for sense gratification. Happiness derived from conquering them and happiness derived by seeing kinsmen alive are both on the basis of personal sense gratification, even at a sacrifice of wisdom and duty. Kṛṣṇa, therefore, wanted to explain to Arjuna that by killing the body of his grandfather he would not be killing the soul proper, and He explained that all individual persons, including the Lord Himself, are eternal individuals; they were individuals in the past, they are individuals in the present, and they will continue to remain individuals in the future, because all of us are individual souls eternally. We simply change our bodily dress in different manners, but actually we keep our individuality even after liberation from the bondage of material dress. An analytical study of the soul and the body has been very graphically explained by Lord Kṛṣṇa. And this descriptive knowledge of the soul and the body from different angles of vision has been described here as Sāṅkhya, in terms of the Nirukti dictionary. This Sāṅkhya has nothing to do with the Sāṅkhya philosophy of the atheist Kapila. Long before the imposter Kapila’s Sāṅkhya, the Sāṅkhya philosophy was expounded in the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam by the true Lord Kapila, the incarnation of Lord Kṛṣṇa, who explained it to His mother, Devahūti. It is clearly explained by Him that the puruṣa, or the Supreme Lord, is active and that He creates by looking over the prakṛti. This is accepted in the Vedas and in the Gītā. The description in the Vedas indicates that the Lord glanced over the prakṛti, or nature, and impregnated it with atomic individual souls. All these individuals are working in the material world for sense gratification, and under the spell of material energy they are thinking of being enjoyers. This mentality is dragged to the last point of liberation when the living entity wants to become one with the Lord. This is the last snare of māyā, or sense gratificatory illusion, and it is only after many, many births of such sense gratificatory activities that a great soul surrenders unto Vāsudeva, Lord Kṛṣṇa, thereby fulfilling the search after the ultimate truth. Arjuna has already accepted Kṛṣṇa as his spiritual master by surrendering himself unto Him: śiṣyas te ’haṁ śādhi māṁ tvāṁ prapannam. Consequently, Kṛṣṇa will now tell him about the working process in buddhi-yoga, or karma-yoga, or in other words, the practice of devotional service only for the sense gratification of the Lord. This buddhi-yoga is clearly explained in Chapter Ten, verse ten, as being direct communion with the Lord, who is sitting as Paramātmā in everyone’s heart. But such communion does not take place without devotional service. One who is therefore situated in devotional or transcendental loving service to the Lord, or, in other words, in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, attains to this stage of buddhi-yoga by the special grace of the Lord. The Lord says, therefore, that only to those who are always engaged in devotional service out of transcendental love does He award the pure knowledge of devotion in love. In that way the devotee can reach Him easily in the ever-blissful kingdom of God. Thus the buddhi-yoga mentioned in this verse is the devotional service of the Lord, and the word Sāṅkhya mentioned herein has nothing to do with the atheistic sāṅkhya-yoga enunciated by the imposter Kapila. One should not, therefore, misunderstand that the sāṅkhya-yoga mentioned herein has any connection with the atheistic Sāṅkhya. Nor did that philosophy have any influence during that time; nor would Lord Kṛṣṇa care to mention such godless philosophical speculations. Real Sāṅkhya philosophy is described by Lord Kapila in the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam , but even that Sāṅkhya has nothing to do with the current topics. Here, Sāṅkhya means analytical description of the body and the soul. Lord Kṛṣṇa made an analytical description of the soul just to bring Arjuna to the point of buddhi-yoga, or bhakti-yoga. Therefore, Lord Kṛṣṇa’s Sāṅkhya and Lord Kapila’s Sāṅkhya, as described in the Bhāgavatam , are one and the same. They are all bhakti-yoga. Lord Kṛṣṇa said, therefore, that only the less intelligent class of men make a distinction between sāṅkhya-yoga and bhakti-yoga ( sāṅkhya-yogau pṛthag bālāḥ pravadanti na paṇḍitāḥ ). Of course, atheistic sāṅkhya-yoga has nothing to do with bhakti-yoga, yet the unintelligent claim that the atheistic sāṅkhya-yoga is referred to in the Bhagavad-gītā . One should therefore understand that buddhi-yoga means to work in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, in the full bliss and knowledge of devotional service. One who works for the satisfaction of the Lord only, however difficult such work may be, is working under the principles of buddhi-yoga and finds himself always in transcendental bliss. By such transcendental engagement, one achieves all transcendental understanding automatically, by the grace of the Lord, and thus his liberation is complete in itself, without his making extraneous endeavors to acquire knowledge. There is much difference between work in Kṛṣṇa consciousness and work for fruitive results, especially in the matter of sense gratification for achieving results in terms of family or material happiness. Buddhi-yoga is therefore the transcendental quality of the work that we perform.
