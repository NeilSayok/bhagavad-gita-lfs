Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The chief mark of the man of steady wisdom is the renunciation of all desires through the experience of the bliss of the Self. Sri Krishna now describes a second mark of the wise -- an even mind in both pleasure and pain. Here his conduct, as the experiencer of what comes to him through his identification with the body, is described.

The muni of steady wisdom is one who is free from attachment, fear and anger. If we study the lives of perfected beings we find in them qualities entirely opposite to those of ordinary men. Ordinary people display hundreds of feelings and traits which are absent in the man of wisdom, so it may seem surprising that only the absence of these three -- attachment, fear and anger -- is mentioned here. A doubt then arises: did Vyasa forget the other qualities? Does this verse give the complete description? On reflection, however, this doubt proves groundless.

The previous verse spoke of the wise man's freedom from desire; this one speaks of the steadiness of his mind. From experience of the many objects of the world we know that as attachment to them grows, fear also arises -- fear as to whether the longed-for object will actually be obtained, and even after it is obtained, anxiety about protecting it persists.

When an obstacle comes between a person overpowered by attachment and fear and his cherished object, the reaction that arises in the mind toward that obstacle is called anger. The intensity of the anger is proportional to the intensity of the attachment and fear behind it -- meaning that attachment itself, given the occasion, expresses itself as anger.

Shri Shankaracharya writes in his commentary that the man of wisdom remains steady of mind amid the three kinds of affliction: (a) adhyatmika -- disease and the like arising in one's own body; (b) adhibhautika -- affliction from external creatures such as tigers and thieves; (c) adhidaivika -- affliction from natural calamities such as storms and earthquakes. Just as fire flares up when fuel is added to it, so in the man of wisdom, even when many objects are placed before him like fuel, the fire of desire does not blaze up. Such a person is called the muni of steady wisdom.

And the teaching continues further.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
By restating this mark of the man of steady wisdom, the text teaches a further duty for the seeker desirous of knowledge. Sorrows are of three kinds: adhyatmika (caused by fever, headache and the like), adhibhautika (caused by tigers, snakes and the like) and adhidaivika (caused by storms, floods and the like). One whose mind remains unagitated even when such sorrows occur is described here; 'unagitated' means not overcome by the distress that arises from being unable to ward off sorrow once it has come. Since pleasures too are of the same three kinds, one who has no longing for them even when they are obtained is called 'free from longing.' The ignorant man's craving for pleasures grows as he obtains them, but not so the wise man's -- hence the analogy 'unlike fire': just as fire flares up when fed with fuel, the ignorant man's craving grows with the pleasures that come to him, but the wise man's craving does not grow in this way, for fire cannot burn what is not combustible even when it is brought near. The sense is that the seeker should give up both craving in pleasure and agitation in sorrow. That such a person has also given up attachment, fear and anger is conveyed by the word 'vita' (free from). Attachment (raga) is a kind of craving marked by delight in objects once experienced and clung to; fear is the mental disturbance -- visible even in changes of the body and eyes -- caused by apprehension of harm from another; anger is the particular agitated state of mind, born of feeling overpowered by another, that drives one to harm oneself or others. Accepting that such a muni is one who knows the Self, he is called a sannyasin, a renunciate. This state -- free from craving for pleasure and the rest, and free from attachment, fear and anger -- is what is spoken of here.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
He whose mind is not agitated when sorrows come, who has no longing when pleasures come, and who has become entirely free of attachment, fear and anger -- such a reflective (mananshil) person is called one of steady wisdom.

Explanation: Arjuna had asked, from the standpoint of outer conduct, how a man of steady wisdom speaks, sits and walks; but the Lord answers from the standpoint of inner attitude (bhava), because it is the inner attitude that is primary in all action. Every action is performed with some underlying attitude, and when the attitude changes, the action itself changes in essence, even though outwardly it may look the same. It is this inner attitude that the Lord is describing here.

'Unagitated in mind amid sorrows' -- even when sorrows are likely or actually occur -- obstacles in performing one's duty, insult and censure, an unfavourable result of action, and other such adversities -- his mind is not agitated. The reason the karmayogi's mind remains free of agitation and disturbance is that his chief concern is to act for the welfare of others, to perform his actions thoroughly, and to remain watchful that no attachment, possessiveness or desire creeps into the results of his actions. Because of this watchfulness he retains an inner contentment, and owing to that contentment his mind is not agitated no matter what adversity comes.

'Free from longing amid pleasures' -- even when pleasures are likely or actually occur -- his work going smoothly, receiving immediate respect and praise, favourable results and other such comforts -- there is no longing in him for these conditions to continue or recur permanently. Favourable circumstances leave no impression whatsoever on his inner being.

'Free from attachment, fear and anger' -- the coloring that worldly objects cast upon the mind is called 'attachment' (raga). When attachment to objects exists, if some powerful person destroys those objects, severs one's connection with them, or obstructs their attainment, 'fear' arises in the mind; if that person is weak, 'anger' arises instead. But one in whom the wish to bring happiness to others, to do good to them and to serve them has awakened, naturally loses his attachment. With the loss of attachment, fear and anger also disappear. Thus he becomes entirely free of attachment, fear and anger.

As long as agitation, longing, attachment, fear and anger remain even partially, one is still a seeker (sadhaka); when one becomes entirely free of them, one becomes perfected (siddha).

[Desire, craving and the like are all forms of attachment differing only in degree. The attachment hidden within the mind is called 'vasana'; its other names are 'attachment' (asakti) and 'fondness.' The wish that some object should come to me is called 'kamana' (desire); the possibility of that desire being fulfilled is called 'hope'; the wish for objects to increase, and for further objects, even after a desire is fulfilled, is called 'greed'; and greed grown excessive is called 'trishna' (craving). In short, the pull one feels toward perishable objects, along with regarding them as supremely important, is what is called by these various names -- vasana, kamana, and so on.]

'He is called a muni of steady wisdom' -- the intellect of such a reflective karmayogi becomes fixed and unwavering. Since the word 'muni' is generally associated with speech, the Lord used it in answering the question 'how does he speak'; but in truth the word 'muni' is not confined to speech alone -- this is why, in the seventeenth chapter, the Lord uses the word 'mauna' (silence) for mental austerity rather than for austerity of speech (17.16). Since this is the context of karmayoga, the reflective karmayogi is here called a muni. Reflectiveness (mananshilata) means constant watchfulness so that no desire or attachment enters the mind. Remaining perpetually unattached is itself the watchfulness of the perfected karmayogi, for it was this same watchfulness that he practiced earlier as a seeker (Gita 3.19), and it is through this that he has attained the Supreme Reality.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
An objection: since this defining mark has already been stated, why is it stated again here beginning with 'in sorrow'? The reply 'that very point' refers to the mark already given, now being clarified -- in particular by describing the abandonment of the other faults implied by the word 'desire.' A further objection: elsewhere, abandonment of desire and the like are spoken of as means to knowledge, as in 'freedom from pride' (13.7); so there would be an over-extension into the description meant for the seeker rather than the man of knowledge. This is answered by 'these' [qualities]: the word 'upaya' (means) is so called because its fruit ('aya') lies near at hand ('upa'). This is true, but for the seeker these qualities must be cultivated through effort, whereas in the man of knowledge they exist naturally -- so there is no fault. In support, the text cites an earlier statement. Those who hold that the very qualities marking the man of steady wisdom are themselves the means to liberation are refuted: means to knowledge are not, by themselves, means to liberation, as stated in the Brahma Sutra (3.4.15) 'and some, on account of desire' -- though their role in increasing bliss is indeed accepted. Alternatively, this is said because, having earlier stated 'but hear this regarding yoga' (2.39), one might ask how this present teaching could be anything other than yoga. Since 'free from longing' already implies freedom from attachment, the term is further explained: attachment (raga) is the illusory perception of excellence in objects that are not inherently disturbing -- this very perception is what is meant by the words rasa, raga and rakti, denoting desire (kama), i.e. the illusion of excellence (shobhanadhyasa).

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
The very qualities that serve as means to knowledge for the ordinary seeker are, for the man of steady wisdom, his own natural and intrinsic qualities -- this is conveyed by the verse beginning 'in sorrow.'

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Now are to be explained the speech, sitting and movement of the man of steady wisdom who has risen above worldly attachment, as distinct from those of the deluded. In answer to 'how does he speak,' two verses beginning with 'in sorrow' are given. Sorrows are of three kinds: adhyatmika, caused by grief, delusion, fever, headache and the like; adhibhautika, caused by tigers, snakes and the like; and adhidaivika, caused by excessive storms, excessive rain and the like. Amid such sorrows -- particular states of mind marked by the distress born of the transformation of rajas, and brought about by one's accumulated past sinful karma -- he whose mind does not become agitated, does not become distressed through inability to ward them off, is called 'unagitated in mind.' In the undiscerning person, when sorrow occurs, a deluded, tamasic mental state called agitation arises, taking the form of remorse: 'Alas, I am sinful, shame on this wretched self of mine who must bear such sorrow -- who will remove this sorrow of mine?' If such a reaction occurred at the time of committing a sin, it would at least serve the useful purpose of restraining that sinful act. But once the experience of the fruit of past action is already underway, its effect cannot be undone; so this reaction -- 'why is this pointless sorrow, whose cause serves no purpose, happening to me?' -- is merely a product of confused, undiscerning thinking, and cannot arise in the discerning man of steady wisdom. Only sorrow itself is brought about by past karma, not the subsequent delusion about it. [An objection, that this delusion too must be the fruit of some further past karma since it is a further cause of sorrow, is refuted:] for the man of steady wisdom, since the root cause of delusion -- ignorance -- has been destroyed, delusion cannot arise in him, and hence there is no store of past karma left to produce sorrow born of such delusion; only that residue of past karma needed to sustain the body somehow continues to bear fruit, even without delusion, through the principle of continuation of an already-commenced effect (this will be explained further ahead). Similarly, amid pleasures -- particular pleasurable mental states born of the transformation of sattva, arising from past virtuous karma, and again of three kinds -- he is 'free from longing,' meaning free from longing for future pleasures of the same kind. Longing means a tamasic, deluded state of mind that arises, at the time of experiencing a pleasure, as a futile craving for further pleasure of that kind without pursuing the virtuous action (dharma) that would actually cause it; this too arises only in the undiscerning, for an effect cannot arise without its cause. Hence, just as the discerning man does not have the futile agitation of wishing an effect not to occur when its cause is absent, so too he does not have the futile craving, in the form of longing, that an effect should occur when its cause is absent -- since past karma alone brings about pleasure. Or, the mental state of elation denoted by 'longing' is likewise a delusion -- the tamasic swelling of pride that says 'how fortunate I am to have such pleasure, who is my equal in this world, by what means can this pleasure of mine be preserved from ever ending?' This is why the traditional commentary states: 'free from longing' means one whose pleasures do not grow the way fire grows when fed with fuel. And it will later be said, 'he neither delights on obtaining what is pleasant nor is agitated on obtaining what is unpleasant' -- for that too cannot occur in the discerning man, since it is a form of delusion. Likewise, he is free from attachment, fear and anger. Attachment (raga) is a particular mental state, rooted in the illusion of excellence, marked by intense clinging delight in objects. Fear is the particular mental state of dejection that arises when, faced with the approaching destroyer of an object of attachment, one feels oneself powerless to prevent it. Anger, similarly, is the particular mental state of inflamed agitation that arises when, faced with the same threat, one believes oneself capable of resisting it. Since all these are contrary to his true nature and have departed from him, such a muni -- reflective, a true renunciate -- is called sthitaprajna, one of steady wisdom. It is this person, so characterized, who, in order to instruct disciples by revealing his own experience, speaks of freedom from agitation and longing and so on, as described in reply to Arjuna's question. The implication is that every seeker of liberation should likewise not be agitated in sorrow, should not be elated in pleasure, and should become free from attachment, fear and anger.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Further, regarding 'in sorrow': the view that, since the previous verse already answered the first question, this verse must instead describe the speech, sitting and movement of the man of steady wisdom risen above worldly attachment, as distinct from the deluded, in answer to 'how does he speak' -- this is questionable, since the concluding phrase 'is called one of steady wisdom' clearly shows that this verse itself completes the answer to the first question. He whose mind remains unagitated and unperturbed amid the threefold sorrows -- adhyatmika, adhidaivika and adhibhautika -- and from whom longing for the threefold pleasures has departed, and consequently from whom attachment, fear and anger have also departed -- such a one is then called sthitadhih, sthitaprajna, one of steady wisdom.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Furthermore: he whose mind is unagitated in sorrow, and whose longing, i.e. desire, for pleasures has departed -- such a muni, endowed with the nature of contemplation, is called sthitadhih, sthitaprajna. An objection: what results from being unagitated in sorrow and free of longing in pleasure? Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
'In sorrow' refers to the causes of sorrow such as being struck by weapons; even when these occur, he is unagitated in mind, unwavering. As will be said later, 'one established in that state is not shaken even by the heaviest sorrow.' 'In pleasures' refers to the causes of pleasure such as garlands and sandal-paste; even when these are obtained, he is free from longing, having attained cessation of desire. Consequently, attachment, fear and anger have departed from him, for in that state neither attachment nor sorrow and the like can arise. Such a person, established in samadhi, is called sthitadhih, sthitaprajna, one of steady wisdom.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
One who is not disturbed in mind even amidst the threefold miseries or elated when there is happiness, and who is free from attachment, fear and anger, is called a sage of steady mind.

 The word muni means one who can agitate his mind in various ways for mental speculation without coming to a factual conclusion. It is said that every muni has a different angle of vision, and unless a muni differs from other munis, he cannot be called a muni in the strict sense of the term. Nāsāv ṛṣir yasya mataṁ na bhinnam ( Mahābhārata, Vana-parva 313.117). But a sthita-dhīr muni, as mentioned herein by the Lord, is different from an ordinary muni. The sthita-dhīr muni is always in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, for he has exhausted all his business of creative speculation. He is called praśānta-niḥśeṣa-mano-rathāntara ( Stotra-ratna 43), or one who has surpassed the stage of mental speculations and has come to the conclusion that Lord Śrī Kṛṣṇa, or Vāsudeva, is everything ( vāsudevaḥ sarvam iti sa mahātmā su-durlabhaḥ ). He is called a muni fixed in mind. Such a fully Kṛṣṇa conscious person is not at all disturbed by the onslaughts of the threefold miseries, for he accepts all miseries as the mercy of the Lord, thinking himself only worthy of more trouble due to his past misdeeds; and he sees that his miseries, by the grace of the Lord, are minimized to the lowest. Similarly, when he is happy he gives credit to the Lord, thinking himself unworthy of the happiness; he realizes that it is due only to the Lord’s grace that he is in such a comfortable condition and able to render better service to the Lord. And, for the service of the Lord, he is always daring and active and is not influenced by attachment or aversion. Attachment means accepting things for one’s own sense gratification, and detachment is the absence of such sensual attachment. But one fixed in Kṛṣṇa consciousness has neither attachment nor detachment because his life is dedicated in the service of the Lord. Consequently he is not at all angry even when his attempts are unsuccessful. Success or no success, a Kṛṣṇa conscious person is always steady in his determination.
