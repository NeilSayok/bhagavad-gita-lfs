Life Application:
Consider the long-term spiritual and emotional consequences of retaliation before reacting to those who wrong you.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Even after Arjuna has said so much, Lord Krishna remains motionless and silent, like a statue. So Arjuna abandons his earlier language and, in a gentler tone, begins to offer emotional arguments in the manner of someone trying to explain something to a dull-witted friend. This changed strategy of Arjuna appears quite comical, given the Lord's continuing silence. In this verse, he first says that no good whatsoever will come from killing Duryodhana and the rest. Yet, seeing Krishna standing as silent as a statue even at this, he cannot understand the reason for this silence. He quickly recalls that the Kaurava family are aggressors, and that according to the rule of scripture an aggressor should be slain at once, whether he be a teacher, an elder, or a scripture-knowing brahmin -- there is no sin whatsoever in killing an aggressor. One who unjustly attacks another is called an aggressor.

Whatever wrong action we perform, contrary to our own pure divine nature, is called sin. To act while identifying oneself with the body, mind and intellect is not the mark of a noble person. Actions performed egotistically for self-interest build a firm wall of desire between us and our pure conscious Self. It is this that is called sin.

Arjuna's unreasoned opposition to killing his enemies is the result of his not understanding scripture, and to act according to one's own limited understanding is to destroy one's own culture. Therefore Lord Krishna neither praises nor criticizes Arjuna's arguments. He knows that Arjuna needs to be allowed to speak out his mind -- this is the best remedy for one in a disturbed mental state. In this way, his mind is gradually calmed.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Anticipating the objection that, should these Duryodhana and the rest not be restrained by you, then, having been restrained by them instead, you would end up suffering, he says, in 'Papam eva...': should these Duryodhana and the rest slay us -- innocent as we are -- here on this battlefield for no reason, then these aggressors, marked by traits such as 'the incendiary, the poisoner' and the rest, would themselves already be sinners on account of harming the innocent -- this is the sense. Or: even though these are indeed aggressors, still, by slaying these most lamentable figures, Duryodhana and the rest, the sin thereby incurred would attach to us alone; hence we ought not to kill them, is the sense. Or: by killing these -- our teachers, brothers, friends and the rest -- we ourselves would become aggressors, and the sin of that violence would then attach to us, the aggressors, ourselves; hence refraining from battle is what is truly beneficial for us, is the sense. Since no benefit results and only harm can come of it, he concludes that violence against others should not be undertaken, in 'Tasmat...'. Moreover, since battle undertaken for the sake of the happiness of a kingdom yields no happiness at all once one's own kinsmen are destroyed by it, he says that war ought not to be waged, in 'Svajanam hi...'.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O Janardana! What joy will we have from killing these kinsmen of Dhritarashtra? Killing these aggressors will only bring sin upon us.

Explanation: 'Nihatya dhartarashtran nah... hatvaitanatatayinah' -- what joy will we have from gaining victory by killing the sons of Dhritarashtra and their many allied soldiers? Even if, carried away by the force of anger or greed, we do kill them, once that force subsides we shall only be left weeping -- that is, we shall have to repent, thinking what calamity have we brought about by falling into anger and greed. Remembering our kinsmen, their absence will keep striking us again and again. Grief over their deaths will keep tormenting the mind. In such a state, can we ever feel joy? The point is: as long as we remain alive in this world after killing them, our minds will never know joy, and the sin we incur from killing them will bring us terrible suffering in the next world.

Aggressors are of six kinds: one who sets fire, one who administers poison, one who stands ready to strike with weapon in hand, one who steals wealth, one who seizes land (or kingdom), and one who abducts another's wife. Duryodhana and the rest fit all six of these marks. They tried to burn the Pandavas alive in the house of lac. They fed poison to Bhimasena and threw him into the water. They stood ready, weapons in hand, to kill the Pandavas. Through deceit in the game of dice they seized the Pandavas' wealth and kingdom. Duryodhana brought Draupadi into the full assembly and grievously insulted her, saying 'I have won you, you have become my slave' and the like; and at Duryodhana's instigation, Jayadratha abducted Draupadi.

According to scriptural statements, no fault (sin) whatsoever attaches to one who kills an aggressor -- 'no fault attaches to the slayer in the killing of an aggressor' (Manusmriti 8.351). But although killing an aggressor is proper, the act of killing itself is not a good thing. Scripture also says that one should never do violence to anyone -- 'do not injure any being'; non-violence is the highest duty -- 'ahimsa is the supreme dharma.' So why should we, falling prey to anger and greed, commit violence against our own kinsmen? Being aggressors, Duryodhana and the rest indeed deserve to be killed; but being our own kinsmen, killing them will bring sin upon us alone, because scripture says that one who destroys one's own family is the greatest sinner -- 'he alone is the most sinful who brings about the destruction of his own lineage.' So how can we kill these aggressors who are also our own close kinsmen? Severing our relationship with them, separating ourselves from them, is proper -- but killing them is not proper. Just as, if one's own son himself becomes an aggressor, one may sever one's relationship with him, but one can hardly kill him.

Transition: having described the evil consequence of war in the previous verse, Arjuna now shows the utter impropriety of waging war.

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
Nihatya etc. upto anususruma. Sin alone is the agent in the act of slaying these desperadoes. Therefore here the idea is this : These ememies of ours have been slain, i.e., have been take possession of, by sin. Sin would come to us also after slaying them. Sin in this context is the disregard, on account of greed etc., to the injurious conseences like the ruination of the family and the like. That is why Arjuna makes a specific mention of the [ruin of the] family etc., and of its duties in the passage 'How by slaying my own kinsmen etc'.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Since no benefit results and only harm can come of it, violence against others should not be undertaken -- he now concludes what was begun with 'Na cha shreyo'nupashyami' (nor do I see any good). The word 'tat' (that) refers back to both the absence of unseen fruit and the arising of harm. He then states the absence of visible happiness, in 'Svajanam hi...'. By addressing Him as 'Madhava' (husband of the goddess of fortune), Arjuna hints: since You are the Lord of Lakshmi, You ought not to induce me into an inauspicious act.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
One might object: it is recalled in the smriti that 'the incendiary, the poisoner, the one who attacks with weapons, the plunderer of wealth, the seizer of land, and the abductor of another's wife -- these six are aggressors.' By these six marks, these Duryodhana and the rest are indeed aggressors, and the killing of aggressors is indeed proper: 'one may slay an approaching aggressor without hesitation; no fault attaches to the slayer of an aggressor.' To this he answers, across a verse and a half, in 'Papam...': the teaching 'one may slay an approaching aggressor' belongs to political science (artha-shastra), which is weaker than sacred law (dharma-shastra); as Yajnavalkya states, 'when the smritis conflict, reasoning grounded in worldly practice prevails; and dharma-shastra prevails over artha-shastra.' Hence, even in killing these aggressors -- our elders and the rest -- sin alone would attach to us, on account of its impropriety and unrighteousness, and no happiness whatsoever would come of it here, is what he shows, in 'Svajanam hi...'.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
One might object: you would surely feel satisfaction from killing the sons of Dhritarashtra, who have caused you so much harm. To this he answers, in 'Nihatya...': what satisfaction would we have in killing the sons of Dhritarashtra? None whatsoever, is the sense. Even toward an elder who has wronged one, wrong should not be done in return -- this is the intent behind mentioning the relation to Dhritarashtra. Whereas by slaying the wicked, satisfaction accrues to Your own kinsmen, we have no such satisfaction either, seeing the certain destruction of our own kinsmen -- this Arjuna hints by addressing Him as 'Janardana.' By this address, some say, Arjuna hints: if these deserve killing, then You Yourself should slay them -- for even in destroying all beings at the time of dissolution, You remain untouched by any sin. One might object further: by the teaching that 'the incendiary, the poisoner... these six are aggressors,' and by the teaching that 'no fault attaches in the killing of an aggressor,' sin will not attach to you for killing these aggressors -- rather, these very ones are the aggressors. To this he answers, in 'Papam...': killing these aggressors, sin alone would attach to us who remain, is the connection. By statements such as 'these two types of persons in the world pierce the solar orb: the renunciate established in yoga, and one who dies facing the enemy in battle,' which declare the special merit attained by one slain in this way, sin's removal is thereby indicated -- so the sin of this violence attaches to us, the agents of the killing, alone. And the teaching 'one may slay an approaching aggressor without hesitation; no fault attaches to the slayer of an aggressor,' being merely political science, is overridden by the more powerful sacred law, 'one should not injure any being.' As Yajnavalkya states this very intent: 'when the smritis conflict, reasoning grounded in worldly practice prevails; and dharma-shastra prevails over artha-shastra.' Whether one reads it as 'sin alone would attach to us who, without engaging in battle, kill these aggressors,' or as 'sin alone would attach to us, having killed these aggressors' -- in all three readings, the past-participle 'having killed' (tyaktva-type construction) works by supplying an implied verb of remaining/continuing.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
O Janardana -- destroyer of all ignorance -- what satisfaction would we have in killing these sons of Dhritarashtra, devoid as they are of the eye of true knowledge? None whatsoever, is the sense. By the address 'Janardana,' Arjuna hints at the impropriety of our acting in this way toward those who belong to You. Since they, as sons of Dhritarashtra, fail to perceive You, their acting thus toward us is understandable -- this is hinted at by the word 'Dhartarashtra' itself. Or: what satisfaction would You have in killing the sons of Dhritarashtra, who are, after all, our own kinsmen? The sense here is: from a worldly standpoint they are indeed ours; in slaying them we would have no satisfaction -- so if it is for Your satisfaction that they must be slain, then, by whatever means, it is You who ought to be pleased, is the sense. One might object: they are indeed aggressors, by the definition 'the incendiary, the poisoner... these six are aggressors' and by 'one may slay an approaching aggressor without hesitation; no fault attaches to the slayer of an aggressor' -- so whether satisfaction results or not, since they possess all these faults, they surely deserve killing; they alone bear responsibility, You are merely the instrument. To this he answers, in 'Papam eva ashrayet...': having killed these aggressors, sin alone would attach to us. Moreover, the absence of fault in killing an aggressor has been established only from the standpoint of sacred law or of political science, not from the standpoint of devotion; from the standpoint of the path of devotion, both of those being weaker authorities, killing them would bring sin upon us alone, and through that sin our very relationship with the Lord would be severed -- for it has been established that only 'men whose sins are exhausted' attain that relationship, as stated in the Gita itself (verse 40).

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Aggressors: 'the incendiary, the poisoner, the one who attacks with weapons, the plunderer of wealth, the seizer of land, and the abductor of another's wife -- these six are aggressors.' 'One may slay an approaching aggressor without hesitation; no fault attaches to the slayer of an aggressor.' Even though this is so, still, by killing these, sin alone would attach to us. For the killing of an aggressor is enjoined by political science (artha-shastra), whereas 'one should not injure any being' is sacred law (dharma-shastra), and that is stronger than the former. As Yajnavalkya states: 'when the smritis conflict, reasoning grounded in worldly practice prevails; and dharma-shastra prevails over artha-shastra.' An alternative construction: 'having killed us, sin alone would attach to these aggressors' -- meaning: let these very ones perish through killing us, but let us not perish through killing them, is the sense.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Sin will overcome us if we slay such aggressors. Therefore it is not proper for us to kill the sons of Dhritarashtra and our friends. What should we gain, O Krishna, husband of the goddess of fortune, and how could we be happy by killing our own kinsmen?

 According to Vedic injunctions there are six kinds of aggressors: (1) a poison giver, (2) one who sets fire to the house, (3) one who attacks with deadly weapons, (4) one who plunders riches, (5) one who occupies another's land, and (6) one who kidnaps a wife. Such aggressors are at once to be killed, and no sin is incurred by killing such aggressors. Such killing of aggressors is quite befitting any ordinary man, but Arjuna was not an ordinary person. He was saintly by character, and therefore he wanted to deal with them in saintliness. This kind of saintliness, however, is not for a kshatriya. Although a responsible man in the administration of a state is required to be saintly, he should not be cowardly. For example, Lord Rama was so saintly that people even now are anxious to live in the kingdom of Lord Rama (rama-rajya), but Lord Rama never showed any cowardice. Ravana was an aggressor against Rama because Ravana kidnapped Rama's wife, Sita, but Lord Rama gave him sufficient lessons, unparalleled in the history of the world. In Arjuna's case, however, one should consider the special type of aggressors, namely his own grandfather, own teacher, friends, sons, grandsons, etc. Because of them, Arjuna thought that he should not take the severe steps necessary against ordinary aggressors. Besides that, saintly persons are advised to forgive. Such injunctions for saintly persons are more important than any political emergency. Arjuna considered that rather than kill his own kinsmen for political reasons, it would be better to forgive them on grounds of religion and saintly behavior. He did not, therefore, consider such killing profitable simply for the matter of temporary bodily happiness. After all, kingdoms and pleasures derived therefrom are not permanent, so why should he risk his life and eternal salvation by killing his own kinsmen? Arjuna's addressing of Krishna as 'Madhava,' or the husband of the goddess of fortune, is also significant in this connection. He wanted to point out to Krishna that, as the husband of the goddess of fortune, He should not induce Arjuna to take up a matter which would ultimately bring about misfortune. Krishna, however, never brings misfortune to anyone, to say nothing of His devotees.
