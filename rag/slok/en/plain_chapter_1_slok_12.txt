Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Because of Duryodhana's foolish talkativeness, the warriors of his army were in a strange state of mind; a shadow of gloom was beginning to fall on them too, and Bhishma, standing nearby, saw it. Grandsire Bhishma understood the anger hidden in industrious Dronacharya's silence. He realized everyone needed to be lifted out of this mood, or else, if left as it was, the warriors would be rendered ineffective when battle was imminent. Understanding the warriors' state of mind, commander Bhishma, with his full strength, blew the conch to stir waves of joy and confidence in the hearts of Duryodhana and all the soldiers. Though Bhishmacharya's conch-blowing was prompted by compassion for Duryodhana, its effect proved to be a declaration of the start of war -- much as, in modern wars, the first shot fired marks the war's beginning. With this lion-roar of the conch the Mahabharata war began, and from history's viewpoint it is the Kauravas who are proven the aggressors.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Seeing the king (Duryodhana) overcome by fear while conversing thus with the teacher, Grandsire Bhishma, standing nearby, acted thus to comply with his wishes -- so it is said. Truly producing joy -- a special elation in the mind -- in king Duryodhana, arising from the prospect of defeating the enemy and securing his own victory, and seeking to dispel his fear, he roared loudly a lion's roar and blew his conch to the full. Why does the grandsire strive to produce joy in Duryodhana? Because, being the eldest of the Kurus, being the Kuru king's own grandfather, it is fitting that his effort should aim at removing Duryodhana's fear, since he depends on him and is under his authority; and by his lion-roar and conch-sound, distress is bound to arise in the hearts of the enemy, since fear-inducing might of this kind acts even at a distance upon a mass of foes.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Duryodhana's heart being filled with joy, the aged and glorious grandsire Bhishma, roaring like a lion, blew his conch loudly.

Explanation: 'tasya sanjanayan harsham' -- although joy arising in Duryodhana's heart is the effect and the conch-sound the cause, so the conch-sound should be described first and the joy after -- i.e., it should say 'blowing the conch, he cheered Duryodhana.' But instead it says 'cheering Duryodhana, Bhishma blew the conch.' Sanjaya says it this way to convey that the very act of Bhishma's conch-blowing would itself produce joy in Duryodhana's heart. To highlight Bhishma's influence, Sanjaya further adds the epithet 'pratapavan' (glorious).

'Kuruvriddhah' -- although among the Kurus, Bahlika (younger brother of Bhishma's father Shantanu) was older in age than Bhishma, still, among all the Kuru elders, Bhishma was the one who best knew dharma and the Divine. Hence, being wisest of the elders, Sanjaya gives him the epithet 'kuruvriddhah.'

'Pratapavan' -- Bhishma's renunciation carried great influence: he renounced both wealth and marriage, never accepting the kingdom nor taking a wife. He was also highly skilled in the use of weapons and deeply learned in scripture; both these qualities had great influence on people. When Bhishma alone abducted the daughters of the king of Kashi from their svayamvara for his brother Vichitravirya, all the assembled kshatriyas attacked him, but he alone defeated them all; he did not even accept defeat before his own teacher Parashurama. Thus in matters of arms he had great influence over the kshatriyas. When Bhishma lay on his bed of arrows, Lord Krishna told Yudhishthira: 'If you have any doubt regarding dharma, ask Bhishma, for the sun of scriptural knowledge is setting' -- meaning Bhishma was departing this world. Thus in matters of scripture too he had great influence over others.

The word 'pitamahah' (grandsire) suggests that Dronacharya gave no answer to Duryodhana's cunningly spoken words, thinking Duryodhana wished to deceive him, and so remained silent. But being the grandfather, Bhishma saw only childishness in Duryodhana's cunning; hence, unlike Dronacharya, Grandsire Bhishma did not stay silent but, out of affection, blew the conch to cheer Duryodhana.

'Roaring a lion's roar loudly, he blew the conch' -- just as a lion's roar terrifies even great beasts like elephants, so too, by this very roar, everyone would become afraid and Duryodhana would be pleased -- with this intent Bhishma roared like a lion and blew the conch loudly.

Transition: what resulted from Grandsire Bhishma's conch-blowing, Sanjaya tells in the next verse.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Dhrtarastra said - Sanjaya said  Duryodhana, after viewing the forces of Pandavas protected by Bhima, and his own forces protected by Bhisma conveyed his views thus to Drona, his teacher, about the adeacy of Bhima's forces for conering the Kaurava forces and the inadeacy of his own forces for victory against the Pandava forces. He was grief-stricken within.

Observing his (Duryodhana's) despondecny, Bhisma, in order to cheer him, roared like a lion, and then blowing his conch, made his side sound their conchs and kettle-drums, which made an uproar as a sign of victory. Then, having heard that great tumult, Arjuna and Sri Krsna the Lord of all lords, who was acting as the charioteer of Arjuna, sitting in their great chariot which was powerful enough to coner the three worlds; blew their divine conchs Srimad Pancajanya and Devadatta. Then, both Yudhisthira and Bhima blew their respective conchs separately. That tumult rent asunder the hearts of your sons, led by Duryodhana. The sons of Dhrtarastra then thought, 'Our cause is almost lost now itself.' So said Sanjaya to Dhrtarastra who was longing for their victory.

Sanjaya said to Dhrtarastra:  Then, seeing the Kauravas, who were ready for battle, Arjuna, who had Hanuman, noted for his exploit of burning Lanka, as the emblem on his flag on his chariot, directed his charioteer Sri Krsna, the Supreme Lord-who is overcome by parental love for those who take shelter in Him who is the treasure-house of knowledge, power, lordship, energy, potency and splendour, whose sportive delight brings about the origin, sustentation and dissolution of the entire cosmos at His will, who is the Lord of the senses, who controls in all ways the senses inner and outer of all, superior and inferior - by saying, 'Station my chariot in an appropriate place in order that I may see exactly my enemies who are eager for battle.'

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
With the thought 'whether he praises me or blames me, this body will fall regardless,' Bhishma, cheering him, roared a lion's roar and had the conch played -- so it is said. Thus, seeing the acharya's indifference -- for Duryodhana, terrified at the sight of the Pandava army, had gone to the acharya seeking refuge from his fear through deceit, and now felt unappreciated since the acharya dismissed him with mere words, thinking 'he is deceiving me again' -- Bhishma, understanding this, and himself effectively praised by Duryodhana's words 'in the fronts...', roared a great lion's roar loudly, generating in the king a fear-dispelling joy, a special elation of mind signaling his own coming victory. (Alternatively, 'simhanadam' may be read as a verbal-noun form, a repeated use of the same root as in 'purnam pushyati' etc.) He blew (dadhmau) the conch, i.e., played it. Being eldest of the Kurus, he understood the intentions of both the acharya and Duryodhana; being the grandfather, he could not remain indifferent as the acharya had; being glorious, his loud lion-roar followed by conch-playing was meant to instill fear in the enemy. Here, though the lion-roar and conch-playing occur one after the other while both serve to produce joy, the participle 'generating' (in the verse) should be understood as expressing an ongoing, inevitably-occurring present action, like in the phrase 'sacrificing for the sake of subduing.'

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Thus, having heard of his own secondary importance and seeing the acharya sitting silently, and noticing the dejected Duryodhana's deep devotion toward himself, Bhishma set about producing joy in him -- so it is said. Truly producing in Duryodhana joy, a special mental elation, achieved through the lion-roar and conch-sound, in order to dispel his sorrow, he roared a loud lion's roar and blew the conch, filling it fully. Being 'kuruvriddhah pitamahah' -- eldest of the Kurus and grandfather -- since Duryodhana depends on him and is under his authority, it is indeed fitting that Bhishma should act as stated. To dispel any doubt about his capability, the word 'pratapavan' (glorious) is used. Some say: being eldest of the Kurus, he understood the intentions of both acharya and Duryodhana; being grandfather, he could not remain indifferent as the acharya had.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Venkatanatha explains: The verse '1.12' shows that Duryodhana's grief naturally preceded the joy Bhishma was about to produce -- this is confirmed by the earlier phrase 'became dejected within himself' (1.11) and by the later phrase 'that uproar rent asunder the hearts of Dhritarashtra's sons' (1.19), which again speaks of the sons of Dhritarashtra being shaken at heart. Since the army and its commander were described in full at the outset, and since at the close mere conch-sound is said to shake hearts, and since in between grief arises and is then dispelled by the promised joy, and since this fits the verse's own natural sense, this is indeed the intended meaning; hence the word 'tasmat' ('therefore', i.e. 'tat') is properly causal. This also answers the objection about the awkward remote reference in verse 1.10. Nor is the enemy force something Duryodhana only sees indirectly now -- earlier too there are direct references such as 'having seen the Pandava army' (1.2), 'behold these' (1.3), 'these' (1.10). As for the objection that it is improper to attribute weakness to one's own army when it is protected by Bhishma and Drona -- that too fails, since even a conditional death of Bhishma, Drona, etc. is something Duryodhana, aware of the condition (Shikhandi, etc.), could reasonably fear. As for the objection that Duryodhana elsewhere repeatedly asserts his own army's strength (e.g. 'Do not fear, O king') and there is now no reason for the opposite impression -- that too fails: just as Arjuna, though he takes up bow and arrow intending to fight, is overwhelmed by intense attachment, compassion, dharma, and fear on actually seeing his kinsmen before him, and has to be steadied again by the Lord, so too here, upon the direct sight of a firmly arrayed enemy force full of great warriors, Duryodhana, overcome by intense fear and dejection, has to be steadied by Bhishma -- what is improper about that? Duryodhana himself had directly witnessed, in the cattle-raid and other episodes (Virata parva), that the enemy's strength exceeded that of all his own warriors combined; he has not forgotten this now -- indeed the Mahabharata itself says his own mind was 'terrified by Arjuna.' As for the objection that the wording here should match the confident wording used at the start of the second day's battle -- that too is weak, since partial similarity does not require total similarity; and if one asks why the sentiment differs between the first and second day, it is because the course of a battle is inherently variable, so moods naturally differ. Moreover, here Bhishma and Drona were actively engaged in arranging the stationing of troops at the different entrances of the formation for mutual protection, which fits this mood -- as shown by 'having reported to the teacher, he became dejected within.' On the second day, by contrast, Duryodhana was busy displaying his own courage and encouraging all the allied kings who supported him, hence the different manner of speaking -- no fault there. It is with all this in mind that the word 'tu' is used in 'having seen' (1.2). And this entire passage -- Duryodhana's unforeseen grief at the very outset, and his statement that his own force is insufficient -- foreshadows the defeat to come. Hence, since the plain sense follows the universally accepted reading and is free of defect, the alternative readings (variant reading, discontinuous construal, sentence-split, etc.) are all undermined, since positing an unestablished sense is itself a serious flaw. In the sentence-split construal, moreover, the two reasons cannot properly connect to the two respective claims in the intended order -- whichever side, strong or weak, protects a given force should be the reason for that force's sufficiency or insufficiency, not for the opposing force's, if stated as a resultant claim; even granting an inverted connection, this again amounts to a discontinuous construal, and if the two reasons are instead taken jointly for each claim, the same awkwardness of discontinuity remains, compounded further by the absence of any explicit conjunctive word. Similar defects apply to the remaining alternatives. Therefore the traditional (Ramanuja's) reading alone gives the correct meaning.

Venkatanatha then glosses the verse's grammar: from 'tasya sanjanayan' up to 'tumulo 'bhavat' (verse 13's close), he explains that the word 'janayan' (producing), being a present participle in the sense of purpose or cause of an ongoing action (per Panini's grammar), is glossed as 'in order to produce.' 'Simhanadam vinadya' (having roared a lion's roar) is explained on the pattern of 'cooking the rice' -- hence the added word 'having done' (krtva) is used to convey the sense 'having also done the conch-blowing,' illustrating that roots like 'kr' (do), 'bhu' (be), 'as' (be) denote action in general. In the phrase 'tatah shankhah' (then, the conches...) of the following verse, the word 'tatah' (then) is said to indicate that it was Bhishma the commander who caused the conches to be sounded, in order to signal eagerness for victory -- hence the gloss 'caused to be done.' 'Shankha-bheri' (conch and kettledrum) is a synecdoche for all such instruments; so too the naming of a few specific instruments in the verse is meant to represent the whole category. The second 'cha' (and) joins the combined sound of conches, kettledrums etc. to the lion-roar and conch-blowing; and the word 'krtva' (having done) is a third element joining in the sense of 'caused to be done.'

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having heard the king's words which, in effect, honored him greatly by saying 'the commander alone is to be protected,' Bhishma, producing joy in the king, made a conch-sound proclaiming his own strength -- so it is said. Producing the king's joy fully -- in the manner of 'I shall fight' and so on. Since Bhishma is devoted, it was not from his own joy at the prospect of the enemy's defeat that he played the conch, but rather, having heard Duryodhana's words and understood the divine will, he did so in order to produce joy in the king -- this is what is meant to be conveyed. 'Kuruvriddhah' -- eldest among or in the Kurus, possessed of knowledge fitting for the time and place; 'pitamahah' (grandfather) is stated as the reason for producing joy. Bhishma roared a great lion's roar loudly, with head raised, as a display of his own prowess, signaling that no rival exists, and blew the conch, i.e., played it. Now, one might object: even though the king showed him great honor, is it proper for Bhishma to roar and blow the conch before the king in this way? To this doubt he replies with 'pratapavan' (glorious) -- by the very roar itself, victory over the enemy is signaled.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
This verse is Sanjaya's account, following Duryodhana's speech thus. 'Simhanadam' is a verbal-noun form; the verb 'vinadya' (having roared) is used in conjunction with it by an irregular formation, similar to 'samulakasham kashatisma daityan' and the like. 'Kuruvriddhah' refers to Bhishma. Having earlier seen, at Virata's city and elsewhere, the manifest power of the Pandavas, and fearing the king (Duryodhana) might be afraid, he blew the conch. He produced 'harsham,' i.e., enthusiasm for battle. The suffix denotes purpose -- the meaning is 'in order to produce joy.'

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Then Bhīṣma, the great valiant grandsire of the Kuru dynasty, the grandfather of the fighters, blew his conchshell very loudly, making a sound like the roar of a lion, giving Duryodhana joy.

 The grandsire of the Kuru dynasty could understand the inner meaning of the heart of his grandson Duryodhana, and out of his natural compassion for him he tried to cheer him by blowing his conchshell very loudly, befitting his position as a lion. Indirectly, by the symbolism of the conchshell, he informed his depressed grandson Duryodhana that he had no chance of victory in the battle, because the Supreme Lord Kṛṣṇa was on the other side. But still, it was his duty to conduct the fight, and no pains would be spared in that connection.
