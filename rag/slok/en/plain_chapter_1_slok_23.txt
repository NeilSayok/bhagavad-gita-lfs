Speaker: Arjuna

Slok:
yotsyamānānavekṣe.ahaṃ ya ete.atra samāgatāḥ .
dhārtarāṣṭrasya durbuddheryuddhe priyacikīrṣavaḥ

Life Application:
Identify the underlying motives behind conflicts around you so you can respond with wisdom rather than anger.

Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:
And gaze over this array of soldiers, eager to please the sinful sons of Dhritarashtra."

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Arjuna, in this verse, emphasizes the very point stated in the previous verse. He also clarifies here the reason for surveying the enemy forces. Being a man of action, he does not wish to invite any unnecessary trouble. So he wants to see who these ill-advised men, intoxicated with power and driven by temptation, are -- who have joined the Kaurava army and are supporting the utterly unjust tyrant Duryodhana.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
In the absence of opponents, how could your eagerness for battle bear fruit? To this he answers, in 'Yotsyamanan...': whatever kings have assembled here at Kurukshetra from various lands, I perceive them as intent on fighting, having taken up their weapons, exceedingly eager for battle -- meaning, the opponents are indeed numerous. But since these kings bear us no prior enmity, how can they be reckoned as opponents? To this he answers, in 'Dhartarashtrasya...': these kings, unable to see a way to protect Duryodhana, ill-minded son of Dhritarashtra, who is bent on inciting war, are seen assembled, wishing to please him by standing on the battlefield in this war -- hence their status as our adversaries, though only incidental, is established.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Let me look upon these kings, wishing to please the ill-minded Duryodhana in battle, who have come to this army eager for battle.

Explanation: 'Dhartarashtrasya durbuddheryuddhe priyachikirshavah' -- here, by calling Duryodhana 'ill-minded,' Arjuna wishes to convey that this Duryodhana has, until now, hatched many kinds of plots to destroy us. He has made many efforts to insult us. By rule and by justice we are entitled to half the kingdom, but he wishes to usurp even that, unwilling to give it. Such is his wicked intent; and yet the kings gathered here wish to please him in this very war! In truth, it is the duty of friends to act and speak in a way that benefits their friend in this world and the next. But these kings, instead of purifying Duryodhana's wicked intent, wish rather to strengthen it -- by helping him wage war, they are in fact bringing about his downfall. The point is: they give no thought at all to what would actually benefit Duryodhana, by what means he might gain the kingdom, and by what means his hereafter might be improved. Had these kings advised him: 'Brother, keep at least half the kingdom for yourself, and give the Pandavas' half back to the Pandavas,' then Duryodhana would have kept half the kingdom, and his hereafter too would have been improved.

'Yotsyamananavekshe'ham ya ete'tra samagatah' -- let me just look at these who are so eager for battle! They have taken the side of unrighteousness and injustice, so they will not be able to stand before us; they will be destroyed.

By saying 'yotsyamanan' (those who are about to fight), Arjuna means: their minds are set strongly on war, so let me really see who they are.

Transition: what the Lord did when Arjuna spoke thus, Sanjaya tells in the next two verses.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Beginning with 'atha vyavasthitan' up to 'bhishmadronapramukhatah' (1.25): then, seeing Dhritarashtra's sons standing eager for battle, monkey-bannered Arjuna said to Hrishikesha, who stood as his charioteer and sustains all who take refuge in Him: 'Station my chariot between the two armies, so long as I look upon these men.'

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
One might object: since these are all kinsmen, will they not simply arrange a peace among themselves, so why fight at all? To this he answers: these -- Bhishma, Drona and the rest -- wishing to please Duryodhana, ill-minded son of Dhritarashtra, who knows no way to protect himself, in battle (not in removing his ill-mindedness or the like), I perceive as intent on fighting, not as desiring peace. Hence it is entirely proper to look upon these opponents before the battle.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
'Yotsyamanan...' -- the construal is: I shall look upon these who have gathered here wishing to please Duryodhana -- for as long as I look upon them, station my chariot that long between the two armies.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
He elaborates on what was said as 'desiring to fight, standing ready,' in 'Yotsyamanan...': I perceive these as intent on fighting, not as desiring peace, nor as standing there in order to dissuade Duryodhana, ill-minded son of Dhritarashtra, but rather as wishing to please him. Whatever warriors have gathered here on this battlefield wish to please wicked-minded Duryodhana, though they are not truly capable of doing so -- this is the sense. By the word 'Dhartarashtrasya' (son of Dhritarashtra), it is also hinted that Dhritarashtra himself is ill-minded.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Venkatanatha explains the passage from 'atha vyavasthitan' up to 'Kurun' (1.25): the whole is a single sentence resolved in three parts. The first sentence explains the sense of 'priyachikirshavah' (those wishing to please). Regarding 'vyavasthitan' -- the word 'vi' signals a special nuance -- namely 'eager for battle,' as is likewise stated shortly after, 'standing ready, wishing to fight.' Regarding 'kapidhvajah' -- to preclude any diminishment implied by merely calling him 'monkey-bannered,' the epithet recalls the boon Hanuman granted at the lotus expedition; and to convey that the mere sight of Hanuman would strike terror into the enemy just as into demons, he is elsewhere called 'the monkey-bannered one whose banner burned Lanka.' The word 'Achyuta' (infallible), establishing the Lord's unfailing nature, is explained through the terms 'jnana' etc.; the term 'Hrishikesha' is explained via 'para' and 'apara' (transcendent and immanent) -- or, the creation and so forth, characterized by His powers, is itself the very meaning of the word 'Hrishikesha.' As stated in the Ahirbudhnya Samhita: 'Being the Lord, He delights manifestly through the sport of creation; His being Hrishikesha, His lordship, and His divinity are thereby made clear. Being endowed, through His own unchanging nature, with the form of vigor called the senses (hrishika), the Lord, ever through independence, engages eternally in the act of creation and the rest; this state of possessing lordship and vigor is called 'Hrishikeshatva.'' As for the word 'Achyuta' meaning 'one who does not let go of those who take refuge in Him, and hence has no falling away' -- he shows this etymology through the phrase 'out of affection for His devotees,' etc. 'Seated as My charioteer' -- the sense being: for one who, as Hrishikesha, governs all the senses in every way, what great matter is it to govern the mere reins of a chariot? Regarding 'nirikshe' (let me look upon) -- the prefix conveys the sense of 'thoroughly.' The word 'yavat' (as long as) here conveys either completeness, or the time-limit of the looking, or, per grammar, the future tense marker in the sense of continuous looking. The verse says 'those with whom I must fight, let me look upon them' -- not simply 'let me observe those with whom I must fight' -- hence the rise of the following verse 'yotsyamanan' (those about to fight). Also, since 'dhartarashtrasya durbuddheh' (of the ill-counseled son of Dhritarashtra) is meant to expose Duryodhana's specific fault, there is no redundancy. Alternatively: since 'between the two armies' has already been mentioned, and since the coming verse says 'he saw them standing in both armies,' this earlier verse concerns those of one's own side and their allies standing in one's own army -- meaning, with whom, standing alongside, must I fight the enemy; the next verse concerns the Kaurava allies standing in the opposing army -- this distinction is clear. Even though he already knew all this beforehand... Seeing the present state of agitation and the like in particular, in order to facilitate the appropriate battle-duties that would follow, Arjuna desired to see them properly here -- as stated in 'between the two armies...'

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
'Avekshe' -- moreover, of ill-minded Dhritarashtra's son -- since he stands opposed to the Lord Himself and, blind (to consequence), does not reflect on his own coming defeat, being the son of a blind father -- those who wish to please him are equally blind; such are these who have properly assembled here for battle -- them, engaged in fighting, I shall observe; until then, station my chariot between the two armies -- this connects with the earlier verse. In this stationing of the chariot in the middle, I have no fear whatsoever, since You are Achyuta (infallible). Thus, in these four verses, the comparison to battle has also been stated.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
'Yotsyamanan' means those intent on fighting, not those desiring peace. Since they wish to please the ill-minded one, it is thereby suggested that they too are of the same character.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Let me see those who have come here to fight, wishing to please the evil-minded son of Dhṛtarāṣṭra.

 It was an open secret that Duryodhana wanted to usurp the kingdom of the Pāṇḍavas by evil plans, in collaboration with his father, Dhṛtarāṣṭra. Therefore, all persons who had joined the side of Duryodhana must have been birds of the same feather. Arjuna wanted to see them on the battlefield before the fight was begun, just to learn who they were, but he had no intention of proposing peace negotiations with them. It was also a fact that he wanted to see them to make an estimate of the strength which he had to face, although he was quite confident of victory because Kṛṣṇa was sitting by his side.
