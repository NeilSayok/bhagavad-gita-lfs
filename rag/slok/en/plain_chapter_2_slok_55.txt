Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Having described the inner and outer life of the God-realized person, the Gita here helps us distinguish the true teacher from hypocritical ones who, like bears in sheep's clothing, only wear the outer garb of a guru. This section is of special importance to seekers, since it describes in detail the values of life needed for Self-realization and the state the mind should be in under different circumstances. The very opening verse of this section lists all the characteristics of the inner mental state of the man of wisdom that we need to know. Only when we are familiar with the distinctive fragrance of these flower-like words blossoming in the garden of the Upanishads can we truly grasp the sense in which they are used here.

One who has renounced all the desires abiding in the mind is called a man of steady wisdom. Studying this verse in the context of all that Sri Krishna has said so far, we can, through Vyasa's inspiring words, actually experience the fragrance of the Upanishads.

The intellect, when clouded by ignorance of one's real nature as the Self, becomes fertile soil in which desires sprout and flourish. But for one whose ignorance has been dispelled by true experiential knowledge of the Self, becoming free of desire follows naturally -- here the absence of the effect (desirelessness) is used to indicate the absence of the cause (ignorance). Where there are no desires, ignorance has already been destroyed, and knowledge is already shining forth.

If this alone -- the mere absence of desire -- were the one criterion that distinguished the wise man from ordinary people, then any educated person today would consider a Hindu sage simply mad, since after Self-realization such a person would supposedly lack even the capacity to desire. What is desire? It is the mind's capacity to plan, for the future, the acquisition of something that will make one happier than before. The criticism of materialists is that the man of wisdom loses even this capacity.

But this verse cannot be criticized in that way, because its second line clarifies that the man of wisdom remains content in his own blissful nature. The verse does not merely say that he gives up all desires -- it also affirms that he definitely experiences the bliss of the Self. It is well known that a child abandons the toys he once played with as he grows into youth; and further, as a person grows old, his desires change, and the things that once seemed so attractive in youth no longer hold any charm for his mind.

In the state of ignorance, a person knows himself only as a limited ego, and so a natural craving for sense-enjoyment, along with attachment to his own feelings and thoughts, arises. But once ignorance is destroyed, this ego dissolves into its own pure, infinite nature, and the man of steady wisdom remains content in the Self by the Self alone. All desires come to an end, because he himself now abides established in the very form of bliss.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
The Lord anticipates, and answers in advance, an implicit question: activities such as movement need not always be preceded by a deliberate motive, just as a mother's spontaneous acts are not -- this is the point that will later be shown through the illustration of "yā niśā" (2.69). Here the question is: if such a person is fully satiated with supreme bliss, why does he engage in any activity at all? The Lord addresses the concern that, since his prarabdha karma has only slightly veiled his Brahman-nature, some trace of desire-driven activity might still occur for him, and he resolves it: he abandons *nearly* all desires -- the word "prajahāti" is qualified this way because even great sages like Shuka show faint traces of desire (for example, the desire for devotion to the Lord's feet, which true knowers of Reality do seek, as scripture affirms). But when an obstinate craving, like that seen in Indra and similar beings, is observed, that indicates such beings have been overpowered by desire. It is said of such great, specially-commissioned souls (adhikārika-puruṣas) that because of the magnitude of their task, their knowledge may sometimes emerge and sometimes recede, and in this they differ from other beings; hence it should be understood that ordinary people without such a special commission who display such obstinate craving are not true jnanis.

Nor does this verse describe merely someone absorbed in formal meditative absorption (samadhi), since elsewhere (2.57) attachment (sneha) of any kind is expressly prohibited for the man of steady wisdom -- and one absorbed in the objectless (asamprajnata) samadhi does not even experience good or bad fortune, while for one in samadhi-with-object (samprajnata) there is no fixed rule that this state applies to him alone. Scripture likewise confirms that desire and its kin do not arise even for jnanis of unsettled mind, since their impurities have been washed away by knowledge through taking refuge in the Divine.

Since desires reside precisely in the mind, and it is there that opposing knowledge must arise for their removal to be appropriate, the verse specifies "manogatān" (residing in the mind). The apparent contradiction is resolved by verse 2.59, where it is said that even taste (attraction) for sense-objects eventually turns back upon seeing the Supreme; this should not be dismissed merely for lack of common examples, since there is a real difference between ordinary persons and such exceptional souls.

"By the Self" (ātmanā) means by the Supreme Self; being established in that which is called the Self, one who abides in Him experiences contentment solely by His grace -- as declared in the Narayana-Rama tradition: "Having renounced all sense-objects, one established in Rama attains contentment only through the Lord, never otherwise." Hence the "Self" spoken of here does not refer merely to the individual soul (jiva).

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
The word "hi" (indeed) in the introductory line hints at the dispassion that is the very cause of renunciation of action, present from the very beginning -- that is, even during the stage of celibate discipline (brahmacharya). "Knowledge alone is yoga," since it leads one to realize identity with Brahman, and steadfastness in that knowledge is its culmination; alternatively, "action alone is yoga," whereby one renounces the fruits of action and remains established in that discipline. An objection is raised: how can a single sentence teach two distinct meanings? And further, characteristics themselves cannot serve as a means, since for the one who has already attained the goal (kritartha), the characteristic is simply the fruit, not the means -- means and fruit being logically incompatible. This is answered: although for the accomplished jnani the characteristics born of knowledge appear only as the fruit and not as a means, the very same characteristics, for the seeker who still wishes to know, become attainable through effort and thereby function as the means. What is being restated here is a characteristic already established through the power of knowledge -- not something newly enjoined -- since the wise are beyond the scope of injunction or prohibition; for the seeker, however, restating this characteristic serves as an instruction to undertake the practice, so there is a singular focus on adopting that one means. With this understanding, the Lord's own statement is introduced: the characteristics that are attainable through the power of knowledge, without needing separate effort.

This verse answers the very first question -- "What is the definition of a man of steady wisdom?" "Prajahāti" and what follows describe the culmination of abandoning desire, namely freedom from the subtle impressions (vasanas) themselves. Some hold that desires reside in the Self, but this is incorrect, since scripture teaches that desires reside in the mind; hence the verse specifies "manogatān" (residing in the mind). The latter half of the verse -- "ātmany evātmanā" -- answers a further implied objection: does the wise man's activity cease altogether for lack of any motivating desire? No, because the body continues due to residual momentum from prior karma. The verse also distinguishes "unmāda" (madness, loss of discrimination through delusion) from "unmatta" (intoxication, where discrimination is present but disregarded).

The emphatic particle "eva" in "ātmany eva" connects also with "ātmanā," meaning "by his own self alone." This clarifies that his contentment is independent of any external gain -- it is genuine, ultimate (paramartha) satisfaction. "Sthitaprajña" is explained as the state in which all desire obstructing wisdom has vanished, and this state is denoted by the word "tadā" (then). In sum: one who wishes to know the Self, through dispassion undertakes renunciation characterized by giving up all worldly cravings, attains that knowledge through repeated hearing of scripture and so forth, and by exclusive attachment to that knowledge -- turning away from sense-objects -- attains the resultant satisfaction found only in the Self; such a person qualifies to be called "sthitaprajña."

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Sri Bhagavan said -- O son of Pritha, when a seeker thoroughly abandons all the desires residing in the mind, and remains content in himself, by himself alone, then he is said to be a man of steady wisdom (sthitaprajna).

Explanation: It is a recurring pattern in the Gita that whichever discipline (sadhana) -- karma yoga, bhakti yoga, jnana yoga, and so on -- perfects a seeker, that same discipline's terms are used to describe his state of perfection. For instance, in bhakti yoga the seeker worships with such exclusive devotion that nothing but God exists for him (12.6); accordingly, in the perfected state he is free from hatred toward all beings (12.13). In jnana yoga the seeker sees himself as wholly unconnected with and untouched by the gunas (14.19); accordingly, in the perfected state he transcends the gunas altogether (14.22-25). Likewise, since karma yoga centers on the renunciation of desire, this verse shows that in the perfected state such a person renounces all desire completely.

The phrase "prajahāti yadā kāmān sarvān pārtha manogatān" implies that desire belongs neither to the true Self nor, ultimately, to the mind. Desire comes and goes, whereas the Self is ever-abiding; hence desire cannot truly belong to the Self. The mind is merely an instrument, and even there desire does not remain permanently -- it merely arises in the mind (hence "manogatān," "gone into the mind"). Yet because of false identification with body, senses, mind and intellect, a person mistakes the desires that arise in the mind as his own.

The prefix "pra" added to the verb "jahāti" indicates total, unqualified abandonment -- not the slightest trace of any desire remains. One's own real nature is never something to be renounced, nor is something with which one has no relationship at all; renunciation applies only to what is not truly one's own but has mistakenly been claimed as one's own. In the same way, desire never truly belonged to the Self, but was mistakenly claimed as belonging to it -- and it is the renunciation of this false claim that the word "prajahāti" conveys here. Although "kāmān" is already plural, the addition of "sarvān" (all) stresses that not even a fragment of any desire is left.

"Ātmany evātmanā tuṣṭaḥ" -- when all desires are thus renounced, one remains content in oneself, by oneself alone; that is, contentment becomes entirely natural and spontaneous. There are two kinds of contentment: one is a quality or attribute (the absence of any wish in the mind); the other is one's very nature (the total absence of dissatisfaction within the Self). This latter, essential contentment exists always and spontaneously, requiring no cultivation or deliberation -- and it is in this natural contentment that the intellect (buddhi/prajna) becomes spontaneously steady.

"Sthitaprajñas tadocyate" -- even while a person mistakenly identified with countless branching desires, he was, in reality, already sthitaprajna in essence; but because of that false identification his intellect was not steady, so he was not called (or did not experience himself as) sthitaprajna. Now, having relinquished the false claim over all desires, he is called sthitaprajna -- meaning he now experiences the steadiness that was always his true nature. A seeker must labor to make the intellect steady, but once desire is totally abandoned, steadiness becomes effortless and spontaneous.

In karma yoga the practitioner's connection with action persists both during practice and even after perfection, since action itself is said to be the cause for one who wishes to ascend to yoga (6.3); the karma yogi, even after perfection, continues to act within the bounds of dharma as an example for others (3.21), remaining unattached even while acting (4.18). Having earlier (in verse 53) spoken of two aspects of a steady intellect -- unmoved with regard to worldly things, and immovable in its fixity on the Supreme -- the Lord now uses "yadā" and "tadā" to convey these same two aspects: when a seeker becomes wholly free of desire (withdrawal from the world) and remains content in his own true nature (establishment in the Supreme), he is called sthitaprajna. This same two-part pattern -- withdrawal from the world followed by establishment in the Self -- recurs throughout the remaining verses of this chapter, up to its conclusion.

Having thus answered the first question (the definition of sthitaprajna), the Lord goes on, in the next two verses, to answer the second question -- how does a man of steady wisdom speak.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
This verse is understood to directly answer the question about the definition (lakshana) of sthitaprajna, rather than the later verse beginning "sthitadhih" -- the commentary shows this to indicate the point where that answer begins, while also indicating the theme of what immediately follows: that activity such as movement need not necessarily be preceded by deliberate purpose, just as with a mother's spontaneous action (anticipating 2.69). Various fine points are then addressed: the qualifier "prāyaḥ" (mostly, generally) softens the word "sarvān" (all desires), since even liberated sages such as Shuka show slight traces of desire, and true knowers of Reality are said to desire devotion to the Lord's feet -- which is not contrary to the definition, since it is directed toward God rather than the world. Where an obstinate, entrenched craving is observed, as with Indra and similar beings, that indicates such beings are actually overpowered by desire; scripture confirms that certain great, specially-commissioned souls (adhikārika-puruṣas), owing to the magnitude of their task, show alternating emergence and eclipse of their knowledge, thereby differing from ordinary beings -- hence others who show such obstinate craving without that special commission should be understood as not being true jnanis.

This characteristic is not meant to describe merely someone absorbed in formal meditative absorption (samadhi), since verse 2.57 separately prohibits attachment (sneha) for the man of steady wisdom in all circumstances -- not only during samadhi; one absorbed in objectless (asamprajnata) samadhi does not experience good or bad fortune at all, while for one in samadhi-with-object (samprajnata) there is no rule restricting this description to that state alone; hence the verse is not confined to describing a person only while seated in samadhi. Further scriptural support is cited that desire and similar afflictions do not arise even for jnanis of unsettled mind, owing to their purification through resort to the Divine.

The word "manogatān" (residing in the mind) clarifies that desires belong to the mind, not the Self, so that when opposing knowledge arises there, it is fitting for desire to be relinquished specifically at its seat, the mind. An apparent contradiction -- addressed in verse 2.59, where even the lingering relish (rasa) for sense-objects is said to eventually turn back upon vision of the Supreme -- is not to be dismissed for lack of ordinary examples, given the real distinction between special, qualified souls and others. The commentary further critiques rival readings (of other traditions) that take "ātmani ātmanā" to refer merely to the individual soul (jiva) or to the mind, arguing instead that it properly refers to one established in, and content through, the Supreme Self -- contentment born from union with the Divine, not from anything else.

"Sthitaprajña" is explained as the compound describing the state in which all desire obstructing wisdom has ceased, indicated by the word "tadā" (then). This state comes about when a seeker desiring Self-knowledge undertakes renunciation of all worldly cravings through dispassion, attains that knowledge through hearing scripture and reflection, and through exclusive attachment to that knowledge -- turning away from sense-objects -- attains the resultant contentment found only in the Self, thereby earning the designation "sthitaprajña."

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
The Lord answers, in order, the four questions that were asked, continuing through the end of the chapter. "Kāmān" refers to desires, resolves, and other specific modifications of the mind, which other treatises classify into five kinds -- valid cognition, error, imagination, sleep, and memory. "Sarvān" means without any remainder. "Prakarṣeṇa" (thoroughly) means by uprooting the very cause. When one becomes entirely free of all such mental modifications, he is called sthitaprajna -- understood to mean one who is absorbed in samadhi.

The verse specifies "manogatān" to show that desires, being properties of the mind rather than of the Self, are indeed capable of being renounced -- for if they were properties of the Self itself, they could never be given up, just as heat cannot be separated from fire, its natural property; but since they belong to the mind, they can indeed be relinquished along with disidentification from the mind.

An objection is raised: the sthitaprajna is known to have a special radiant contentment, visible for instance in the serenity of his face -- how could this arise from the total abandonment of all desire? The answer: he is content "in the Self" -- meaning in his nature as supreme bliss, not in the trivial non-Self -- "by the Self" -- meaning through his own self-luminous, conscious nature, not through any mental modification -- because he has attained the highest goal of life. This is confirmed by the scriptural verse: "When all the desires lodged in one's heart are relinquished, then the mortal becomes immortal and attains Brahman right here." Thus one absorbed in samadhi, the sthitaprajna, is described through such defining terms -- this is the answer to the first question.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
In this section, the very disciplines that serve as means to knowledge for the seeker are, at the same time, the natural characteristics of the one who has already attained perfection; hence, in describing the characteristics of the accomplished sage, the Lord simultaneously teaches the inner disciplines of knowledge, continuing to the end of the chapter. This verse answers the first question. Sri Bhagavan said: when one thoroughly abandons the desires residing in the mind -- the reason for such renunciation being that he remains content in himself alone, in his own nature as supreme bliss. Being thus content in the Self (atmarama), when he abandons even trivial desires for sense-objects, then by that characteristic the sage is called sthitaprajna.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Thus questioned, Sri Bhagavan Vasudeva, describing the characteristics natural to one liberated while living (jivanmukta) -- characteristics that are attainable through effort by a seeker of liberation -- answers the first question. When one thoroughly abandons all desires, every variety of craving residing in the mind -- here an objection arises: even after abandoning all desire, since the body continues to persist owing to prior (prarabdha) karma, would not such a person's behavior resemble that of a madman, lacking any motivating desire? The answer: he remains content in his own true Self-nature, by himself alone, independent of any external gain, his desire fully satisfied through having attained the nectar of the vision of ultimate Reality, so that nothing else is needed; then he is called sthitaprajna -- one whose wisdom, born of discrimination between Self and non-Self, has become steady, that is, the knower. This state comes about for one who, desiring to know the Self, undertakes through dispassion a renunciation marked by giving up craving for progeny, wealth, and worldly enjoyment, attains that knowledge through hearing scripture and so on, and through exclusive attachment to it -- turning away from sense-objects -- attains the resultant contentment found only in the Self, thereby earning the title sthitaprajna. (This also sets aside the view that "samadhistha" -- one absorbed in samadhi -- should be supplied as an unstated qualifier, since supplying such an unstated term serves no purpose once this reasoning is understood.) It is by this characteristic relationship that he is called sthitaprajna -- just as Arjuna, by his relation to Pritha, is addressed here as "Partha."

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having been asked, the Lord states the definition of the sthitaprajna. "O Partha" -- addressing Arjuna as one fit to hear His words, being the son of Pritha, His own devotee, and thus qualified to receive His teaching. "Yadā manogatān" -- when the desires residing in his own mind (as opposed to those attained through the Lord's own will and grace, i.e., devotional longings granted by God) -- "sarvān kāmān prajahāti" -- he completely abandons all such desires, "completely" meaning so thoroughly that not even the memory of them remains.

An objection is raised: what is the result of abandoning desire? The answer: "ātmany eva" -- he becomes content in his own true nature as the individual self (jivatma), through the very realization of that identity. The point is this: ordinary desires bring a kind of worldly satisfaction pursued through effort at fulfilling them; but upon relinquishing such desires, contentment arises spontaneously from the Self itself, becoming a satisfaction that transcends the worldly and is directed toward God as its fruit. When a person attains such a state, he is called "sthitaprajña" -- one of unwavering wisdom.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
The Lord answers these questions in order, beginning with "prajahāti" and so on. Here, the very characteristics of the accomplished sage are taught as the means to knowledge for those who have not yet attained it, since these traits, though requiring effort for the unaccomplished, are entirely natural and spontaneous for the accomplished -- as it is said: "For one in whom Self-awakening has arisen, qualities such as freedom from hatred arise effortlessly, and are not means to be cultivated like those of a practicing seeker."

When this yogi thoroughly and completely -- down to the root -- abandons all desired objects of enjoyment pertaining to the gross, subtle, and causal bodies, what kind of desires are these? Those "residing in the mind," that is, existing within the mind in the form of resolve and doubt (samkalpa-vikalpa), not existing externally -- as the logician Akshapada states: "Sense-objects such as form, which are the basis of attachment and aversion, are in fact constructed by mental resolve."

Among these, the renunciation of gross desires occurs merely through solitary practice and similar disciplines -- this is the coarsest level. For one whose senses have withdrawn and who is absorbed within, the desires that surface in the dream state from waking impressions are relinquished through the strength of cultivating good impressions, such as meditation on the Lord. And for one whose senses are fully withdrawn, during the state of samadhi-with-object (samprajnata), even the subtle divine desires that arise merely through mental resolve -- well known in contexts such as the meditation on the small space within the heart (dahara-vidya) -- are relinquished through the strength of practicing objectless (asamprajnata) samadhi.

Having thus abandoned all three kinds of desire, when he becomes content in the Self alone -- in the undivided, single essence -- by himself, through the bliss of his own true nature, independent of all external objects, then he is called sthitaprajna.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: O Pārtha, when a man gives up all varieties of desire for sense gratification, which arise from mental concoction, and when his mind, thus purified, finds satisfaction in the self alone, then he is said to be in pure transcendental consciousness.

 The Bhāgavatam affirms that any person who is fully in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, or devotional service of the Lord, has all the good qualities of the great sages, whereas a person who is not so transcendentally situated has no good qualifications, because he is sure to be taking refuge in his own mental concoctions. Consequently, it is rightly said herein that one has to give up all kinds of sense desire manufactured by mental concoction. Artificially, such sense desires cannot be stopped. But if one is engaged in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, then, automatically, sense desires subside without extraneous efforts. Therefore, one has to engage himself in Kṛṣṇa consciousness without hesitation, for this devotional service will instantly help one onto the platform of transcendental consciousness. The highly developed soul always remains satisfied in himself by realizing himself as the eternal servitor of the Supreme Lord. Such a transcendentally situated person has no sense desires resulting from petty materialism; rather, he remains always happy in his natural position of eternally serving the Supreme Lord.
