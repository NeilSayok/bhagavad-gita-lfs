Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Every individual is, in his real Self, ever complete and perfect. Because of ignorance of this fullness, the intellect gives rise to countless desires for happiness -- we desire only what we feel we lack. Corresponding to each desire, thought-modifications (vikshepa) arise in the mind, and these thoughts express themselves at the level of the body as outward action. Thus we are bound in a chain: ignorance, desire, thought, and action. Looked at more deeply, these are not really separate things but different expressions of the one root ignorance of the Self, appearing at the levels of intellect, mind, and body as desire, thought, and action respectively. If the Real is defined as the experience beyond this ignorance, then the state free of desire, thought, and action is itself our true nature -- this actionlessness is called naishkarmya, and it really means fullness or perfection. Hence the Lord says that mere renunciation of actions does not by itself bring naishkarmya or perfection: running away from life's struggles is not the path to the highest goal of human development. Arjuna's wish was precisely to flee the battlefield, and that is why he needed re-education in the true wisdom of the Vedic culture -- which is the very purpose of Krishna's teaching in the Gita. In brief, the path indicated here is purification of the mind through Karma Yoga, followed by Self-realisation through Jnana Yoga; that is why this verse is so often quoted by writers on Hindu thought. Why mere renunciation of action, without knowledge, cannot give naishkarmya or perfection is explained in what follows.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
The Lord gives Arjuna a further reason he must act. A man does not attain naishkarmya -- freedom from action, i.e. liberation -- merely by not undertaking actions such as fighting the war, by giving up desire-prompted (kamya) acts; knowledge alone is the means to liberation, not mere non-performance of action, because the individual soul (purusha) is always joined to a body, gross or subtle. If liberation could come simply from not acting, even immobile beings like plants would be liberated. Nor does non-action today remove karma, since countless karmas accumulated from beginningless past births remain to be experienced, and one body cannot exhaust the fruits of all of them. Freedom from bondage comes only through acting without desire (nishkama), not through the mere absence of action. One might object that desireless action, having no fruit, is itself equivalent to non-action and so grants liberation on its own -- but the answer is that 'renunciation' here means giving up desire-driven acts, not action itself: through desireless action the mind is purified, knowledge arises, and liberation follows -- not from the mere abandonment of action. The renunciate order of life is meant for striving toward God and for pleasing Him, since those in other stages of life are mostly occupied with worldly duties; only the pure and truly effortful attain knowledge, and those established in that order, or other qualified aspirants who strive earnestly, please the Lord greatly.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
This is a sub-commentary glossing Shankara's bhashya phrase by phrase. It clarifies that steadfastness in knowledge (jnana-nishtha) is properly for renunciates, and that steadfastness in action (karma-nishtha) is not by itself an independent means to liberation -- it is a means toward jnana-nishtha, which alone directly yields liberation. It explains 'anarambha' (non-commencement) and distinguishes true naishkarmya -- the state reached through the yoga of knowledge, in which one abides in one's actionless Self -- from mere physical inactivity. It shows that obligatory and occasional rites (yajnas etc.) purify the mind by destroying accumulated sin, and thereby indirectly give rise to knowledge, citing both shruti and smriti in support. It establishes, from Upanishadic and Gita passages alike (including verses yet to come, such as 5.6, 5.11, and 18.5), that Karma Yoga is the means (upaya) to the end (upeya) of Jnana Yoga. It then answers the objection that scriptures elsewhere show renunciation of duty alone -- 'having given fearlessness to all beings, one should practise actionlessness' -- and common usage both suggest that simply not acting brings naishkarmya, asking why then undertake action at all; the reply is that mere physical abstention without knowledge is not true naishkarmya, and Karma Yoga remains the necessary means.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
In Karma Yoga, performing action is essential -- perfection comes only from acting without desire for results, and this cannot be attained without acting at all. There is a natural inner impulse (vega) to act; it is pacified not by abandoning duty but by performing one's duty while giving up desire, since acting with desire only strengthens this urge rather than removing it. 'Attaining naishkarmya' through Karma Yoga means that, while continuing to act, one's actions cease to bind, becoming as if non-actions -- this state is called naishkarmya. Desireless action loses its capacity to bear fruit, just as a roasted or boiled seed loses its power to germinate; such a person's actions no longer drive the cycle of birth and death. Giving up desire is possible only when all actions are performed for others' sake and not one's own, since action belongs to this world while one's true Self is related to God alone, not to action. In the verse's second half, Krishna addresses the Sankhya Yogi: merely giving up the outward form of action does not bring siddhi -- what matters is giving up the sense of doership (ahamta), not literal abandonment of action, whereas the Karma Yogi must actually act to reach perfection (cf. 6.3). The deeper point: the Gita teaches the art of reaching the highest goal through worldly action, not through escaping it -- hence in both Karma Yoga and Jnana Yoga the Lord commands action, as with 'yogastha kuru karmani' (2.48) and 'tasmad yudhyasva bharata' (2.18), showing His intent is to purify action of desire, attachment, and craving, not to abolish it. Rather than outwardly renouncing action, the seeker should sever the sense of ownership of it: the Karma Yogi acts selflessly, treating body, senses, mind, and intellect as belonging to the world and dedicated to its service (5.11); the Jnana Yogi, through discrimination, understands it is only the gunas acting upon the gunas, with the Self wholly unconnected to action (3.28, 5.8-9). A seeker's aversion to action, once the longing for liberation awakens, is really a subtle desire for comfort rooted in attachment to the body, which actually hinders progress; what is needed is not literal renunciation of action, objects, or people, but renunciation of attachment to them. In Sankhya Yoga this requires intense dispassion, but in Karma Yoga, acting purely for others makes releasing attachment easier even without such intense dispassion. True solitude means severing the sense of 'I and mine,' even regarding the body -- not mere physical withdrawal.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
This is Jayatirtha's detailed sub-commentary defending Madhva's reading against objections. It considers whether liberation is caused by action itself or only by knowledge with action merely as an obstacle to be removed; it argues that if mere cessation of action gave naishkarmya, then immobile beings like plants -- perpetually inactive -- would already be liberated, which is absurd, so 'purusha' (the embodied self) must always be understood as bound to action across countless past lives whose karmas cannot be exhausted in one lifetime. It explains that freedom from bondage comes through absence of desire (akama), not absence of action, and that texts prescribing renunciation (sannyasa) as a path to naishkarmya refer to giving up desire-driven action -- with a purified mind arising from desireless action -- not literal cessation of all activity, refuting Shankara's alternate view along the way. It further discusses the renunciate order (yati-ashrama) as prescribed in shruti and smriti for cultivating effort and devotion toward the Lord, explains why that order is especially pleasing to Him, and affirms that other qualified aspirants who strive earnestly in their own stations likewise attain that same pleasure of the Lord, so mere abandonment of duty without knowledge and purification cannot itself be the cause of siddhi.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
It has already been established, by the principle that each of action, knowledge, and devotion has its own distinct role in purifying the mind, that if this were not so there would have been no need to enquire into the three paths separately; as stated in the Aptabhashya, perfection is attained through yoga (action), knowledge, and devotion respectively, each a means to the human goal. Confirming the Yoga view here, the Lord teaches that liberation is not attained merely by undertaking action. Yet for those qualified for action, such as Arjuna, perfection is likewise not attained by giving up action -- whether through outright renunciation or through Sankhya, the path of knowledge alone -- since even the liberated, whether liberated-while-living or endowed with the qualities of Brahman, are seen to still perform action so long as they possess a body.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Since an effect cannot arise without its cause, those who -- as the shruti says, 'Brahmins seek to know [the Self] through study of scripture, sacrifice, charity, and austerity combined with fasting' -- are meant to attain Self-knowledge do not attain naishkarmya (the state of being wholly free of action, i.e. steadfastness in knowledge) if they fail to undertake these disciplines, because without them the mind remains impure and unfit for knowledge. One might object, citing 'those who desire the world of the wandering renunciate depart [from household life],' that steadfastness in knowledge is reached simply through total renunciation, making these disciplines needless. The Lord's answer: nor does one attain siddhi -- the fruit of true steadfastness in knowledge -- merely through renunciation undertaken without purification of mind, for renunciation itself is not truly possible without the purity born of desireless action; a renunciation adopted out of mere impulse, without that purification, does not bear its fruit.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
To properly purify the mind so that knowledge may arise, one must perform the duties proper to one's station in life (varna and ashrama); without that purification, knowledge cannot arise -- hence the Lord says that by not undertaking action, one does not attain naishkarmya, i.e. knowledge. One might object, citing the shruti 'those who desire the world of the wandering renunciate depart,' that renunciation itself is the direct cause of liberation and so ask what need there is for action. The Lord replies: nor, without purification of mind, does one attain siddhi, i.e. liberation, merely through renunciation devoid of knowledge.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Seeing Arjuna dejected and asking, 'then why do you engage me in this terrible action, Keshava?', and thinking that action ought not to be performed at all, the Lord speaks this verse to correct him. Steadfastness in action -- as the citation 'knowledge arises in men from the destruction of sin through action, just as one sees one's own reflection in a polished mirror' shows -- purifies the mind and thereby becomes a means to the rise of knowledge, while steadfastness in knowledge, once reached through that means, becomes independently the direct cause of liberation. Thus, without undertaking the actions enjoined as means to knowledge through purification of mind, a person does not attain naishkarmya -- steadfastness in the yoga of knowledge -- but attains it precisely by undertaking them. To the objection, based on 'having given fearlessness to all beings, one should practise naishkarmya,' that naishkarmya can be reached by simply renouncing one's duties, so why act at all -- the Lord replies that nor does one attain siddhi, the state marked by naishkarmya, through mere renunciation of action alone, devoid of knowledge.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
An objection is raised: since the very desire for liberation impels a person even toward Karma Yoga, why, once that desire has arisen, should one not proceed directly to Jnana Yoga without the intermediate step? This verse answers that doubt. A person whose senses are agitated by worldly objects, embedded in ordinary life, cannot suddenly -- that is, without practising Karma Yoga -- attain naishkarmya, understood here as steadfastness in knowledge reached only through the yoga of knowledge whose ground is scripturally enjoined action, not mere non-performance (which would apply equally to everyone and so cannot be the intended sense). Likewise 'renunciation' here refers to giving up already-undertaken scriptural action -- recalling Arjuna's own wish to abandon the battle -- so: nor does one attain, merely by giving up already-begun action, the fulfilment meant, i.e. steadfastness in knowledge; for that fulfilment arises only from actions performed without craving for their fruits and offered in worship of the Supreme Person. Without devotion to Govinda through such desireless action, the beginningless accumulation of sin is not removed, the impurities of rajas and tamas persist, attachment and aversion remain strong, the senses stay agitated by outward objects, and one cannot be established in the inner Self -- so for one lacking this purification to attempt jumping straight to steadfastness in knowledge by merely abandoning action is as absurd as trying to reach the topmost, seventh tier of a temple tower while skipping all the steps below.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
If this is so, Arjuna may ask, with what intent was I commanded to perform action? The Lord replies: by not undertaking action, a person does not attain naishkarmya -- here meaning the state of devotion free from the sense of being the doer. The point is that without true knowledge of the nature of action, mere renunciation accomplishes no real human goal; action is therefore enjoined so that one may come to know what ought to be given up -- hence 'undertaking' is spoken of here, not doership itself. He adds that renunciation without knowledge of one's true nature likewise achieves nothing: nor does one, through mere renunciation born of ignorance of one's true nature and consisting only of abandoning action, attain siddhi -- the fruit of true renunciation.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
This verse sets out the relationship between action and knowledge as that of a subordinate part to the whole. By not undertaking sacrifices and other prescribed actions, a person does not attain naishkarmya, understood here as steadfastness in knowledge, since the shruti 'Brahmins seek to know [the Self] through sacrifice' enjoins such actions as auxiliary to the pursuit of knowledge. Neelkantha considers and rejects the view that naishkarmya here means literal renunciation (pravrajya) rather than knowledge itself, noting that in a parallel Gita passage naishkarmya-siddhi is called 'supreme,' which would not fit mere wandering-forth; he clarifies that in 'seek to know through sacrifice,' the instrumental connects with 'seek to know,' not with naishkarmya directly -- sacrifice aids the seeking, it does not constitute the goal. He acknowledges scriptural support for formal renunciation even among the already learned, undertaken for the ripening of knowledge or the bliss of liberation-while-living, and explains that 'knowledge does not arise for the unrenounced' means giving up distracting activity, not literally donning ochre robes -- citing sages like Gargi, the hunter Vyadha, and Vasishtha who attained knowledge without such outward renunciation. In sum, for a person of unpurified mind, dull intellect, and gripped by attachment and aversion, naishkarmya (knowledge) cannot arise through discrimination of Self and non-Self, nor through mental control -- this is the meaning of the first half. As for the objection that 'having given fearlessness to all beings, one should practise naishkarmya' implies mere renunciation grants it, Neelkantha answers that without purification of mind through action, mere renunciation does not lead to liberation; the smriti passage cited presupposes renunciation preceded by that purification, since one still gripped by attachment and aversion cannot truly extend fearlessness to all beings. Hence it is rightly said: nor does one attain siddhi merely by renunciation alone.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Not by merely abstaining from work can one achieve freedom from reaction, nor by renunciation alone can one attain perfection.

 The renounced order of life can be accepted when one has been purified by the discharge of the prescribed form of duties which are laid down just to purify the hearts of materialistic men. Without purification, one cannot attain success by abruptly adopting the fourth order of life ( sannyāsa ). According to the empirical philosophers, simply by adopting sannyāsa, or retiring from fruitive activities, one at once becomes as good as Nārāyaṇa. But Lord Kṛṣṇa does not approve this principle. Without purification of heart, sannyāsa is simply a disturbance to the social order. On the other hand, if someone takes to the transcendental service of the Lord, even without discharging his prescribed duties, whatever he may be able to advance in the cause is accepted by the Lord ( buddhi-yoga ). Sv-alpam apy asya dharmasya trāyate mahato bhayāt. Even a slight performance of such a principle enables one to overcome great difficulties.
