Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Arjuna said -- O Achyuta! Station my chariot between the two armies, until I have seen these men standing here eager for battle, so that I may know with whom I must fight in this military endeavor.

Explanation: 'Achyuta senayorubhayormadhye ratham sthapaya' -- the two armies stood facing each other for battle at such a distance that one army could strike the other with arrows and the like. The 'middle' of the two armies has two senses: (1) the midpoint of the width across which the armies stood, and (2) the midpoint between the two armies, equidistant from where the Kaurava army stood and where the Pandava army stood. Arjuna asks the Lord to station the chariot at such a midpoint so that both armies could easily be seen.

The phrase 'senayorubhayormadhye' (between the two armies) occurs three times in the Gita -- here (1.21), again in verse 24 of this chapter, and in verse 10 of chapter 2. The threefold repetition conveys: first, Arjuna valiantly commands his chariot stationed between the two armies (1.21); then the Lord, having stationed the chariot between the two armies, tells him to behold the assembled Kurus (1.24); and finally, it is between the two armies that the Lord delivers the Gita's teaching to the grief-stricken Arjuna (2.10). Thus, Arjuna was first valiant; then, seeing his kinsmen, delusion overcame him and he withdrew from battle; and finally he received the Lord's great teaching, which dispelled his delusion. The implication is: wherever and in whatever circumstance a person finds himself, remaining right there, he can put that circumstance to good use and become free of desire, and it is right there that he can attain the Supreme -- for the Supreme is ever present, uniformly, in all circumstances.

'Yavadetan nirikshe'ham...ranasamudyame' -- for how long should the chariot remain stationed between the armies? Arjuna answers: until I have seen all the kings, with their armies, who have come to the Kaurava side desiring battle -- keep the chariot standing there that long. With whom must I fight in this military endeavor? Who among them is my equal in strength, who weaker, who stronger -- let me look at them all a little. Here, by the word 'yoddhukaman' (desiring to fight), Arjuna is saying: we ourselves had thought of peace, but they did not accept the peace proposal, since their minds are set more strongly on war. So let me see with how much strength they intend to fight.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Having shown that fear had come upon Dhritarashtra's sons, he now cites the opposite condition of the Pandavas, in 'Atha...'. Even though the moment of fear had arrived and flight was possible, seeing the enemy standing firm in readiness for battle -- quite the reverse -- at the very moment weapons were about to be discharged, Arjuna, son of Pandu, graced by having the great hero Hanuman on his banner, utterly free of fear, raised his Gandiva bow and spoke this forthcoming statement to Hrishikesha, Sri Krishna, who as impeller of the senses knows the workings of every inner faculty. He did not act rashly on his own without deliberation -- unlike the enemy, whose lack of deliberation is contrasted with skill in policy and dharma; by implication, the address 'lord of the earth' conveys that 'you seized the kingdom rashly, without deliberation, and hence have no policy or dharma, and thus no victory awaits you.' He then introduces Arjuna's actual words: 'Station my chariot between the two armies' -- 'the two armies' meaning one's own side and the opposing side, both present -- 'station,' i.e., make it firm. Thus Arjuna directs the Lord of all as though commanding Him; for what is impossible for devotees, when even the Lord Himself carries out their commands? Hence the Pandavas' victory is certain. Anticipating the Lord's possible objection -- 'while I station the chariot thus, will not these enemies dislodge me and the chariot?' -- Arjuna says 'Achyuta' (the infallible one): who indeed could dislodge You, who are infallible in place, time, and substance? By this, any anger arising from being commanded is also forestalled, since You are ever unchanging.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
That He spoke, prompted by the very impeller of speech (the Lord), is conveyed by the word 'Hrishikesha' -- some say Hrishikesha means: as impeller of the senses, He knows the workings of every inner faculty. Thus, hinting that the kingdom you (Dhritarashtra), being weak and lacking wisdom, seized by deceit, will in fact belong to the exceedingly valiant, wise Pandavas, favored by the Lord and by Hanuman, and that you should abandon any false hope of retaining it, Sanjaya addresses him with 'lord of the earth.' He then cites the actual statement, in 'Senayoh...': 'Station my chariot between the two armies.' Anticipating the objection 'but will they not drive away the chariot you have stationed there?', he says 'Achyuta' -- the sense being: You are capable of making even the chariot immovable. Thus, what wonder is there in the victory of one for whose charioteer even the Supreme Lord stands, functioning just like an ordinary charioteer? It is fitting that the Lord submits Himself to His devotee, and no anger is warranted here either, since the Lord's true nature never undergoes diminishment -- this, some say, is the intent of the address 'Achyuta.' Anticipating the Lord's own possible concern -- 'while I station the chariot thus, will not these enemies dislodge me and the chariot?' -- Arjuna says 'Achyuta': who indeed could dislodge You, infallible in place, time, and substance? -- so say others.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Venkatanatha explains the passage from 'atha vyavasthitan' up to 'Kurun' (1.25): the whole is a single sentence resolved in three parts. The first sentence explains the sense of 'priyachikirshavah' (those wishing to please). Regarding 'vyavasthitan' -- the word 'vi' signals a special nuance -- namely 'eager for battle,' as is likewise stated shortly after, 'standing ready, wishing to fight.' Regarding 'kapidhvajah' -- to preclude any diminishment implied by merely calling him 'monkey-bannered,' the epithet recalls the boon Hanuman granted at the lotus expedition; and to convey that the mere sight of Hanuman would strike terror into the enemy just as into demons, he is elsewhere called 'the monkey-bannered one whose banner burned Lanka.' The word 'Achyuta' (infallible), establishing the Lord's unfailing nature, is explained through the terms 'jnana' etc.; the term 'Hrishikesha' is explained via 'para' and 'apara' (transcendent and immanent) -- or, the creation and so forth, characterized by His powers, is itself the very meaning of the word 'Hrishikesha.' As stated in the Ahirbudhnya Samhita: 'Being the Lord, He delights manifestly through the sport of creation; His being Hrishikesha, His lordship, and His divinity are thereby made clear. Being endowed, through His own unchanging nature, with the form of vigor called the senses (hrishika), the Lord, ever through independence, engages eternally in the act of creation and the rest; this state of possessing lordship and vigor is called 'Hrishikeshatva.'' As for the word 'Achyuta' meaning 'one who does not let go of those who take refuge in Him, and hence has no falling away' -- he shows this etymology through the phrase 'out of affection for His devotees,' etc. 'Seated as My charioteer' -- the sense being: for one who, as Hrishikesha, governs all the senses in every way, what great matter is it to govern the mere reins of a chariot? Regarding 'nirikshe' (let me look upon) -- the prefix conveys the sense of 'thoroughly.' The word 'yavat' (as long as) here conveys either completeness, or the time-limit of the looking, or, per grammar, the future tense marker in the sense of continuous looking. The verse says 'those with whom I must fight, let me look upon them' -- not simply 'let me observe those with whom I must fight' -- hence the rise of the following verse 'yotsyamanan' (those about to fight). Also, since 'dhartarashtrasya durbuddheh' (of the ill-counseled son of Dhritarashtra) is meant to expose Duryodhana's specific fault, there is no redundancy. Alternatively: since 'between the two armies' has already been mentioned, and since the coming verse says 'he saw them standing in both armies,' this earlier verse concerns those of one's own side and their allies standing in one's own army -- meaning, with whom, standing alongside, must I fight the enemy; the next verse concerns the Kaurava allies standing in the opposing army -- this distinction is clear. Even though he already knew all this beforehand...

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Thus, having described the battle-enthusiasm arisen in both armies for the meeting of Krishna and Arjuna, he now tells, in four verses beginning with 'Atha...', the reasoned prompting Krishna gave to Arjuna -- who, having entered the midst of battle at Krishna's direction, was about to feel detachment at the sight of his kinsmen's destruction. First he shows the occasion for that prompting. 'Atha' (now/then) -- seeing, in reverse order, without any fear, Dhritarashtra's people standing arrayed -- their position clearly discerned -- monkey-bannered Arjuna (the epithet 'monkey-bannered' conveys the lightness of his weapon-handling), as the discharge of weapons began, raised his bow -- Pandu's son, desiring to regain his own kingdom, spoke this forthcoming statement to Hrishikesha, the impeller of the senses, at that very moment. The address 'lord of the earth' is meant to convey that kings, too, should act according to dharma. He then gives Arjuna's actual words, beginning with 'senayoh...': 'O Achyuta, station my chariot between the two armies.'

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Hrishikesha -- being the impeller of all the senses, He knows the minds of others. It is stated that Arjuna spoke only a statement, not an object of speech in the ordinary sense -- the word 'vakyam' (statement) itself signals this. He states only the actual statement, in 'Senayoh...'.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Arjuna said: O infallible one, please draw my chariot between the two armies so that I may see who is present here, who is desirous of fighting, and with whom I must contend in this great battle.

 Although Lord Kṛṣṇa is the Supreme Personality of Godhead, out of His causeless mercy He was engaged in the service of His friend. He never fails in His affection for His devotees, and thus He is addressed herein as infallible. As charioteer, He had to carry out the orders of Arjuna, and since He did not hesitate to do so, He is addressed as infallible. Although He had accepted the position of a charioteer for His devotee, His supreme position was not challenged. In all circumstances, He is the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Hṛṣīkeśa, the Lord of the total senses. The relationship between the Lord and His servitor is very sweet and transcendental. The servitor is always ready to render service to the Lord, and, similarly, the Lord is always seeking an opportunity to render some service to the devotee. He takes greater pleasure in His pure devotee’s assuming the advantageous position of ordering Him than He does in being the giver of orders. Since He is master, everyone is under His orders, and no one is above Him to order Him. But when He finds that a pure devotee is ordering Him, He obtains transcendental pleasure, although He is the infallible master in all circumstances. As a pure devotee of the Lord, Arjuna had no desire to fight with his cousins and brothers, but he was forced to come onto the battlefield by the obstinacy of Duryodhana, who was never agreeable to any peaceful negotiation. Therefore, he was very anxious to see who the leading persons present on the battlefield were. Although there was no question of a peacemaking endeavor on the battlefield, he wanted to see them again, and to see how much they were bent upon demanding an unwanted war.
