Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The completeness of the scientific study of any object or phenomenon lies only in its intellectual analysis and practical realization. When physical science reveals that the atom is the unit of matter, then along with this knowledge, one must also understand that these atoms, combining in various numbers and ways, create this world of objects with countless forms and properties. Similarly, knowing that a single Self-element (Atma-tattva) is the truth behind all names and forms is only partial knowledge. The completeness of knowledge lies in knowing how this creation of diversity has manifested from this one Self. Just as a person who knows the ocean sees the existence of countless and diverse waves in that one ocean, similarly, the self-realized soul sees the separate existence of all beings as abiding in the one Supreme Lord. All waves are but an expansion of the ocean. The wise man also experiences that this creation has expanded from the one Self alone. In these sacred moments of self-realization, the wise man, becoming Brahman Himself, experiences that the same Self-element pervades and embraces all within and without, sustaining everyone. It is present not only in the deep, grave, and limitless-infinite, but it also pervades all superficial names and forms. The Self is experienced only by becoming the Self. One who has fully realized that the Self residing in one's own heart is the Self (Brahman) residing everywhere in all beings, and how the radiant nature of Truth becomes covered by various names and forms due to the veil of ignorance (Avidya), that person alone is called a knower of truth (Tattvajna) and a seer of truth (Samyakdarshi). In that experience, he attains identity with Brahman, which is beyond all limitations (Upadhis). Since the same Self resides in all bodies, it might be suspected that the defects of those limitations also affect the Self. To remove this doubt, the Lord says:

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
If the Self, being all-pervading, is the Self of all, then does it not become associated with the body and consequently possess qualities like agency (kartritva), etc.? Indeed, it is seen that even pure substances like panchagavya get defiled by contact with impure things. Raising this doubt, the commentator introduces the next verse starting with 'ekasya...' (referring to the next verse) as the answer. He establishes its beginningless nature (anaditva) by saying 'adi...' (beginningless). Even so, what follows? He states that because it is not an effect, there is no destruction (vyaya) caused by it, by saying 'yad hi...' (for that which is...). Still, could there be a decrease (vyaya) through the diminution of qualities? He says 'no' by stating 'tatha...' (likewise). Since the Self is partless, there is no destruction through parts, and since it is without attributes (nirguna), there is no destruction through qualities. But could there be a natural decay? To this he says 'paramatma...' (Supreme Self). Pointing out the result of the absence of decay in the Supreme Self either from itself or from another, he says 'yatah...' (since...). How can that which is established in its own glory reside in the body? To this he says 'sarireshu...' (in bodies). Although residing in the body, etc., due to its all-pervading nature and being the Self of all, it does not act on its own or through the nature of the body, etc., because it is immutable (kutastha) and the body, etc., are imagined (kalpita). Lest it be doubted that even in the absence of agency, there could be experience of pleasure and pain (bhoktritva), he says 'tad-akaranat...' (due to its not acting). He explains this by saying 'yo hi...' (for he who...). If the Supreme Self lacks agency, etc., whose then are these? He asks 'kah punah...' (who then is this?). He states the opponent's view that agency, etc., belong to some other individual soul (jiva) separate from the Supreme, by saying 'yadi...' (if...). He refutes this by saying that in that case, the consistency of the beginning (prakrama-bhanga) would be violated, by saying 'tatah...' (then...). He doubts that since a jiva distinct from Ishvara is not accepted, there is no contradiction with the beginning, by saying 'atha...' (if it be argued...). Then the substrate of the perceived agency, etc., must be stated, says the opponent, by asking 'kah...' (who?). Lest it be argued that there is no need to state this since the Supreme Self itself is the substrate of agency, etc., he says 'paro va...' (or the Supreme...). The connection is with the previous statement 'no, it cannot be said'. Indeed, if the Supreme Self were the seat of agency, etc., it would not be Ishvara, but would be like us, is the idea. If agency, etc., are not different in the Supreme Self or another, the scriptural texts like 'although residing in the body...' would be impossible to understand or explain, and should therefore be abandoned, concludes the examiner by saying 'sarvatha...' (in every way). He resolves this by saying 'tatra...' (therein...) that the doctrine of the Lord is acceptable because agency and experiencing, which do not belong to the Supreme Reality, are superimposed upon it through ignorance (Avidya). He elaborates on this resolution by saying 'avidya...' (ignorance). If worldly agency, etc., are accepted, why is the absolute reality of it not accepted? To this he says 'na tu...' (but not...). He adduces proof for the absence of real agency, etc., by saying 'atah...' (hence...).

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
When the seeker sees the separate existence of all beings centered in the one Prakriti (Nature) alone and sees the expansion of all of them from that Prakriti alone, at that time he attains Brahman.

Commentary -- [Prakriti has two aspects -- action (kriya) and matter (padartha). To sever connection with action, the twenty-ninth verse (actually 13.29, but referred to here) was told; now to sever connection with matter, this thirtieth verse (actually 13.30 in some counts, here 13.31) is being stated.] 'Yada bhutaprthagbhavam... brahma sampadyate tada' -- When the seeker sees the diverse states of all living beings, that is, whatever viviparous (jarayuja), oviparous (andaja), soil-born (udbhijja), and sweat-born (svedaja) creatures are born in the three worlds, and sees their gross, subtle, and causal bodies as resting in the one Prakriti alone, at that time he attains Brahman. The bodies, names, forms, structures, mental tendencies, qualities, modifications, origin, sustenance, and dissolution of all moving and non-moving beings of the three worlds are all born of the one Prakriti alone. The bodies of all beings are produced from Prakriti, exist in Prakriti, and dissolve in Prakriti. He who sees in this manner attains Brahman, that is, he realizes the self-evident Supreme Reality, his own true nature, which is beyond Prakriti. In reality, he was already established in it, but only by assuming his relationship with the products of Prakriti did he fail to experience his true self. But when he sees everything as resting in Prakriti and rising from Prakriti, then he realizes his self-evident nature. Just as all moving and non-moving bodies born of the earth, and whatever changes, transformations (Footnote p. 705.1), and actions (Footnote p. 705.2) take place in those bodies, all occur on the earth itself. Similarly, all qualities and modifications born of Prakriti, and whatever changes or fluctuations take place in them, occur entirely within Prakriti. The point is that just as objects born of the earth are of the nature of earth by resting in the earth and merging in the earth, similarly, the entire world born of Prakriti is of the nature of Prakriti by resting in Prakriti and merging in Prakriti. In the same way, the conscious principle in the form of moving and non-moving beings remains constantly established in the Supreme Self. However many modifications may seem to appear in it due to association with Prakriti, it always remains unattached (asanga). When this clear experience arises, the seeker attains Brahman. It is a rule that by assuming relationship with Prakriti, one looks at beings with a sense of selfishness, enjoyment, pleasure, etc., in separate ways, which gives rise to attachment and aversion. When there is attachment, one sees merits in them, and when there is aversion, one sees defects. Thus, because the veil of attachment and aversion comes before the vision, reality is not experienced. But when the seeker sees the origin, existence, and destruction of the bodies of all beings, including his own gross, subtle, and causal bodies, as taking place in Prakriti alone, and sees their absence in his own self, then the veil of attachment and aversion is removed from before his vision and he realizes the self-evident Supreme Self. Relationship -- He who was described as beyond the body in the twenty-second verse, and whose attainment of Brahman was described in the previous (thirtieth) verse, the real nature of that Purusha (conscious entity) is described in the next verse.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Keeping in mind that the explanation of 'ekastham' as 'being of the nature of one' (or one with the Self) is contradicted by all sources of knowledge, the commentator says 'ekastham' (resting in one). And keeping in mind that the explanation of 'tatah eva ca vistaram' as 'the expansion of the universe from one's own self' is incorrect, he says 'tatah eva' (from that alone). 'Vistaram' means origination (utpatti).

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
The commentator shows the fruit in the third case as well, with the verse beginning with 'yada'. When there are beings like demigods, etc., composed of the principles of Prakriti and Purusha, their separate existence (prithag-bhavam) or diversity is perceived in the form of divine nature, human nature, tallness, shortness, stoutness, thinness, etc. He sees this dual nature as situated in one (ekastham), i.e., abiding in Prakriti and in the collective person (samashti-purusha). And he directly realizes the emergence and expansion from That alone, as heard in the scriptures: 'Just as tiny sparks fly out from a fire... so do all these beings come forth from this Self' (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.1.20). At that time, he attains the Imperishable Brahman (Aksara Brahman) and achieves oneness with the unlimited Aksara Self. This is the meaning: The individual (vyashti) is like a small stream, and the collective (samashti) is like the waters of the Ganges. Aksara is like the power, and the Supreme Lord Hari is like the presiding deity. Oneness of the soul with Aksara is declared by the Sruti. However, oneness and entry into Purushottama (the Supreme Person) is by His own divine will. In this regard, the views of the illusionists (mayavadins), who teach that difference is imagined due to ignorance by accepting the subject-object relationship (drig-drishya-vyavahara), should be disregarded. For the followers of the Vedas, the conduct is strictly scriptural, not based on logical speculation, as said by the wise. The details should be understood from the treatises (Nibandha).

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Thus, having provisionally permitted the perception of differences in the Kshetra (field), the perception of differences in the Kshetrajna (knower of the field) was refuted. Now, the commentator refutes even the perception of differences in the Kshetra by showing it to be illusory (mayika) through the verse beginning with 'yada'. When, at which time, following the instructions of the scriptures and the teacher, one directly realizes that the separate existence (prithag-bhavam) or diversity of all moving and non-moving beings, comprising the entire insentient world, is situated in one (ekastham), that is, imagined in the one Self alone which is of the nature of Existence. Since the imagined is not different from its substratum (adhisthana), it is non-different from the Self which is of the nature of Existence, and one realizes 'All this is indeed the Self'. Even so, by the power of Maya, he perceives the expansion (origination) and diversity of beings from that one Self alone, like a dream or magic. At that time, due to the absence of the perception of any homogeneous (sajatiya) or heterogeneous (vijatiya) differences, he becomes Brahman itself, free from all miseries. At that time, as the Sruti says: 'When to the knower all beings have become the Self, then what delusion, what sorrow can there be for him who sees oneness?' (Isha Upanishad 7). In the previous verse ('prakrityaiva ca...'), the difference in the Self was refuted; whereas in this verse ('yada bhuta-prithag-bhavam...'), the difference in the non-self (Kshetra) is also refuted—this is the distinction.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Now, the commentator says that since all beings are non-different by being merely of the nature of Prakriti, by not seeing any difference in the Self due to the diversity of beings, one attains Brahmanhood. This is stated in the verse beginning with 'yada'. When one reflects and sees that the separate existence (prithag-bhavam), i.e., difference or diversity of all moving and non-moving beings, is situated in one (ekastham), that is, resting in the one Prakriti which is the power of the Lord, at the time of dissolution. And when he sees the expansion (evolution) of beings from that very Prakriti alone at the time of creation. Then, seeing the non-difference of beings as they are merely modifications of Prakriti, he attains the all-pervading Brahman. The meaning is that he becomes Brahman itself.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Refuting the Samkhya view of the absolute separation of Prakriti and its modifications from the Purusha, the commentator elaborates on the same right vision (samyag-darshana) in different words through the verse beginning with 'yada'. When, at which time, following the instructions of the scriptures and the teacher, one directly realizes that the separate existence (prithag-bhavam) or diversity of beings is situated in one (ekastham), that is, in the one Self, the non-different inner Brahman, which is the substratum of the entire universe including Prakriti, and is of the nature of Existence. Since the imagined is not different from its substratum, it is non-different from the Self, and one realizes: 'All this is indeed the Self', 'All this is indeed Brahman', 'There is no diversity here whatsoever', 'I am Brahman'. And one perceives the expansion (evolution) of the entire universe from that Supreme Self alone, as in the scriptural declarations: 'From whom indeed these beings are born...', 'From Bliss indeed these beings are born...', 'From that Self, space was born...', 'It willed, it created fire/light...'. Then, at that time, he attains Brahman. The meaning is that he becomes Brahman itself, as the Sruti declares: 'He who knows Brahman becomes Brahman'. The alternative explanation—that 'one sees them resting in the one Prakriti, the power of the Lord, at the time of dissolution, and sees their expansion from that very Prakriti at the time of creation'—was not accepted by the Acharya (Shankaracharya) because it fails to refute the aforementioned Samkhya objection and makes the statement 'he becomes Brahman' illogical.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Now, the distinction characterized by changeability (of Prakriti) and unchangeability (of Purusha) is stated in this verse. With the word 'eka' (one) intended to specify one of the two, the word 'bhuta' (beings) here refers to the combination of consciousness and matter mutually bound like sesame seeds and oil; with this in mind, the commentator says 'Prakriti and Purusa...'. The word 'prithag-bhava' here includes without distinction the differences in species, qualities, etc. All of these, being of the nature of state-transitions, represent difference from the changeless Purusha; with this in mind, he says 'the separate existence of divine nature, human nature, shortness, tallness, etc.'. The word 'eka' here is meant to specify one of the two natures, hence the commentator says 'abiding in one principle'. What is that one? To this he says 'resting in Prakriti'. If the Self (Atma) were intended by the word 'eka' here, then the perception of differences of demigods, etc., would be ascribed to the Self itself, which would contradict statements like 'seeing equally' (13.29) and 'the wise see with an equal eye' (5.18); with this in mind, the commentator says 'not resting in the Self'. The talk of others (Advaitins) that here the one Brahman, which is Pure Existence, is described as the locus of all distinctions and transformations, stands refuted already. In passages like 'You are indeed the Self named son...', whatever transformation of the Self into a son, etc., is perceived, it appears only as pertaining to the aspect of Prakriti, keeping in view the text 'You are born from limb to limb...'; with this in mind, the verse says 'tatah eva ca vistaram' (and the expansion from That alone). He explains this as 'from Prakriti alone'. In the initial multi-form transformation of Prakriti as demigods, etc., and in the subsequent lineage-based transformations, only the proximity of the experiencing Purusha for the sake of experience is required, but the Purusha does not undergo modifications like the body, which is the meaning of the word 'eva' (alone). In 'brahma sampadyate', since becoming the Supreme Self is contradictory (in Visishtadvaita) and the state of being an individual soul is eternally established, attaining similarity with the Supreme Brahman is meant, in agreement with texts like 'attains supreme equality' (Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.3) and 'have attained similarity to My nature' (14.2); with this in mind, the commentator says 'unlimited'. Or rather, the idea is that by mere discrimination of body and Self, the direct attainment of the Supreme Self does not take place. 'Brahma sampadyate' means he becomes Brahman; the practical import is 'attains the Self'. Or 'Brahma' is in the accusative case, meaning he fully attains Brahman.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Similarly, when one sees the subtlety in the dissolution of all and the expansion in the creation as arising from Brahman itself, then one attains Brahmanhood. This is stated in the verse beginning with 'yada'. When one perceives that the separate existence (prithag-bhavam), i.e., diversity of all moving and non-moving beings, consisting of various wonderful and manifold forms, is situated in one (ekastham), that is, resting in the very nature of Brahman which is characterized by the desire for dissolution and play, at the time of dissolution. And again, when one perceives their expansion (manifestation) from that Brahman alone, who desires to play in the universe, at the time of creation. Then, he attains Brahman, meaning he achieves Brahmanhood.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
An objection may be raised: How is agency (kartritva) attributed only to Prakriti and not to the Self? Anticipating this, the commentator says 'yada'. When one perceives, in accordance with the teachings of the scriptures and the preceptor, that the separate existence (prithag-bhavam) or diversity of all beings beginning with space (ether) and including the viviparous, etc., which is visible here, is situated in one (ekastham), that is, resting in the one Self, merged like a snake in a rope, or earrings in gold. And when he sees the expansion (manifestation) from that one Self alone, following the arising of the diversity of beings, like a dream. Then he attains Brahman, i.e., he becomes Brahman itself. This is the idea: Agency is indeed the possession of action, and action is movement (parispanda); and that is possible only for the limited, separate, and material intellect (buddhi), etc., and not for the all-pervading Self which swallows up the diversity of all beings.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
When a sensible man ceases to see different identities due to different material bodies and he sees how beings are expanded everywhere, he attains to the Brahman conception.

 When one can see that the various bodies of living entities arise due to the different desires of the individual soul and do not actually belong to the soul itself, one actually sees. In the material conception of life, we find someone a demigod, someone a human being, a dog, a cat, etc. This is material vision, not actual vision. This material differentiation is due to a material conception of life. After the destruction of the material body, the spirit soul is one. The spirit soul, due to contact with material nature, gets different types of bodies. When one can see this, he attains spiritual vision; thus being freed from differentiations like man, animal, big, low, etc., one becomes purified in his consciousness and able to develop Kṛṣṇa consciousness in his spiritual identity. How he then sees things will be explained in the next verse.
