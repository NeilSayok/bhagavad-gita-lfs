Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
To treat karma-yoga and jnana-yoga as rival, mutually opposed paths is to misunderstand both of them. Being complementary to one another, they are meant to be taken up in sequence, one after the other. When action is first performed without desire for its fruit, the many latent impressions (vasanas) lodged in the mind are gradually worn away. As the mind thus becomes pure, it gains one-pointedness and steadiness, through which it becomes absorbed in meditation and directly realises the Supreme Truth.

People of foreign cultures find it hard to understand Hindu thought, since the variety of spiritual disciplines and the seemingly contradictory instructions confuse their understanding. But it would be as great and absurd an error to call Hinduism unscientific merely for this reason as it would be to call medicine unscientific because the same physician prescribes different medicines to different patients on the same day.

Among those fit for spiritual practice there are broadly two types of people: the active/dynamic and the reflective/contemplative. Their natures differ so greatly that prescribing one single discipline for both would mean discouraging and neglecting one of the two groups. The Gita is scripture meant for the welfare not of Hindus alone but of all mankind; hence, for the benefit of all, it is necessary to prescribe disciplines suited to the mental and intellectual capacity of each group.

Therefore the Lord states clearly here that karma-yoga is taught for the person of active nature, and jnana-yoga for the contemplative aspirant. By the word 'pura' (of old) He indicates that these two paths have existed in the world since the very beginning of creation.

In this verse Sri Krishna, for the first time, gives a glimpse of His true identity. Had the teaching of the Gita been merely that of a mortal man named Krishna, son of Devaki, it would at best have consisted of conclusions reached through that person's own limited experience of life -- and experienced facts keep changing, so that earlier conclusions become outdated as they change. With changing social, political and economic conditions, and with the progress of science, countless earlier theories of sociology, economics and science have become obsolete. Had the Gita been merely the conclusions reached by the intellect of a man named Krishna, it too would by now have become obsolete and only its remnants would survive. But here Sri Krishna clearly says that these two paths were declared by Him at the beginning of creation (pura) -- meaning that He was not speaking here as the blue-complexioned cowherd of Vrindavan, the beloved companion of the gopis, or as the great statesman of His age, but as one who knows His own true nature, the seer of Truth, the perfected being, and God Himself, giving this teaching. At that moment He was speaking neither as Arjuna's charioteer, nor as a friend, nor as a well-wisher of the Pandavas, but with full awareness of His own transcendent nature as the very substratum and cause of the universe, established in the Truth beyond time and causation, introducing Himself as the original teacher of these two paths.

Karma-yoga is a gradual, indirect means to the goal, not a direct one -- it renders one fit to receive knowledge, through which jnana-yoga leads directly to the goal. To explain this the Lord continues in the next verse.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
Even though knowledge (buddhi) is the higher path, thinking 'since you are entitled to action too, I shall engage you in that', the Lord speaks this verse beginning 'loke...' There are two kinds of people in the world: those established in jnana-nishtha by renouncing householder and other duties, like Sanaka and the other sages; and those established equally in jnana-nishtha while remaining engaged in their prescribed duties in accordance with My ordinance, like Janaka. 'Sankhyas' means knowers such as Sanaka; 'Yogins' means those established through the means of action, such as Janaka. Even those in jnana-nishtha, when required by their eligibility, by the will of the Lord, and for the sake of the welfare of the world (loka-sangraha), take up action -- they too are called 'yogins.' Nishtha means an abiding state. You, however, like Janaka, are fit for knowledge while continuing to act, not for knowledge attained by renouncing action, as Sanaka did. Indeed, even Priyavrata and others, who acted solely by the Lord's will, were themselves men of knowledge, as stated: 'By the Lord's will, the eligibility for action was imposed [upon them]' (Bhagavata 5.1.23).

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Having explained the earlier question as implying a contradiction (between combining knowledge and action), the answer is now shown to resolve that very contradiction. In the sphere of ordinary experience, the three twice-born classes are entitled to follow either scriptural knowledge or scriptural action; that their state is twofold was declared by Me -- this is how the first half of the verse is construed, and 'nishtha' is explained as meaning 'that which is to be practised.' By pointing to the Lord's teaching of this at the beginning of creation, and by noting the Lord's omniscience, all possibility that the teaching is unreliable or mistaken is removed. The word 'anagha' (sinless) indicates Arjuna's fitness to receive the Lord's instruction. 'Jnana' here means knowledge of the Supreme Reality, itself called 'yoga' because it is 'that by which one becomes united with Brahman.' Explaining who is meant by 'Sankhya': one endowed with discriminative knowledge of Self and non-Self, who has embraced renunciation from the very stage of celibacy, whose understanding of the Vedantic truth is fully ascertained -- such a monk, called a paramahamsa, established in Brahman alone. 'Karma' refers to duties enjoined by varna and ashrama, itself called 'yoga' in the sense of 'that by which prosperity is attained'; steadfastness through this is declared for men of action. Having thus explained each word of the verse, the final purport is given: had it been the Lord's intention -- stated, or to be stated later in the Gita, or taught in the Vedas -- that knowledge and action are to be combined and practised together by one and the same person for one and the same goal, He would not here tell His dear and surrendered Arjuna that steadfastness in knowledge and steadfastness in action are meant for different, specifically qualified persons. Nor can it be supposed that the Lord meant Arjuna alone to combine both after hearing of them, while telling others they are meant for different people -- for that would make the Lord partial and unreliable, which is untenable. This conclusion, that knowledge and action are not to be combined, is then summarized.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Sri Bhagavan said: O sinless (anagha) Arjuna, in this human world two kinds of steadfastness (nishtha) were declared by Me before -- the steadfastness of the wise (jnanis), through jnana-yoga, and the steadfastness of the yogis, through karma-yoga.

Explanation: The very fact that Arjuna asks about his own true welfare is itself proof of his sinlessness, since an intense longing for one's own good destroys one's accumulated sins. 'Loke asmin' (in this world) is to be understood as referring to the human body/human birth, since it is only in human birth that one has the right (adhikara) to practise either jnana-yoga or karma-yoga.

'Nishtha' -- abiding in equanimity -- is really one single state, attainable by two different means, jnana-yoga and karma-yoga. This is what Krishna meant earlier at 2.39: 'This understanding has been explained to you with regard to Sankhya; now hear it with regard to Yoga.' 'Pura' (of old) here means 'a little before' -- i.e., in the previous chapter, on which Arjuna's present doubt rests -- rather than 'from time immemorial'; though both nishthas were explained separately in the previous chapter, in neither of them was renunciation of action actually prescribed.

A subtle point: just as there are two kinds of steadfastness in the world, there are also two kinds of beings in the world (15.16) -- the perishable (kshara, the world) and the imperishable (akshara, the true Self). Remaining equanimous toward gain and loss regarding the perishable is karma-yoga; turning away from the perishable and abiding in the imperishable is jnana-yoga. But superior to both the perishable and the imperishable is the Supreme Person, called Purushottama (15.17-18), beyond the perishable and higher than the imperishable. Total, wholehearted surrender to that Supreme Person is called bhagavan-nishtha, or bhakti-yoga. Thus karma-yoga is predominantly concerned with the perishable, jnana-yoga with the imperishable, and bhakti-yoga with the Supreme Person Himself.

Sankhya-nishtha and yoga-nishtha are states the aspirant reaches by his own effort, but bhagavan-nishtha is not something he attains purely on his own. In jnana-yoga and karma-yoga the aspirant has direct experience of 'I am' and 'the world is'; the jnani severs his relation with the world and abides in his own true nature, while the karma-yogi uses worldly things (such as the body) in the service of the world and thereby severs attachment to it. But in bhagavan-nishtha, the aspirant does not initially have direct experience of God, but firm faith that there exists a Reality (God) distinct from both his own self and the world; trusting in this with faith, he surrenders himself entirely to God. Thus in Sankhya-nishtha and yoga-nishtha, 'knowing' (discrimination) is central, while in bhagavan-nishtha, 'believing' (faith and trust) is central. Yet there is no real difference between the two, since firm faith, like firm knowledge, admits no doubt.

Sankhya-nishtha and yoga-nishtha are means dependent on the aspirant's own effort, but bhagavan-nishtha depends on God and His grace. This bhagavan-nishtha is described at various places in the Gita -- for instance, later in this very chapter after describing the two nishthas, devotion is described at 3.30 ('surrendering all actions to Me'); similarly in chapter 5, after describing the two nishthas, devotion is again described at 5.10 and elsewhere.

'Jnanayogena sankhyanam': Understanding that all the qualities born of Prakriti act only among the gunas and senses (3.28), and that 'I' (the Self) have no real connection with them, and thereby completely renouncing the sense of being the doer in all actions -- this is jnana-yoga. At the very start of His teaching, the Lord described Sankhya-yoga (jnana-yoga) by distinguishing the perishable body from the imperishable indweller, also called the unreal (asat) and the real (sat) at 2.16.

'Karmayogena yoginam': Whatever scripturally sanctioned duty presents itself according to one's varna, ashrama, nature and circumstance, performing it while entirely giving up desire, sense of ownership and attachment regarding the action and its fruit, and remaining equanimous in success and failure -- this is karma-yoga. The Lord describes karma-yoga mainly in the second chapter, verses 47 and 48 -- verse 47 states the principle of karma-yoga, and verse 48 states the method of putting it into practice.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
If this is indeed the question, how does the Lord's answering statement, dividing steadfastness into two kinds, actually resolve it? The purport is this: though it is true that knowledge (buddhi) is superior to action performed for desired results, the Lord still directs Arjuna toward the alternative duty of fighting, because Arjuna remains entitled (adhikari) to action -- even action that causes some disturbance -- since duties enjoined for the three ashramas, such as sacrifices and battle, are not purely desire-driven acts, but, depending on the performer's intention, can serve the purpose of knowledge as well; hence there is nothing improper in enjoining Arjuna to it, given that battle can indeed serve the purpose of buddhi (knowledge).

The verse itself is then explained: 'dvividha' (twofold) shows that people of the world are not of one type alone. Some, like Sanaka and the other sages, are established in jnana-nishtha having renounced householder and other duties; others, equally established in jnana-nishtha, remain within their station (such as the householder stage) while practising duties like battle that also serve as means to knowledge, as with Janaka -- so there is no single, uniform class implied. 'Sankhyas' means those established in knowledge (jnana-nishtha), those who have attained correct understanding (sankhya, 'right knowing') of the Self; 'Yogins' means those fit for karma-yoga, i.e., persons whose intellect is still engaged with sense-objects, as opposed to those whose intellect is unperturbed and hence fit for jnana-yoga alone -- for jnana-nishtha itself, according to eligibility and by the Lord's will, for the sake of the world's welfare, can also involve action, such persons too being called yogins. Because there is thus a genuine difference of eligibility (adhikara-bheda) among people, this teaching is not self-contradictory.

The text further addresses the objection that Arjuna, too, should simply be permitted to take up the renunciate's life like the sages, rather than being directed to action: only those not otherwise qualified (for action) have the right to adopt the renunciate order; those already qualified for action must, by the Lord's rule, continue performing their prescribed duties -- such as those of the householder stage -- without abandoning them, but doing so without desire for their fruits; and Arjuna, being so qualified, falls under this rule. In illustration, it is noted that Priyavrata, though wishing to take up the renunciate's life, was restrained from doing so by Brahma (Hiranyagarbha) precisely because his qualification for action had not yet been transcended -- showing that persons still bound by their eligibility for action are not simply free to renounce it at will.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
[Krishna responds to Arjuna's charge of confusing him with seemingly contradictory words:] 'I do not deceive anyone, let alone one so dear to Me as you. If you feel confused, it is not because My words are truly conflicting, for they teach two distinct nishthas meant for two distinct classes of aspirants -- there is no real contradiction. Rather, it is your own limited grasp of My meaning that produces the appearance of confusion' -- hence the qualifying 'as if' (iva) is used, since the Lord, being supremely compassionate, does not actually delude anyone; the confusion arises from the hearer's own shortcoming.

If a single class of aspirant were meant, then knowledge and action -- being mutually contradictory -- could not be combined by one person, nor, having different objects, could they simply be treated as optional alternatives; hence Arjuna's earlier question, asking which single one should be followed, presupposed a single class of aspirant. The resolution given here is that there are, in fact, two different classes of aspirants -- pure-minded and impure-minded -- and so two distinct nishthas are meant, not a genuine contradiction. This distinction of eligibility (adhikara-bheda) was already clearly explained in the previous chapter, spoken to Arjuna out of the Lord's supreme compassion, precisely so that Arjuna would not wrongly assume that only a single nishtha applies to him. The address 'anagha' (sinless) indicates Arjuna's readiness to receive this teaching.

The singular word 'nishtha' (rather than a dual or plural) shows that there is really one steadfastness, differentiated only by the distinction between means and end (sadhana and sadhya), not two wholly separate, independent steadfastnesses -- as later confirmed by 'he who sees Sankhya and Yoga as one, he truly sees' (5.4-5, cf. 4.42 idea). That one nishtha's two aspects are then explained: for the Sankhyas -- those who have attained true knowledge of the Self, renounced from the very stage of celibacy, whose understanding of Vedantic truth is fully ascertained, who have risen to the plane of knowledge, whose minds are pure -- steadfastness through jnana-yoga (knowledge itself as 'yoga', in the sense of 'that by which one is united with Brahman') is declared, in keeping with verses such as 'having restrained all the senses, one should sit engaged, devoted to Me' (2.61 and similar). For the yogins -- those of impure mind, not yet risen to the plane of knowledge, but qualified for action -- steadfastness through karma-yoga ('yoga' meaning 'that by which the mind is purified') is declared, in keeping with verses such as 'there is nothing more beneficial for a kshatriya than a righteous war' (2.31), the purpose of such karma-yoga being purification of mind leading in due course to the plane of knowledge.

Hence there is neither a genuine combination (samuccaya) nor a mere optional choice (vikalpa) between knowledge and action; rather, for those of purified mind, knowledge alone through complete renunciation of action is meant, while this twofold teaching to Arjuna himself reflects only the difference between the states of an impure and a purified mind for one and the same person over time -- as summarized in 'this teaching has been given to you with regard to Sankhya; now hear it with regard to Yoga' (2.39). Since a single person benefits from both stages in sequence, there is no futility in teaching both despite their immediate difference of application. This is illustrated later: action prescribed for one of impure mind, up to the attainment of purity, is described in the thirteen verses from 'not by abstention from action...' through 'he lives in vain' (3.4-16); while for the pure-minded knower who delights in the Self alone, no action whatever is required, as shown in the two verses beginning 'but he who rejoices in the Self alone...' (3.17-18). It will then be shown, beginning 'therefore, unattached...' (3.19), that even ordinarily binding action, when performed skilfully and without desire for its fruit, becomes a means to liberation through the purification of mind and the consequent rise of knowledge. Thereafter, raising the question 'impelled by what does a man commit sin...' (3.36), it is explained that action itself is not the true cause of impurity -- desire (kama) is the real culprit -- so that desireless action itself becomes a qualification for knowledge through the purification of mind; this the Lord continues to teach through the rest of the chapter.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Sri Bhagavan answers here, beginning 'loke asmin...' The meaning is this: had I taught two mutually independent paths -- karma-yoga and jnana-yoga -- as separate means to liberation, then your question, 'tell me which one is better,' would indeed be appropriate. But that is not what I taught; rather, through both, one and the same devotion to Brahman (brahma-nishtha) was taught, since the two stand related as subordinate and principal, and cannot function as independent paths. It is merely a difference of stage within a single path, spoken of according to a difference of eligibility (adhikara-bheda). In this world of aspirants divided into the pure-minded and the impure-minded, this twofold steadfastness toward liberation was clearly declared by Me, the omniscient one, in the previous chapter. The two divisions are: for the Sankhyas -- those of pure mind who have risen to the plane of knowledge -- steadfastness through jnana-yoga (meditation and the like) is taught for the maturing of knowledge, as in 'having controlled all the senses, one should sit engaged, devoted to Me' (2.61). For the yogins -- those aspiring to rise to the plane of the Sankhyas, fit for karma-yoga as the means of purifying the mind and thereby ascending -- steadfastness through karma-yoga is taught, as in 'there is nothing better for a kshatriya than a righteous war' (2.31). Thus this twofold teaching was given only according to the difference between the state of a pure and an impure mind, as stated: 'this teaching has been given to you with regard to Sankhya; now hear it with regard to Yoga' (2.39).

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Sri Bhagavan gives an answer suited to Arjuna's question, beginning 'loke...' In this world, among the twice-born classes qualified for scriptural action, divided into the pure-minded and impure-minded, a twofold steadfastness -- differentiated as means and end -- was declared of old by Me, the omniscient Lord, at the beginning of creation, after bringing forth beings, when I revealed the Vedic tradition as the means to both worldly prosperity and liberation. Through karma-yoga -- 'yoga' meaning 'that by which one is united with the knowledge which purifies the mind', i.e. action itself -- steadfastness is declared by Me for the yogins, men of action whose minds are still impure.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Revealing that His earlier statement was in fact free of contradiction, Sri Bhagavan speaks here, beginning 'loke asmin...' Since He says 'declared by Me', there is an implicit, gently ironic suggestion that Arjuna had not properly grasped what had already been well said. 'In this world' points to the world as full of aspirants of varied types, indicating that a genuine difference of eligibility (adhikara-bheda) between jnana-yoga and karma-yoga is possible -- illustrated by how mutually conflicting duties are assigned to different people according to varna, ashrama, place, time, or desire, as with a physician treating patients differently. The word 'anagha' conveys the same idea: just as you, being sinless, are qualified for the very means of liberation, others -- merely free of gross sin -- are qualified only for karma-yoga aimed at desired results, while those still more purified are qualified for jnana-yoga; this is the fitting instruction of an omniscient physician treating the fever of worldly existence according to each patient's condition. 'Prokta' (declared), with its intensifying prefix, implies the teaching did not transgress the bounds of each one's eligibility, and, since the twofoldness itself was already understood, that it was stated without confusion or admixture. 'By Me' implies one who knows the differences among aspirants, desires their true welfare, and is untouched by illusion, deception, error, or attachment.

Anticipating the objection -- if jnana-yoga is superior, why is karma-yoga needed at all? -- it is explained that even when the desire for liberation has arisen, not everyone is immediately fit for jnana-yoga; only those whose impurities have been worn away through hundreds of past lives of good conduct are fit at once, while others remain governed by their present capacity -- hence the distinction between fitness for jnana-yoga and karma-yoga. To the further objection that the unqualified might then never attain fitness for jnana-yoga, it is explained that action performed without desire for its fruit, offered purely as worship of the Supreme Person, itself becomes -- through the Lord's grace, an unseen result of His omniscience, omnipotence and compassion -- a cause of knowledge. This is because the agitation of the senses that obstructs jnana-nishtha is rooted in mental impurity, itself born of beginningless good and bad deeds composed of rajas and tamas; and that impurity is removed precisely by desireless action, which brings forth sattva -- hence karma-yoga is described as favourable to jnana-nishtha, since it produces the calm that is its prerequisite, as in 'by righteousness one removes sin' (Taittiriya). It is further shown that karma performed purely as worship of the Supreme Person, without desire for fruit, itself leads directly to perfection, as will be stated later ('by worshipping Him through one's own duty, man attains perfection', 18.46).

The latter half of the verse is then explained: jnana-yoga is declared specifically for the Sankhyas -- here 'Sankhya' does not denote adherence to any particular metaphysical school, but simply one endowed with sankhya-buddhi, an intellect focused solely on discrimination of the Self; those unfit for this are eligible instead for karma-yoga, the word 'yogin' here meaning one connected with, and fit for, karma-yoga. This resolves the apparent contradiction, showing there is no real admixture in the Lord's teaching -- the same conclusion reached before.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
In order to dispel Arjuna's confusion, Krishna gives this question and answer, beginning 'loke asmin...' 'O sinless one (anagha), fit to hear My words -- in this world, established as it is in ordinary activity, a twofold steadfastness was declared by Me before, to you previously [in the second chapter], for the sake of establishing your eligibility for devotion (bhakti) -- not for your sake alone,' is the implied meaning. The twofoldness is then clarified: through jnana-yoga, steadfastness in Brahman is declared for the Sankhyas, i.e. those everywhere possessing knowledge of the Self as one with God; through karma-yoga, steadfastness in Brahman is declared for the yogins, i.e. the worshippers of the Lord. This twofold teaching was given to explain the true nature of each path, not specifically for Arjuna's sake alone.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Sri Bhagavan answers here, beginning 'loke asmin...' In the previous chapter I declared but a single steadfastness (nishtha), though it is of two aspects. Only one path to Brahman was taught, differentiated according to the aspirant's eligibility -- not two wholly independent paths to liberation, unrelated to each other. Addressing Arjuna as 'anagha' (pure-minded), the Lord asks him to properly consider the meaning of His words.

The two aspects are then explained: for the Sankhyas -- those who know the distinctness of Prakriti and Purusha, possessing discriminative knowledge of Self and non-Self -- steadfastness in Brahman is reached through jnana-yoga, the means to knowledge consisting of hearing, reflection and meditation on the Vedanta (shravana, manana, nididhyasana). For the yogins -- those characterised by equanimity in success and failure, as defined later (2.48) -- steadfastness in Brahman is reached through karma-yoga, meaning here the practice of unwavering meditative absorption (nirvikalpa-samadhi) alongside worship such as the evening prayers (sandhya).

The idea is this: through actions performed in this life or past lives for the Lord's pleasure, one whose mind (sattva) becomes purified, endowed with discrimination, dispassion, calm and the other virtues, whose intellect turns inward with a longing for liberation, becomes accomplished through hearing and reflection on the Vedanta alone. But if the mind is not yet sufficiently collected before undertaking such hearing, then meditation (nididhyasana) is additionally required for such a person -- hence the Vedanta-sutras describe nididhyasana as an optional supporting practice. This is the path of the Sankhyas.

Alternatively, one who offers all actions to the Supreme Guru and, without the discriminative process of hearing and reflection, through faith alone, ascertains from the guru's word that his true inner Self is of the nature of undifferentiated Brahman -- becoming free of doubt and misconception through an accomplished teacher, learning the method of worshipping the attributeless Brahman, and practising absorption (samadhi) in the intervals between actions -- directly realises the partless inner Self. This is the path of the yogins.

Thus, those endowed with the capacity for subtle discrimination (uha-apoha) are called Sankhyas, and those without it are called yogins. This is therefore a single steadfastness of two aspects, not two separate steadfastnesses -- one should not be confused on this point. As stated by sage Vasishtha: 'There are two methods for the cessation of mind, O Raghava -- yoga and knowledge. Yoga is the restraint of mental modifications; knowledge is right seeing. For some, yoga cannot be accomplished; for others, ascertainment of truth [through knowledge] cannot be accomplished -- hence the Supreme Lord Shiva declared two methods.' Just as, if the mind is held to be unreal (like a rope mistaken for a snake), correct knowledge alone destroys the illusion, so too, if the mind is held to be real (like an actual snake whose movement is restrained and stilled by a mantra), it is likewise restrained and destroyed through yoga. Yet in either view, the final, rootless dissolution of the mind occurs only at the exhaustion of one's prarabdha karma (the karma that has already begun to bear fruit in this life).

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: O sinless Arjuna, I have already explained that there are two classes of men who try to realize the self. Some are inclined to understand it by empirical, philosophical speculation, and others by devotional service.

 In the Second Chapter, verse 39, the Lord explained two kinds of procedures – namely sāṅkhya-yoga and karma-yoga, or buddhi-yoga. In this verse, the Lord explains the same more clearly. Sāṅkhya-yoga, or the analytical study of the nature of spirit and matter, is the subject matter for persons who are inclined to speculate and understand things by experimental knowledge and philosophy. The other class of men work in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, as it is explained in the sixty-first verse of the Second Chapter. The Lord has explained, also in the thirty-ninth verse, that by working by the principles of buddhi-yoga, or Kṛṣṇa consciousness, one can be relieved from the bonds of action; and, furthermore, there is no flaw in the process. The same principle is more clearly explained in the sixty-first verse – that this buddhi-yoga is to depend entirely on the Supreme (or more specifically, on Kṛṣṇa), and in this way all the senses can be brought under control very easily. Therefore, both the yogas are interdependent, as religion and philosophy. Religion without philosophy is sentiment, or sometimes fanaticism, while philosophy without religion is mental speculation. The ultimate goal is Kṛṣṇa, because the philosophers who are also sincerely searching after the Absolute Truth come in the end to Kṛṣṇa consciousness. This is also stated in the Bhagavad-gītā . The whole process is to understand the real position of the self in relation to the Superself. The indirect process is philosophical speculation, by which, gradually, one may come to the point of Kṛṣṇa consciousness; and the other process is directly connecting everything with Kṛṣṇa in Kṛṣṇa consciousness. Of these two, the path of Kṛṣṇa consciousness is better because it does not depend on purifying the senses by a philosophical process. Kṛṣṇa consciousness is itself the purifying process, and by the direct method of devotional service it is simultaneously easy and sublime.
