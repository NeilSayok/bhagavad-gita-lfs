Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
It is natural for the human mind to wonder what happens once peace is attained. When peace is gained, all sorrows come to an end — this verse gives us the very definition of happiness. Distraction (vikshepa) is what is called sorrow; therefore the peace of mind that results from the absence of distractions must itself be happiness. Peace is happiness, and happiness is peace.

Here, the destruction of sorrows should be understood as the result of the cessation of desires (vasanas). As explained in the introduction to the Gita, it is the veil of desires covering the intellect that causes a person to fall into grief and delusion, whereas the wise, through the practice of buddhi yoga described earlier, wear away their desires and thereby recognize the Self that lies beyond them. Ordinarily, freedom from desires seems difficult to attain, but it can be accomplished through self-restraint and the yoga of equanimity (samatva yoga).

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
How does mere serenity bring about the destruction of all sorrow? Because the intellect of one whose mind is serene becomes firmly established through direct, immediate realization of Brahman. Serenity (prasada) means the natural, spontaneous withdrawal from sense objects.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Since a person overwhelmed by manifold sorrows might think that steadiness of mind is unattainable, the question ‘what results from serenity’ is raised here, and this half-verse supplies the answer. Even though the intellect gains ease through the destruction of all sorrows, one might still ask how the steadiness of wisdom spoken of earlier is actually established — this is clarified by the phrase ‘of one whose mind is serene.’ A further fruit of serenity of intellect is also indicated. Hence, one should strive for serenity of the intellect. Having explained the literal sense of these two verses, the commentary concludes with their essential purport: to be ‘disciplined’ (yukta) means to be composed and free from subjugation to sense objects.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
A karma-yogi whose inner mind is under control, engaging with sense objects through senses that are mastered and free from attachment and aversion, attains purity of the inner faculties (antahkarana shuddhi). Once that purity arises, all of the practitioner’s sorrows are destroyed, and the intellect of such a pure-minded seeker very quickly becomes established in the Supreme.

Key points of the commentary: A controlled mind (‘vidheyatma’) is essential for karma yoga — without it, there remains a risk of attachment and downfall even while performing action. Likewise the senses must be ‘self-controlled’ (atmavashya), meaning free from attachment and aversion; what matters is not whether one accepts or rejects sense objects, but that this be done without attachment or aversion, since both bind one to the world. ‘Engaging with objects’ (vishayan charan) means conducting all necessary worldly dealings without an enjoyment-seeking mindset (bhoga-buddhi), since it is this enjoyment-seeking attitude, not engagement itself, that causes downfall.

Through such attachment-free engagement, the practitioner attains inner clarity or serenity (prasada) — described elsewhere in the Gita (17.16) as a mental austerity higher than physical or verbal austerity. With that clarity, all sorrow is destroyed: attachment produces agitation, agitation produces craving, and craving produces all sorrow; when attachment ceases, clarity arises and sorrow ends along with it. All sorrows ultimately stem from one’s bondage to the body and the world, which in turn stems from craving for pleasure; when craving ends through serenity, that bondage ends, and so sorrow is wholly absent. This does not mean painful circumstances born of past karma will cease to occur, but that the mind of such a person will no longer be disturbed by them. The intellect of the serene-minded thus quickly becomes fixed in the Supreme, free from all doubt.

A further insight: whether it is serenity or intense longing toward God that becomes very strong, either one can quickly lead to realization of God — illustrated by the gopis, whose anguished longing to meet Krishna destroyed their sins, and whose joy in remembering Him destroyed their merits, so that freed of both they reached Him first. By contrast, ordinary worldly joy and sorrow only strengthen one’s bondage to the world, as seen in ordinary beings caught up in both. In both serenity and agitation the heart becomes, as it were, melted like wax — whatever impression enters it then, worldly or divine, becomes fixed and later leads to either spiritual elevation or downfall. Hence a practitioner should remain even-minded, neither elated on gaining the most cherished thing nor disturbed on encountering the most unwanted. This sets up the contrasting explanation given in the next two verses.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
An objection is raised: what exactly results from mere serenity, since scripture (Chandogya Upanishad 7.1.3, ‘the knower of the Self crosses over sorrow’) suggests it is knowledge, not serenity alone, that removes sorrow, and the destruction of all sorrow should not be stated as an immediate consequence without an intervening step of knowledge. This second half of the verse answers that objection: it should not be taken to mean that serenity has two separate results. Rather, serenity leads to direct realization of Brahman, which in turn brings about the destruction of all sorrow — so nothing is left unstated nor wrongly stated. This continues what was said at verse 2.50 about the wise discarding both good and bad karma as a fruit of knowledge, addressing the objection that such discarding would not be a valid human goal. A further question: someone whose desired objects come to him effortlessly might also be called untroubled or ‘serene’ — so how can serenity here specifically be the fruit of sense-mastery and a means to knowledge? The answer is that what is meant here by ‘prasada’ is the particular serenity that arises spontaneously and effortlessly as freedom from active pursuit of sense objects.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
An objection is raised: since the senses are by nature drawn toward their objects and this pull cannot be checked, the resulting fault seems unavoidable — so how can wisdom ever become firmly established? These two verses answer that objection. One who is self-controlled, and who, using senses free from attachment and aversion, engages with sense objects without indulging in them for enjoyment, attains serenity and tranquility of mind. The wisdom of such a serene-minded person is to be understood as firmly established.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Having said that one ‘attains serenity,’ the text now explains what results from that serenity. When the mind attains the clarity that is serenity, all the sorrows of this renunciant — physical, mental, and others, all born of ignorance — are destroyed. This is because the intellect of the serene-minded renunciant quickly becomes firmly and completely established in the form of the identity of Brahman and the Self, since there is no longer any obstruction from contrary notions. Strictly speaking the sequence is: serenity leads to steadiness of intellect, which removes the ignorance opposed to it, which in turn destroys all sorrow as its effect — yet the verse attributes the destruction of all sorrow directly to serenity itself, simply to emphasize the importance of striving for serenity; there is no real contradiction in this.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
What results from serenity is explained here: when serenity arises, all sorrow is destroyed, and consequently the intellect of the serene-minded person becomes firmly established.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
When serenity arises, this discerning person’s threefold sorrows — physical, mental, and those caused by unseen or divine forces — are destroyed. Why does this happen? Because the intellect of the serene-minded quickly becomes unwavering, fixed firmly in the nature of the Self. Since the serene-minded person of steady intellect thus attains fulfillment, the sense of the passage is that a disciplined person should, with senses free from attachment and aversion, engage only in those necessary, life-sustaining activities not prohibited by scripture.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Anticipating the question of what results from serenity, the verse explains: once serenity arises in this person who has thereby received grace, all his sorrows are destroyed. The word ‘all’ indicates the destruction even of the sorrow of separation from the Divine, implying that he remains constantly united with the Lord. Then, once all sorrow is destroyed, what follows? The intellect of the person whose mind has become serene through this destruction of sorrow quickly becomes firmly established — understood as being established in Me, the Lord.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Moreover, when the mind attains serenity, all of this person’s sorrows, which are rooted in desire, are removed, because desire itself ceases to arise. The reason desire no longer arises is explained: because the intellect of the serene-minded person, taking the form of the ascertained identity of Brahman and the Self, quickly becomes firmly and steadily established. And once that is established, since there remains nothing further to be attained, no desire arises.
