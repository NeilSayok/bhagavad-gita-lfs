Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The fruit of all actions depends on the quality of their standard. Earlier, the threefold abandonment was described, and now, their threefold fruits are being described here. The projection of man's desire into the outer world is called action. And every action, according to the state of mind and purpose of the doer, stamps its impression on the inner instrument (antahkarana) of that doer. The nature of the human mind is to repeat actions. Future thoughts merely follow the thought-prints of the past. Thus, actions give rise to desires (vasanas), and then our reactions occur in accordance with these desires with the events of the world. In philosophy, the fruit of action refers not only to the worldly fruit, but also to the impressions (samskaras) of actions that get stamped on our inner instrument. These fruits of actions are stated here to be threefold: (1) desired (ishta), i.e., auspicious and beneficial; (2) undesired (anishta), i.e., inauspicious and harmful; and (3) mixed (mishra), i.e., a combination of both. In the continuous flow of time, the present determines the near future. Therefore, it is clear that our desires with various structures will determine our reactions to the events of the near future. If we extend this principle to the moment of death of our present body, the fact will become clear that our post-mortem body, place of birth, etc., will be determined in accordance with our desires at that time. This is the principle of rebirth propounded in Sanatana Dharma. The fruit of auspicious actions is the happiness of higher worlds, and the fruit of inauspicious actions is undesired, i.e., lower-level, painful animal life. The mixture of auspicious and inauspicious results in the attainment of a human body. While residing in this body, we can either make our bonds even stronger or attain complete liberation from them. Therefore, we must choose our goal. Undoubtedly, the call to attain the highest goal continues to occur in the hearts of all human beings, but the beasts in the form of defects residing in the same heart also keep barking, screaming, calling, and roaring. They delude us and lead us away from our goal. If a person identifies with his noble ideal, his defects gradually disappear. And if he gets attached to the limitations (upadhis) and objects, his defects keep increasing continuously, shrouding his divine nature. In short, in this test of strength between the higher and the lower, the deciding factor is man's own personality. To attain complete liberation from the threefold fruits mentioned in this verse, the seeker must realize his Self-nature which is beyond the three attributes (trigunatita). The subtle distinction between abandonment (tyaga) and renunciation (sannyasa) is clarified here. Abandonment means the effortful renunciation of identification with the modifications of the mind influenced by the three attributes, and renunciation means the abandonment of that very ego which becomes the doer of auspicious/inauspicious actions and the enjoyer of desired/undesired fruits. In the entire Gita, a very beautiful and detailed discussion of the principles and practices of reorganization of man's personality has been presented. Its summary is: (1) First of all, performing duties with an attitude of dedication to God, abandoning ego and selfishness; (2) upon attaining purification of the inner instrument in this manner, hearing (shravana) and reflecting (manana) on the Self-nature through the authority of Vedanta; and (3) thereafter, realizing the Self through meditation (nididhyasana) and attaining steadfastness in that very Reality. In the experience of the Self, the ocean of worldly existence dries up. In the pure Self, there is no action of becoming anything. It always remains untouched by the desires (vasanas) of the mind. Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Raising the doubt, "Even though the renunciation of all actions is impossible for the competent person described, if there is no fruit, why should he perform action?", he asks: "What then...?". He gives the alternative options, wishing to ask about the fruit of secondary or primary renunciation, in the words: "It is being stated...". The abandonment of all actions means the abandonment of the desire for fruits even while performing them. And that is secondary renunciation; he states its fruit: "undesired...". But in primary renunciation consisting of the complete abandonment of all actions, the fruit is indeed the destruction of the entire transmigratory existence through right Knowledge; this He says in: "but not...". He explains the first three lines of the verse in: "aniṣṭam..." etc. The distinction is: the word "etc." in "animals, etc." is for the purpose of collecting the remaining lower births, and the word "etc." in "gods, etc." is for the purpose of grasping the remaining higher births. He explains the etymology of the word "fruit" (phalam): "external...". Having stated its manifoldness through various instruments, he states its falsity: "produced by ignorance...". To show that it is merely of the nature of appearance because it is created by ignorance, he gives an illustration: "like jugglery...". He indicates its deceptiveness in appearance in: "great delusion...". Since what is produced by ignorance rests on ignorance, it does not in reality rest on the innermost Self; this he says in: "innermost...". If the said fruit is intended for active persons, how is it proper that the three lines of the verse refer to the fruit of secondary renunciation? To this he says: "unreal...". The meaning is: for active persons who are devoid of desire for fruits, after the fall of the body, a fruit corresponding to their actions is inevitable. Having stated the fruit of secondary renunciation in the case of those who are indeed active but are free from desire for fruits—they being secondary renouncers—he explains the fourth line of the verse: "but not...". He distinguishes it from the secondary renunciation which was the immediate context: "absolute...". He teaches their principal attribute: "solely...". The connection is: the said fruit does not exist for them in any place or at any time. If so, raising the doubt "Then absolute renunciation should not be performed because it has no fruit", he says "Not so, because it culminates in Liberation": "indeed, not...".

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
The threefold fruit of action — desired (pleasant), undesired (painful), and mixed — accrues after death also to those who have not renounced the fruits of actions; but never to those who have renounced the fruits of actions.

Commentary — aniṣṭamiṣṭaṃ miśraṃ ca trividhaṃ karmaṇaḥ phalam — The fruit of action is of three kinds: desired, undesired, and mixed. The circumstance which a person wants is the desired fruit of action, the circumstance which a person does not want is the undesired fruit of action, and that which has some part of the desired and some part of the undesired is the mixed fruit of action. In reality, if observed, the fruit in the world is mostly mixed. For example, having wealth brings both favorable (desired) and unfavorable (undesired) circumstances. Livelihood is maintained by wealth — this is favorableness; and tax is levied, wealth is destroyed or snatched away — this is unfavorableness. The implication is that even in the desired, there is a partial undesired, and even in the undesired, there is a partial desired. Because the entire world is composed of the three attributes (Gita 18.40); this birth is also a place of misery (8.15) and devoid of happiness (9.33). Therefore, whether it is a desired (favorable) or undesired (unfavorable) circumstance, it is never entirely favorable or unfavorable. Here, saying desired and undesired means that favorableness predominates in the desired and unfavorableness predominates in the undesired. In reality, the fruit of actions is indeed mixed, because no action is entirely free from defects (18.48). bhavatyatyāgināṃ pretya — All the above-mentioned fruits accrue only to the non-abandoners, i.e., those who perform actions keeping desire for fruits, and not to sannyasis. The reason is that whatever actions occur, they all occur by Nature (prakriti), i.e., by the body, senses, mind, and intellect which are the effects of Nature, and the circumstance in the form of fruit is also created by Nature. Therefore, the relationship of actions and their fruits is only with Nature, not with the Self (the conscious nature). But when the Self severs its relationship with them, then it does not become the enjoyer, but rather becomes an abandoner. Non-abandoner (atyagi) means: those who are not like the abandoners mentioned in the previous two verses (tenth and eleventh), i.e., those who have not renounced the fruits of actions. Favorable or unfavorable circumstances in the form of the three fruits of action — desired, undesired, and mixed — continue to come before such non-abandoners, due to which they continue to be happy or sad. Being happy or sad due to them is indeed bondage. In reality, being happy with favorableness is the cause of being sad in unfavorableness, because one who enjoys pleasure born of circumstances can never escape pain. As long as he enjoys pleasure, he will inevitably be sad in unfavorable circumstances. Anxiety, grief, fear, agitation, etc., can never leave him, nor can he ever escape from them. By saying "pretya bhavati" (accrues after death), the implication is that those who are not renouncers of the fruits of actions definitely get these three fruits of action after death. But along with this, in the words "na tu saṃnyāsināṃ kvacit", it is said that those who are renouncers of the fruits of actions do not get the fruit of action anywhere, i.e., here or after death. This proves that non-abandoners get the fruit of action after death indeed, but they can also get the fruit of action here while alive. na tu saṃnyāsināṃ kvacit — Sannyasis (abandoners) do not have to experience the fruit of action anywhere, i.e., in this world or the next, in this birth or after death. Yes, according to the actions performed in past births, favorable or unfavorable circumstances do come before them in this birth; but by the power of their discrimination, they do not become enjoyers of those circumstances, nor do they become happy or sad due to them; that is, they remain completely untainted. Why do sannyasis, i.e., abandoners, not have to experience the fruit? The reason is that they do not do anything for themselves. They attain clear discrimination that for their own true nature, there is no need for any activity or object at all. If a practitioner does anything with the desire to acquire for himself, he merely keeps his personality stable, because he considers his own interest separate from the interest of the entire world. When he does not consider his own interest separate from the interest of the entire world, i.e., considers his interest in the interest of all, then he automatically becomes devoted to the welfare of all beings (sarva-bhūta-hite ratāḥ). Then the activities performed by his physical body, the thought of welfare of others by his subtle body, and the stability of his causal body — all three occur solely for the welfare of all living beings in the world. The reason is that all the materials like the body etc. are non-different from the world. Desiring one's own interest from that material — this is the mistake, which is the cause of one's limitation. Here, in the word "saṃnyāsinām", the identity of the abandoner (Karma Yogi) and the renouncer (Sankhya Yogi) is established. For example, just as a Karma Yogi remains unattached to actions, so does a Sankhya Yogi remain completely untainted by actions. The Karma Yogi, while performing actions (without desire), does not keep a relationship with the fruit, whereas the Sankhya Yogi does not keep even the slightest relationship with action itself. The Karma Yogi severs relationship with the fruit, i.e., renounces possessiveness (mamata), whereas the Sankhya Yogi renounces the conceit of agency, i.e., egoism (ahanta). Upon the renunciation of possessiveness, egoism also is automatically renounced; and upon the renunciation of egoism, possessiveness also is automatically renounced. Therefore, the Lord has taught the renunciation of egoism after the renunciation of possessiveness in Karma Yoga — "nirmamo nirahankāraḥ" (2.71); and in Sankhya Yoga, the renunciation of possessiveness after the renunciation of egoism — "ahankāraṃ balaṃ darpaṃ kāmaṃ krodhaṃ parigraham; vimucya nirmamaḥ..." (18.53). There is a difference in the process of renunciation of these two, but neither of them has any relationship with changing Nature or the effect of Nature. That is, in essence, the Karma Yogi and the Sankhya Yogi become one. Earlier, Arjuna asked that he wanted to know the essence of sannyasa and tyaga; therefore, the Lord has explained this essence of both with the word "saṃnyāsinām" here. The attitude of a Karma Yogi is: nothing is mine, nothing is needed for myself, and nothing has to be done for myself. Similarly, the attitude of a Sankhya Yogi is: nothing is mine, and nothing is needed for myself. The Sankhya Yogi does not consider even the slightest relationship with Nature and its effect; therefore, saying "nothing has to be done for himself" does not even arise for him. The purpose of using the word "saṃnyāsinām" instead of "tyāginām" here is that the same unattachment which is attained through Sankhya Yoga is also attained through abandonment, i.e., Karma Yoga (Gita 5.4-5). Secondly, up to this point the Lord has explained unattachment through Karma Yoga; now, by using the word "saṃnyāsinām", He also sows the seed for explaining unattachment through Sankhya Yoga in the following verses. Relationship — In the previous verse it was said that only the renouncer of the fruits of actions is in reality a man of renunciation. What happens if a person does not renounce the fruits of actions? This is explained in the next verse.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
The 'undesirable result' is Naraka etc., 'the desirable' is heaven etc., 'the mixed' is sons, cows, food etc., which are combined with some undesirable results. Those who have not renounced, namely, those who are devoid of renunciation of agency, possessiveness and fruits - they meet with threefold conseences after death. The meaning of 'after death' (Pretya) may be understood as subseent to the performance of actions. But 'to those who have renounced, none whatsoever,' viz., to those who have relinished the sense of agency etc., no such results antagonistic to release accrue.

Here the purport is this:  Agnihotra, the great sacrifices etc. are obligatory throughout life and are reired for attaining the objects of desire; but in regard to release their application is different. Though externally they appear to be the same in their nature in both the conditions, they are different in their fruits by virtue of difference in application. Their application to release is seen in such texts as 'The Brahmanas desire to know Him by the study of the Vedas, by sacrifices, by gifts, by austerities conjoined with fasting' (Br. U., 4.4.22). Here the performance of actions without sense of agency is enforced. Such giving up agency etc., are relevant only with regard to acts that are actually performed. Thus Sannyasa or renouncing of this kind is established in the Sastras. The same is also called Tyaga or giving up.

Sri Krsna now explains the manner of realising that one is non-agent, by attributing all agency to God, who is the Supreme Person and the Inner Ruler, By cultivating this attitude, an aspirant can attain the renunciation of possessiveness with regard to actions and also their fruits. For it is the Supreme Person who performs all actions through the individual selves who belong to Him. The organs, bodies and Pranas of embodied beings are His. They exist for the sake of His own sport as the only purpose. Therefore, even the appeasement of hunger etc., and such other acts which affect the life of the individual souls and their works constitute only the means for accomplishing that purpose, namely, the sport of the Highest Purusa Himself. The purport of the argument is this:  The analogy of seeds producing the tree and its fruits is not applicable to the actions of release-seekers. Their actions may look like those of fruit-seekers externally. But as mentally they do not entertain any such purpose, the conseence of their actions can be ite different. The purpose served by their actions is only affording sport for the Supreme Being.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
These trividham, threefold-of three kinds; phalam, results; karmanah, of actions characterized as the righteous and the unritheous; anistam, the undesirable, consisting in (birth in) hell, (among) animals, etc.; istam, the desirable, consisting in (birth as) gods and others; and misram, the mixed, having a mixture of the desirable and the undesirable, consisting in (birht as) human beings;-these results that are of these kinds, bhavati, accrues; pretya, after death, after the fall of the body; atyaginam, to those who do not resort to renunciation, to the unilllumined, the men with rites and duties, who are not men of renunciation in the truest sense.
The derivative sense of the word phala (pha-la) is this: On accunt of being accomplished through the operation of diverse external accessories, and a result of ignorance, comparable to the charm cast by jugglery, a source of great delusion and appearing as though close to the indwelling Self, it is phalgu (unsubstantial), and as a conseence it undergoes layam (disappearance).
(The result that is of this kind accrues to those who do not resort to renunciation). Tu, but; na kvacit, never; sannyasinam, to those who resort tomonasticism for the sake of the highest Reality, to the class of monks called paramahamsas who remain steadfast in Knowledge alone. For, it cannot be that those who are devoted wholly to steadfastness in complete enlightenment do not dig out the seed of transmigration. This is the meaning.
Therefore it is only for those who have realized the supreme Truth that it is possible to become a monk who renounces actions totally, because action, accessories and results are superimmpositions on the Self through ignorance. But the renunciation of all actions is not possible for an unenlightened person who perceives the locus (the body etc.), action, agentship and accessories as the Self. Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Objection: "The absence of the fruits of actions is indeed the cessation of transmigratory existence; how is that spoken of as the fruit of abandonment?" To this he says: "abandonment...". This was explained in the verse "peace immediately follows abandonment" (Gita 12.12).

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Objection: "But then in the scriptures, actions like sacrifices are ordained as connected with fruits that are heard of, such as 'For householders, Prajapatya ceremony' (Vishnu Purana 1.6.37); therefore, in the performance of actions which are understood to be of the nature of means to those fruits, the connection with fruits in the form of desired and undesired is indeed unavoidable, just like in sowing seeds, even for one who has no desire for fruits; hence, because the fruits are contradictory to release, actions should not be performed by a seeker of release." Raising this doubt, He says: "aniṣṭam...". The undesired (anishtam) is the fruit of hell, etc.; the desired (ishtam) is the fruit of heaven, etc.; and the mixed (mishram) is sons, wealth, animals, food, etc. This threefold fruit of action accrues after death (pretya) to the non-abandoners, i.e., active persons who are devoid of the said abandonment of agency; it arises due to the sprinkling of the water of desire for actions. But for those who renounce desire, etc., and whose actions are dedicated to the Lord, no fruit contradictory to release ever accrues. Here, this opinion was shown by Sri Ramanujacharya: "Sacrifices and other actions are reconciled by virtue of the difference in application to the qualification for release, just as to the qualification for life and the qualification for desire; and their application to release is shown in passages like 'The Brahmanas desire to know Him by the study of the Vedas, by sacrifices, by gifts, by austerities conjoined with fasting' (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.4.22), etc." Therefore, thus, the renunciation of agency, fruit, desire, etc., in one's own actions performed is the scriptural sannyasa, and that itself is called tyaga.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Objection: "What is the difference in the fruit of the secondary sannyasi, who is an active person devoid of knowledge of the supreme Self but is an abandoner of the desire for the fruits of actions, and the primary sannyasi, who is a knower of the supreme Self, free from body-identification, and a renouncer of all actions? By the non-attainment of which one is called secondary, and by the attainment of which the other is called primary, since the abandonment of the fruits of actions is indeed common to both? Some other distinction must be stated." To this He says: "aniṣṭam..." (undesired...). For the non-abandoners, i.e., the ignorant, secondary sannyasis who perform actions even though they are abandoners of the fruits of actions, after death (pretya), if they die even before the purification of the mind up to the point of desire to know (vividisha), the fruit of previously performed action is indeed the assumption of a body. According to the etymology, "fruit" (phala) is that which vanishes into invisibility because of its deceptiveness and worthlessness. The word "action" (karmaṇaḥ) is in the singular denoting class, because it is impossible for a single action to have a threefold fruit. And that fruit is threefold because action is threefold: the undesired (anishta) is of the nature of vice, experienced as disagreeable, characterized by hellish birth, animal birth, etc.; the desired (ishta) is of the nature of virtue, experienced as agreeable, characterized by birth as a god, etc.; and the mixed (mishra) is of the nature of a combination of both vice and virtue, experienced as a mixture of agreeable and disagreeable, characterized by human birth. Thus He restates the threefold fruit in order to show its worthlessness. Thus, having stated that the assumption of another body is inevitable for secondary sannyasis after the fall of the body, He says: "but never to sannyasis", stating that for primary sannyasis, upon the cessation of ignorance and its effects through the realization of the supreme Self, there is indeed disembodied liberation (videha-kaivalya). The word "tu" (but) is for emphasis, meaning: for the primary sannyasis (Paramahamsa Parivrajakas) who possess the knowledge of the supreme Self, after death, the fruit of action in the form of body-assumption — undesired, desired, or mixed — never occurs indeed in any place or at any time. Because upon the destruction of ignorance by Knowledge, actions which are its effects are destroyed. And so says the Sruti: "The knot of the heart is untied, all doubts are solved, and all his actions are destroyed, when He who is high and low is seen" (Mundaka Upanishad 2.2.8). And the sage's aphorism: "On attaining That, there is non-clinging and destruction of the subsequent and previous sins, because it is so declared" (Brahma Sutras 4.1.13) shows the destruction of all actions by the knowledge of the supreme Self. By this, the distinction in the fruit is stated: transmigratory existence again for the secondary sannyasis, and release for the primary sannyasis. Here, someone says: "Because the use of the word sannyasi is seen in the case of abandoners of the fruits of action in passages like 'He who performs his bounden duty without depending on the fruits of action is a sannyasi...' (Gita 6.1), the active persons alone are taken by the word sannyasi here on account of the similarity in the abandonment of fruits. And for them, who are Sattvik, since sin is impossible due to performing obligatory duties and avoiding prohibited ones, the undesired fruit does not accrue. Nor does the desired fruit accrue due to not performing desire-driven actions and because the fruit is renounced through dedication to God. Therefore, the mixed fruit also does not accrue. Thus the threefold fruit of action does not accrue. Hence it is said: 'A seeker of release should not engage in desire-driven or prohibited actions; he should perform obligatory and occasional duties to avoid sin'." He should be told: "The limit of word and meaning has not been determined by you." To explain: the rule of language is: "Between the secondary and the primary, the comprehension is of the primary." This being the case, since the word sannyasi is primary in the sense of one who renounces all actions, and secondary in the sense of an active person on account of the similarity in the abandonment of fruits; and since the primary sense is not blocked here, the word sannyasi must refer to the former. Thus the rule of language is established. And the rule of reality is: "When the assembly of causes is present, the production of the effect takes place." To explain: even for one who has renounced the fruits of actions by dedication to God, if he performs obligatory duties for the purification of the mind and dies in the interim, who can prevent the assumption of a threefold body due to previously acquired actions? For the Sruti says: "He, O Gargi, who departs from this world without knowing this Imperishable is a miserable person" (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.8.10). Ultimately, to produce the knowledge which is the fruit of mental purification, a body qualified for that is indeed necessary for him. Therefore, for the seeker of knowledge who has renounced (vividisha-sannyasi) but dies in the interim while performing hearing (shravana), etc.—who is called "one fallen from yoga"—it is determined in the sixth chapter that the attainment of a body qualified for knowledge is indeed inevitable: "Having fallen from yoga, he is born in the house of the pure and wealthy" (Gita 6.41). When even for an ignorant person who has renounced all actions the assumption of a body is necessary, what need be said of the ignorant active person? Therefore, it is established by the rule of reality that the ignorant person must assume a body. And the theory of single birth has been refuted at length by the wise. Therefore, the explanation stated by the revered and worshipful commentator (Adi Shankara) alone is superior. The conclusion here is this: upon the cessation of ignorance of the Self through the knowledge of the identity of Brahman and the Self — which is generated by the Upanishadic declarations, is non-conceptual, has its authority determined by reflection, is free from all doubts of invalidity, and is of the nature of realization of the non-dual, true, self-luminous, supreme bliss of the non-doer and non-enjoyer Self — one is free from the conceit of agency, etc., which are its effects. Such a primary sannyasi, being pure and isolated due to the destruction of all actions, does not experience the assumption of another body caused by ignorance, action, etc., because all delusions are destroyed by the destruction of their cause. But he who is ignorant, identifies with agency, and is an embodied being, is of three kinds: first, one who performs desire-driven and prohibited actions at will due to the strength of defects like attachment, and is unqualified for the scripture of release; second, one who, due to previous merits, has somewhat reduced the defects of attachment, and being unable to renounce all actions, gives up prohibited and desire-driven actions, and performs obligatory and occasional duties without desire for fruits for the purification of the mind — he is a secondary sannyasi qualified for the scripture of release; third, one who, having attained purification of the mind through the performance of obligatory and occasional duties, has a desire to know (vividisha) arisen in him, wishes to accomplish knowledge as the means to release through hearing (shravana), etc., renounces all actions according to scriptures, and approaches a preceptor who is established in Brahman — he is called a vividisha-sannyasi. Among these, the transmigratory existence of the first is well-known to all; that of the second is explained by "undesired", etc.; and that of the third, i.e., the transmigratory existence of the ignorant person, is determined in the sixth chapter by raising the question "He who is unable to control himself but is endowed with faith..." (Gita 6.37), because the presence of the assembly of causes is certain. The distinction, however, is that for some it is unfavorable to knowledge, and for others it is favorable to knowledge. But for the wise man, due to the absence of the cause of transmigratory existence, there is indeed liberation automatically. Thus, these two subjects are aphorized in this verse.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
He states the fruit of such abandonment of the fruits of actions: "aniṣṭam..." (undesired...). Undesired, i.e., hellish state; desired, i.e., godhood; and mixed, i.e., human state — thus the threefold fruit of action which is well-known for vice, virtue, and a mixture of both respectively; all that accrues after death, in the next world, only to the non-abandoners who have desires. Because of the possibility of threefold actions in their case. But it never accrues to sannyasis. By the word sannyasi here, the present abandoners of the fruits of actions are also grasped on account of the similarity in the abandonment of fruits. Because the use of the word sannyasi is seen in the case of abandoners of the fruits of action in passages like "He who performs his bounden duty without depending on the fruits of action is a sannyasi and a yogi..." (Gita 6.1). For them, who are Sattvik, since sin is impossible and the fruit of virtue is renounced through dedication to God, even the threefold fruit of action does not accrue. Such is the meaning.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
In response to the expectation, "What is the distinct purpose of primary renunciation in comparison with secondary renunciation?", he says: Undesired (anishtam), characterized by hell, animals, etc.; desired (ishtam), characterized by gods, etc.; and mixed, which is combined of the desired and undesired, characterized by human birth — thus threefold is the fruit of action characterized by virtue and vice. By the derivation of the word "fruit" (phalam) as that which vanishes or disappears due to its worthlessness like an appearance approaching the inner Self, it is manifold because it is produced by the operation of various external factors, and it is false, like a magical illusion, created by ignorance, causing great delusion. This threefold fruit, thus, accrues after death (pretya), i.e., after the fall of the body, to the non-abandoners, namely, the ignorant active persons who are not primary renouncers. The idea is that for active persons who are free from the desire for fruits, after the fall of the body, a fruit corresponding to the sequence of accumulated (sanchita) actions, etc., is inevitable. But for sannyasis, namely, the primary renouncers (Paramahamsa Parivrajakas) who are established in pure Knowledge and whose seed of transmigratory existence consisting of ignorance, etc., is uprooted, the said fruit does not accrue in any place or at any time. Therefore, the complete renunciation of all actions is possible only for the knower of the supreme Truth who perceives that action, agent, and fruit are superimposed on the Self by ignorance, and not for the ignorant person who perceives the seat (adhishthana), etc., which are the agents of action, as the Self. As for what others explain, namely: "He states the fruit of such abandonment of the fruits of action. The threefold fruit consisting of undesired, etc., accrues after death, in the next world, only to the non-abandoners who have desires. Because of the possibility of threefold actions in their case, but it never accrues to sannyasis. By the word sannyasi, the present abandoners of the fruits of actions are grasped on account of the similarity in the abandonment of fruits. For, the use of the word sannyasi is seen in the case of abandoners of the fruits of action in passages like 'He who performs his bounden duty without depending on the fruits of action is a sannyasi and a yogi...' (Gita 6.1). For those Sattvik ones, since sin is impossible and the fruit of virtue is renounced through dedication to God, even the threefold fruit of action does not accrue"—that is not to be accepted. Because the word sannyasi is primary in the sense of the absolute renouncer who abandons all actions, and secondary in the sense of an active person on account of the similarity in abandoning fruits; and since the primary sense is not blocked here, the word sannyasi must refer to the former. Taking it in the secondary sense is due to a lack of understanding of the linguistic rule: "Between the primary and the secondary, the comprehension of the word must be in the primary sense". And also because it is rooted in the ignorance of the rule of reality: "When the assembly of causes is present, the production of the effect takes place"; for even one who has renounced the fruits of action by dedication to God, if he performs obligatory duties for the purification of the mind and dies in the interim, the production of the effect in the form of a threefold body is indeed inevitable due to the assembly of causes consisting of previously acquired actions. This is the direction.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Indeed, the establishment of Vedic actions as means to fruits is in the same manner as the process of worldly actions as means to fruits. And worldly actions produce results according to their own capacity even in the absence of desire for the fruit. Thinking that the same applies to Vedic actions, he raises the doubt: "But...". To the objection: "By virtue of the difference in application of Agnihotra, great sacrifices, etc., there is no contradiction between their being auxiliary to the stage of life (ashrama) and their being for the sake of heaven, etc. Let them not be performed for the sake of heaven, but what is the contradiction in performing them for the sake of the stage of life?", he says: "of the obligatory and occasional actions also...". By the illustration of sowing seeds, etc., the binding nature of even inadvertent actions, spoken of as "disagreeable" (Gita 18.10), is indicated. Indeed, a seed fallen on the ground by mistake does not fail to bear fruit. Therefore, for one who desires the nectar of release, this group of actions is like sowing the seeds of a poisonous tree. With this idea, he states the resulting abandonment of the form of action: "Therefore...". This threefold division into undesired, etc., is for those who do not seek release, because from the perspective of a seeker of release, even heaven, etc., is undesired. With this intent, hell, heaven, etc., are mentioned. Since fruits like rain, which are to be accomplished by Kariri sacrifice, etc., occur during one's lifetime, the word "after death" (pretya) is for illustrative purposes. Otherwise, there would be the contingency of admitting the beginning of all three fruits even for the abandoners in that very body; and since that too is blocked by the negative statement "but never to those who have renounced", with this intent he says: "subsequent to the performance of actions". Here also, the non-connection with undesired fruit must be understood as referring to inadvertent actions in the manner stated before. To the objection: "This answer to the said question is stated merely as a declaration and not with any reasoning", he says: "This is what is said...". The difference in application is to be understood by the difference in the text of application. By the difference in application, the manifoldness of fruits such as the destruction of sin is established in Jyotishtoma, etc. The remaining words: "the objection of contradictory fruits is refuted" are to be supplied. Objection: "Since it is restricted by passages like 'Knowing Him thus, one becomes immortal here; there is no other path' (Yajur Samhita 31.18), knowing (vedanam) alone is ordained as the means to release, and action is excluded. Therefore, how can there be a statement of application to the qualification for release?" To this he says: "and the application to release...". Just as in "he wishes to kill with a sword", "he wishes to go with a horse", etc., here also, by the nature of the sense, there is connection as the instrument of the meaning of the verbal root denoting desire. Therefore, there is application to release because it is an indirect means through the production of knowledge. But with the desire to speak of the immediate means, the negation is in passages like "there is no other path" (Svetasvatara Upanishad 3.8), etc. This is the idea. With the intent that He begins to answer the implication of the question in another way also, he translates the portion spoken for the sake of connection in the form of a conclusion: "Therefore...". The word "that" means because the abandonment of the form of action, etc., is Tamasik. The word "thus" means in the manner of being difficult to abandon for one who is bound by the duties of stage of life and class, etc. In this very verse, by restating the opposite of the absence of abandonment through the word sannyasa in "accrues after death to the non-abandoners, but never to the sannyasis", the doubt about the difference between tyaga and sannyasa is also refuted; this he says in: "and that itself is tyaga".

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Thus, having established the state of being an abandoner for one who renounces the fruits of actions, He now states the abandonment of the fruits of actions: "aniṣṭam..." (undesired...). The fruit of action is threefold: undesired, desired, and mixed. Among these, the undesired is of the nature of suffering such as attaining hell or births of pigs, etc.; the desired is of the nature of happiness of heaven, etc., as a celestial being; and the mixed is of the nature of enjoyment of good human birth, kingdom, etc. This threefold fruit of action accrues after death, in the next world, to the non-abandoners, i.e., those who do not renounce the fruits of actions. But it never accrues to sannyasis, i.e., those who renounce the fruits of actions. Such is the meaning.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
He states the fruit of such abandonment: "aniṣṭam...". Undesired, in the form of hell, animals, etc.; desired, in the form of gods, etc.; and mixed, in the form of human existence — thus the threefold fruit of action, i.e., of the class of actions, accrues after death (pretya), i.e., after dying, to the non-abandoners, namely, those who are devoid of the previously mentioned primary renunciation. But it never accrues to the primary sannyasis, due to their absence of the conceit of agency. Others, however, say that this non-tainting of action is indeed for the secondary sannyasis. And this was explained in the verse "yathā kāryam..." (Gita 18.9). Otherwise, there would be no distinction between primary sannyasis and secondary sannyasis. And it cannot be argued that in this way, there would arise the contingency of non-distinction between primary sannyasis and secondary sannyasis. Even though both are similar in the non-attachment of subsequent actions, there is a distinction consisting in the burning of previous actions in the case of the former, and non-burning in the case of the latter. For secondary sannyasis, even future births, etc., will occur indeed due to previous actions, like the path of one who has fallen from yoga, or as an inevitable consequence according to the illustration of the mango tree stated by Apastamba. But its principal fruit cannot be heaven, etc., because it is not intended. Such is the meaning.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
For one who is not renounced, the threefold fruits of action – desirable, undesirable and mixed – accrue after death. But those who are in the renounced order of life have no such result to suffer or enjoy.

 A person in Kṛṣṇa consciousness acting in knowledge of his relationship with Kṛṣṇa is always liberated. Therefore he does not have to enjoy or suffer the results of his acts after death.
