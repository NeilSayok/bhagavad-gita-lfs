Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Distinct from this deluded and miserable Kshetrajna Purusha, there is the pure Supreme Person untouched by the limitations of the Kshetra (field). For every reflection to exist, there is a need for a real object as the source. The status of the reflection depends on the surface of the mirror or water, etc., but the source object does not even touch that surface. For example, the reflection of the moon depends on the water in the vessel, but the moon remains situated in its own light-form. The conscious Self becomes the Kshetrajna due to the limitation (upadhi) of the Kshetra. This proves that the Self, in its true nature, is free from all limitations (upadhis). In this verse, from the perspective of scientific study and analysis, Lord Krishna indicates these two types of Purushas—the one with limitations (Sopadhika) and the one without limitations (Nirupadhika). It must be remembered here that, in truth, the Purusha is one and the same. Here, the same Supreme Self is indicated by different names. These different names are given from the perspective of the individual's mental state, i.e., the density or thinness of the veil of ignorance. In a person with complete ignorance of the nature of the Self and a mind full of attachments and aversions, the Self exists, as it were, only as a spectator (Upadrashta)—that is, it merely illuminates even the sinful acts of this person as a witness; indeed, the Self is only the spectator of the thoughts and actions of all living beings. But when that person's mind becomes somewhat purified and he engages in good deeds, then the Supreme Self becomes, as it were, the permitter (Anumanta)—that is, He gives His consent to his good deeds. With further purification of the inner instrument (antahkarana), when that person becomes aware of his divine nature, then the Lord becomes the supporter (Bharta), who fulfills his actions. In actions performed with the spirit of surrender, success is achieved by the grace of God. It appears as if God brings completeness even to the small efforts of that seeker. When that seeker, forgetting his ego, becomes completely established in yoga, then the Self appears to be the enjoyer (Bhokta) in the heart of such a person. This verse concludes with the statement that the Self indeed is the Supreme Lord (Mahesvara). He alone is the Supreme Person in this body. Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
Referring to the statement 'what it is and what its powers are' (13.4), the Lord says: 'Upadrashta' etc. Anumanta means one who approves, specifically one who observes. In the phrase 'Purusha is the experiencer of pleasure and pain', the Jiva (individual soul) is referred to. In 'Purusha and Prakriti', both the Jiva and Ishvara are spoken of together. Otherwise, it would contradict the great purport (maha-tatparya) of the scriptures. For the great purport is in the supremacy of Vishnu. As the Saukarayana Sruti says: 'The great purport of all words and all logic is in the inexpressible supremacy and greatness of Vishnu, the infinite, the highest of the high. That greatness indeed exists, there is no doubt here. Therefore, any evidence that contradicts this, whether based on perception or reasoning, is not declared as valid by the wise; nor is logic valid, for it is of inferior intellect compared to direct revelation (sruti).' Thus, denying this through logic is not proper. Hence, any logic by which one assumes the non-existence of His greatness is only a fallacy (abhasa); it is established that the greatness is always declared by the Vedas. There is also a secondary (avantara) purport there. It is said in the same text: 'The secondary purport is also in the existence of His attributes, or rather their greatness, because of their oneness in the Infinite,' and because of descriptions like being dark-complexioned (shyamatva), etc. It is proper that logic imagined by human intellect is fallacious because of the possibility of ignorance, but not the Vedas, which are self-evident pramāna. The lack of perception of His greatness is indeed possible for many due to ignorance. One should not say that there might be a contradiction in the Srutis we have not studied. For it is said in the same text: 'No words are contrary to this, no logic is contrary to this, thus spoke Prajapati, thus spoke Prajapati.' And it is said that Jiva's identity with Him, which is contrary to this, is only a fallacy (abhasa). Janamejaya said: 'O Brahman, are there many souls, or is there only one? Who is the highest Purusha here, and what is said to be the source? You should tell me this.' Sri Vaisampayana said: 'O bull of the Kuru race, they do not accept only one Purusha. Just as a single source is spoken of for many souls, so I shall describe to you that all-pervading Purusha, who is superior in qualities.' Thus in the Moksha Dharma (MBh 12.350.13). And it is not all like a dream or magic, because of the Lord's statement 'due to difference in nature, it is not like a dream' (Brahma Sutras). We do not see any proof for the universe being imagined by a single Jiva like a dream. The proofs for the contrary (i.e. reality of the world) are stated in the second chapter. It is also said in the Ayasya Shakha: 'This life is indeed called a dream due to its transience, but it is not a dream in reality, for there is no absolute cancellation of it.' This is not a defect. The identity of Ishvara and Jiva is not spoken of; rather, the Jiva's contemplation of identity with Ishvara is intended. Even that is not unconditional (nirupadhika). Therefore, there is no contradiction between identity and the reflection-status (pratibimbatva). As the Madhuchanda Sruti says: 'The sages speak of the Jiva's oneness with Vishnu as being only through reflection.' The greater fruit of the 'Ahangraha' (I am Brahman) meditation is established in the Agniveshya Sruti: 'The worshipper who contemplates Ahangraha attains proximity to His likeness, there is no doubt here.' In the Vamana Purana: 'The knowledge "I am His" is called Ahangraha.' Since the Jiva is under His control, he says 'I am He' as a servant speaks of himself as his master, but not independently. The contemplation is: 'By way of being His reflection, I am He, and I am His servant.' Thus in the Ayasya Shakha: 'I am His servant, and by way of being His reflection, I am He; thus should the Supreme Person be worshipped.' And reflection-status means similarity to Him.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
'In the manner described' means as the substratum of all projections (kalpanā) like Jiva and Ishvara. It means directly and immediately. 'As described, the beginningless and indefinable' refers to that which is the limiting condition (upādhi) for all worldly miseries. He connects this with: 'He who knows Prakriti, which is of the nature of ignorance (avidyā), along with its effects, as reduced to non-existence by the knowledge of oneness previously mentioned.' 'In whatever way' means whether performing prescribed or forbidden actions. The repetition of the word 'not' is for connection (anvaya). He states the reasoning implied by the particle 'api' (also). An objection is raised against the statement: 'He is not born again.' Objection: 'Since it is said that birth does not occur after the rise of knowledge, there should be no objection regarding the assumption of a new body.' Anticipating this, he says: 'Even though...' Still, there would be three births. The connection is: In the present body, since the fruits of actions done before and after the rise of knowledge cannot be destroyed without being experienced, two births are necessary; and even in the many past bodies, since it is remembered that 'actions not experienced do not perish,' and they do not perish without giving fruits, there is a third birth as well. He states the defect in the destruction of actions without giving fruits: 'By what is done...' He concludes: It is not proper to say that actions are destroyed without giving fruits. He says: 'Just as...' thinking that disputed actions are not destroyed without giving fruits because they are Vedic actions, like actions that have already commenced (prārabdha). The destruction is not by knowledge, is the remainder. Objection: 'Is it not proper that uncommenced actions (anārabdha) are destroyed by knowledge because their fruits have not yet begun, whereas commenced actions (prārabdha) cannot be removed by knowledge due to their strength in having already begun to yield fruit?' He answers: 'Not so.' Since both arise from ignorance, there is no difference in their opposition to knowledge, and therefore, whether fruits have begun or not is irrelevant. He states the result if actions are not destroyed without giving fruits: 'Therefore...' Objection: 'Since actions are many, why should there be only three births resulting from their fruits?' If it is because the commencing actions are of three types, he says: 'No, because they can be of a single type as they have not commenced.' To this, he says: 'Combined...' The remainder is: Knowledge does not have an absolute (aikāntika) result. If the said actions do not commence birth, he refers to the previously stated defect and shows its over-application: 'Otherwise...' 'Everywhere' means even in commenced actions. Unpredictability of fruit-generation is lack of confidence (anāśvāsa). If actions do not commence birth, another defect is the meaninglessness of the ritualistic section of scriptures (karmakānda): 'Scripture...' If uncommenced actions, even in the presence of knowledge, definitely commence another birth, he states the result: 'Hence...' He resolves this with the support of Sruti: 'Not so, etc.' He shows the Lord's agreement on the burning of uncommenced actions by knowledge: 'Here too...' The concluding statement 'abandoning all duties' (Gita 18.66) also serves as authority for the burning of all actions by knowledge: 'And he will say...' A logical reasoning can also be stated for the destruction of all uncommenced actions by knowledge: 'And by reasoning...' He explains the same: 'Ignorance...' For an ignorant person, the afflictions (kleśas) called ignorance (avidyā), egoism (asmitā), attachment (rāga), aversion (dveṣa), and clinging to life (abhiniveśa) are the seeds of all miseries. With these as cause, whatever virtuous or sinful actions are done, they initiate another birth. But for the wise person whose seeds of afflictions are burnt by knowledge, the actions are merely appearances (pratibhāsa-mātra) and do not initiate a body, just like a burnt cloth has no capacity to perform any action. He shows the Lord's agreement that actions which are mere appearances in bodies of mere perception do not initiate fruits: 'Here too...' He cites another passage as authority for the fact that after attaining truth-knowledge, apparent afflictions do not initiate a body through actions: 'Seeds...' He accepts the burning of actions performed after the rise of knowledge by knowledge: 'Let it be...' He states the reason why only those actions that are overtaken by opposition arise: 'Knowledge...' In this birth or in another birth, actions done prior to knowledge are not burnt by it because they operate without opposition: 'But not...' He refutes this: 'Not so, etc.', because it contradicts Sruti and Smriti. This is due to the hearing of the word 'all' (sarva). He doubts the limitation: 'Knowledge...' He says: 'No,' because there is no limiting factor like context (prakaraṇa). He repeats the inference stated during the objection: 'What was said...' He refutes it as invalid: 'That is incorrect.' He asks how it is invalid when pervasion (vyāpti) etc. are present: 'How?' He says the inference is invalid due to the condition (upādhi) of 'having commenced to yield fruit' which breaks the pervasion of the cause: 'Of them...' He explains the same: 'Just as...' An arrow released from the bow does not fall in the middle in the absence of a strong obstacle, so also commenced actions, in the absence of a strong obstacle, are not destroyed except by experiencing their fruits, and truth-knowledge is not such an obstacle since its power is already obstructed by the action that commenced earlier. He anticipates the doubt: 'Where there is non-burnability by knowledge, there is having commenced to yield fruit'—even if this is positive pervasion, negative pervasion 'where there is uncommenced action, there is burnability by knowledge' is not established. He says: 'That itself...' The analysis is: 'anārabdha' means the velocity that has not commenced to produce its effect. It means those residing in their own substratum, i.e., in the intellect along with the appearance of consciousness. The negative pervasion is established: 'Disputed actions are terminated by truth-knowledge because they are caused by what is terminated by it, like the rope-snake.' He concludes that since the wise person's current body falls, there is no cause for a future body, and therefore truth-knowledge has an absolute result: 'Fallen...'

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
This Purusha, by maintaining a relationship with Prakriti (body), becomes the 'spectator' (Upadrashta); by joining with it and giving consent/approval, he becomes the 'permitter' (Anumanta); by considering himself the sustainer of the body, he becomes the 'supporter' (Bharta); by experiencing happiness and sorrow in association with it, he becomes the 'enjoyer' (Bhokta); and by considering himself the master of it, he becomes the 'great Lord' (Mahesvara). But in essence, this Purusha is called the 'Supreme Self' (Paramatma). Even while residing in the body, he is transcendental to (untouched by) the body.

Commentary - Upadrastanumanta ca bharta bhokta maheśvarah - This Purusha in essence is eternal, all-pervading, stable, immovable, and everlasting (Gita 2.24). Even being so, when he casts his gaze towards Prakriti and its effect, the body, i.e., considers himself associated with them, then he is termed the 'Upadrashta' (spectator). He gives consent or approval to the execution of every action. Hence, his name is 'Anumanta' (permitter). Joining with a particular individual body and identifying with it, he maintains and nourishes the body with food, water, etc., and protects it from cold, heat, etc. Hence, his name becomes 'Bharta' (supporter). By joining with the body, he considers himself happy upon the arrival of favorable circumstances and unhappy upon the arrival of unfavorable circumstances. Hence, he is termed the 'Bhokta' (enjoyer). He considers himself the owner of the body, senses, mind, intellect, wealth, property, etc. Hence, he is addressed by the name 'Mahesvara'. Paramatmeti capyukto dehe'smin purusah parah - The Purusha is supreme, the Supreme Self, therefore in the scriptures, he is referred to by the name 'Paramatma'. Even while residing in the body, he is by nature free from association with the body. Later in the 31st verse of this chapter, it is said of him that even while residing in the body, he neither acts nor gets tainted. In this verse, the same single entity is addressed by terms like 'Upadrashta' due to association with different limiting adjuncts (upadhis); therefore, these different names should be understood as describing the nature of the Purusha himself. In reality, there is no division in him. Just as the same person is called by different names (father, uncle, grandfather, brother, etc.) according to place, time, dress, relationship, etc., similarly, the Purusha, though called by different names, is in reality one and the same. Connection - Having discussed Prakriti and Purusha from the 19th to the 22nd verse, the Lord now states the fruit of knowing both of them in truth in the next verse.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
The self existing in the body becomes the 'spectator and approver' of this body by means of the will in consonance with the functioning of the body. Likewise, It is the 'supporter' of the body, Similarly, It becomes 'experiencer' of the pleasure and pain resulting from the activities of the body. Thus, by virtue of ruling and supporting the body and by making the body completely subservient, It becomes 'the great lord' (Mahesvara) in relation to the body, the senses and the mind. Sri Krsna will further declare:  'When the lord acires the body, and when he leaves it and goes on his way, he takes these as the wind carries scents from their places' (15.8). In the body, It is said to be the 'supreme self' in relation to the body, the senses and the mind. The word 'self' (Atman) is applied to the body and the mind subseently. It is said afterwards:  'Some perceive the self by means of the self through meditation' (13.24). The particle 'also' (api) indicates that the self is the 'supreme lord'?  in relation to the body just as It is the supreme self. The supremacy of the self has been described in the text beginning with 'It is the beginningless brahman having Me for the Highest' (13.12). It is true that the self (in Its emancipated state) has limitless power knowledge. But It becomes the great lord and the supreme self only in relation to the body. Such lordship and supremacy is the result of attachment to the Gunas arising from the beginningless conjunction with Prakrti.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
He who is the upadrasta, Witness, who while staying nearby does not Himself become involved: As when the priests and the performer of a sacrifice remain engaged in duties connected with the sacrifice, there is another (called Brahma) remaining nearby who is unengaged, is versed in the science of sacrifices and witnesses the merit or demerit of the activities of the priest and the performer of the sacrifice, similarly, He who is not engaged in the activities of and is different from the body and organs, who has characteristics other than theirs, and is the proximate (upa) observer (drasta) of the body and organs engaged in their duties, is the upa-drasta. 
Or: The observers are the body, eyes, mind, intellect and the soul. Of them the body is the external observer. Proceeding inwards from that (body), the Self is the inmost as also the proximate observer, compared with which there is no other higher and inner observer. The Self, because of being the most proximate observer, is the upadrasta. Or, It is the upadrasta since, like the non-looker of a sarifice, It witness everything.
And He is the anu-manta, Permitter: Anumananam, approval, means satisfaction with those performers (viz body and organs) as also their perfomances. The agent of that (approval) is the anumanta. Or, He is the anumanta since, even though Himself not engaged in the activities of the body and organs, He appears to be favourably disposed towards and engaged in them. Or, He is the anumanta because, when the body and organs are engaged in their own functions, He remains as a witness and never dissuades them.
It is the bharta, Sustainer: Bharanam means the continuance in their own state of the body, organs, mind and intellect, which reflect consciousness and have become aggregated owing to the need of serving the purpose [Viz enjoyment, or Liberation.-Tr.] of some other entity, viz the conscious Self. And that (continuance) is verily due to the consciousness that is the Self. In this sense the Self is said to be the Sustainer.
It is the bhokta, Experiencer: As heat is by fire, similarly, the experiences of the intellect-in the form of happiness, sorrow and delusion in relation to all objects-, when born as though permeated by the consciousness that is the Self, are manifested differently by the Self which is of the nature of eternal Consciousness. In this sense the Self is said to be the Experiencer.
He is maheswarah, the great God, because, as the Self of all and independent, He is the great Ruler.
He is paramatma, the transcendental Self, because He is the Self which has the characteristics of being the supreme Witness etc. of (all) those-beginning from the body and ending with the intellect-which are imagined through ignorance to be the indwelling Self. He is api ca, also; uktah, spoken of, referred to, in the Upanisads; iti, as, with the words; 'He is the indwelling One, the paramatma, the transcendental Self.' [Ast reads atah in place of antah. So the translation of the sentence will be: Therefore He is also referred to as the transcendental Self in the Upanisads.-Tr.] Where is He? The parah, suprem; purusah, Person, who is higher than the Unmanifest and who will be spoken of in, 'But different is the supreme Person who is spoken of as the transcendental Self' (15.17); is asmin, in this; dehe, body.
What has been presented in, '৷৷.also understand Me to be the Knower of the field' (2), has been explained and conclude.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Answering the question: 'By whom is the statement "what it is and what its powers are" (13.4) spoken?', the Lord says: 'yatashca' etc. 'Thus promised' is the remainder. Some explain 'Anumanta' as the permitter. That is incorrect, because it would contradict the descriptions like supporter, enjoyer, etc., which would conflict with complete indifference; with this intention, he explains: 'Anumanta' etc. The word 'anu' is repeated to show repetition of action, and that it indicates this. 'Specifically' is the meaning of the suffix. He is the observer, the examiner of what should be done. To explain the word 'Purusha' in 'He who knows this Purusha', he introduces it with: 'Purusha' etc. The idea is that it is due to the characteristic of experiencing pleasure and pain. In the explanation that He is the cause of experiencing pleasure and pain, this is what is said; whereas in another explanation, the Lord alone is the Purusha here, and the Jiva is referred to in 'Purusha existing in Prakriti' (13.22). This is illustrative; it should also be observed that the Lord is spoken of as the 'Upadrashta'. Then what? He says: 'Purusha' etc. Since both are thus in Prakriti, therefore in 'He who knows this Purusha' (13.24), both Jiva and Ishvara are grasped by the word Purusha, as both are referred to. If so, dual number should have been used; to this, he says: 'sahaiva' (together), meaning in a combined form. Here, someone [Shankaracharya] has explained the identity of Jiva and Ishvara in 'He who knows this Purusha' by inserting 'I am this' directly; he refutes this: 'elsewhere' etc. If there is identity between Jiva and Ishvara, it would contradict the fact that all Vedas primarily teach His supremacy. How? He says: 'for the great purport...' For the great purport of all scriptures is indeed in the absolute supremacy of the Lord. Why? He states it from the previously mentioned reasoning and Sruti: 'likewise' etc. Due to Vishnu's greatness, when His inexpressible supremacy is spoken of, the great purport of all Vedic words and all logic—meaning the Mimamsa scripture which systematicises logic—is in Him. Great purport means the primary intent.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Furthermore, 'Upadrashta' etc. In this body, which is like a tree, 'Two birds, inseparable companions, dwell on the same tree. One of them eats the sweet fruit, while the other looks on without eating' (Rig Veda 2.3.17.5, Mundaka Upanisad 3.1.1, Svetasvatara Upanisad 4.6). According to the unified meaning of this mantra, the Lord describes the other, the Supreme Self, the indwelling Person, who is unattached. Therefore, the terms 'Paramatma' and 'Parah Purushah' (Supreme Person) are given. He is also spoken of as the 'Upadrashta' (witness), who is in reality free from attachment to the body, organs, and experience, and is Himself the primary enjoyer and agent, as per the statement 'The Lord of six qualities experiences the sixfold group.' Though unknown, He is the permitter (Anumanta), the great Lord (Mahesvara), the ruler, and the prompter, who dwells in this body; yet He is untainted because of not eating (remaining detached). But the individual soul (pranabhrt) is tainted by eating (experiencing). He who knows the Purusha existing in this body in this two-fold manner—who is in reality of imperishable power, the knower of the field, a conscious part of Brahman, of the nature of a swan (pure), distinct in essence, and free from qualities—and who knows Prakriti with its Gunas, such a person, even though living amidst the Gunas, is not born again; he does not experience the transmigratory cycle of the Gunas but is liberated.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Thus, it has been said that the transmigratory state of the Purusha is due to the false identification with Prakriti, and not in his true nature. Now, in response to the query: 'What indeed is his true nature in which transmigration does not occur?', the Lord directly indicates his nature and says 'Upadrashta' etc. Even though residing in this body, which is a modification of Prakriti, in the form of a Jiva, the Purusha is 'parah'—untouched by the Gunas of Prakriti, and in reality non-transmigratory in his own nature. Because He is the 'Upadrashta' (witness). Just as when the priests and the sacrificer are engaged in the sacrificial rites, another person who is nearby, himself unengaged but skilled in the science of sacrifice, observes the merits and defects of the activities of the priests and the sacrificer; similarly, the Purusha is unengaged in the activities of the body and senses, is distinct from them, and acts as a spectator situated near the body and senses along with their activities, but is not the agent. As the Sruti says: 'Whatever he sees there, he is untouched by it, for this Purusha is unattached.' Or, among the body, eyes, mind, intellect, and the Self, which are objects of perception, the Purusha is the 'Upadrashta' because He is the inmost Self, most proximate compared to the external body, etc. The prefix 'upa' denotes proximity, which culminates in the inmost Self since there is no mediation. He is also the 'Anumanta' (permitter) because, though Himself not acting, He appears to favor and engage in the activities of the body and organs by His mere presence. Or, He is the permitter because, being the witness, He never prevents the body and organs when they are engaged in their own functions. As the Sruti says: 'He is the permitter and the witness.' He is the 'Bharta' (sustainer and nourisher) of the aggregate of body, senses, mind, and intellect, which are superimposed with consciousness, by supporting them with His own existence and illumination. He is the 'Bhokta' (experiencer) in the sense that He is the changeless perceiver who illumines the modifications of the intellect—which are of the nature of pleasure, pain, and delusion—with His own conscious essence. He is 'Mahesvarah' because He is the great Lord, being the Self of all and independent. He is 'Paramatma' (the Supreme Self)—the supreme Self characterized by the aforementioned attributes like being the witness, etc., compared to the insentient body, etc., up to the intellect, which are imagined through ignorance to be the Self. He is also spoken of in the Srutis by this term. By the word 'ca' (and), He is also referred to by terms like Upadrashta, etc. He is the supreme (parah), highest Person, as will also be stated later in: 'But different is the supreme Person who is spoken of as the transcendental Self' (Gita 15.17).

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
In this manner, showing that the transmigration of the Purusha is due to the non-discrimination from Prakriti and not in essence, the Lord states his true nature in the words: 'Upadrashta' etc. Even though residing in this body which is an effect of Prakriti, the Purusha is 'parah'—distinct indeed, and is not joined with its attributes. The reasons for this are: because He is the 'Upadrashta'—remaining separate, He is the observer situated nearby; and He is the 'Anumanta'—the permitter who favors by His mere presence, as per the Sruti: 'He is the witness, the knower, unique, and free from attributes.' Similarly, in His divine form, He is called the 'Bharta'—the supporter who holds together; and the 'Bhokta'—the protector (nourisher); and He is called 'Mahesvarah'—the great Ruler, the lord of Brahma and others; and He is called 'Paramatma'—the indwelling Ruler, by the Sruti. As the Sruti says: 'He is the lord of all beings, He is the ruler of the worlds, He is the protector of the worlds' etc.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
The Lord directly indicates the Purusha: 'Upadrashta' etc. Just as when priests and sacrificers are engaged in sacrificial rites, another person who is neutral, unengaged, and skilled in the science of sacrifice is the observer of the merits and defects of their activities; similarly, the Purusha, while remaining near the active body and senses, is Himself unengaged, acting as a spectator and witness by His mere presence. Or, the body, eyes, mind, intellect, and Self are all seers; of these, the body is the external seer, and proceeding inwards, the Self is the inmost and nearest seer, beyond which there is no other seer; because of being the most proximate seer, It is the 'Upadrashta'. Or, like the spectator of a sacrifice, It is the 'Upadrashta' because of experiencing everything. When others are active and performing actions, the approval or satisfaction of the bystander is 'anumanana' (permitting); He who does this by His mere presence is the 'Anumanta'. Or, He is the permitter because, though Himself not active, He appears to be active and favorable to the activities of the body and organs. Or, He is the permitter because He, being their witness, never prevents them when they are active in their own functions. The Self is called the 'Bharta' (supporter) because the maintenance of the form of the aggregate of body, senses, mind, and intellect—which reflect consciousness and serve the purpose of the conscious Self—is due to the conscious Self. The Self is called the 'Bhokta' (experiencer) because He is the distinct Knower who experiences the modifications of the intellect—which are of the nature of happiness, sorrow, and delusion in relation to all objects—through His nature of eternal consciousness. He is 'Mahesvarah' because He is the great Ruler, being the Self of all and independent. He is 'Paramatma' (the Supreme Self)—the supreme Self characterized by the attributes of being the witness, etc., compared to the body, etc., up to the intellect, which are imagined through ignorance to be the indwelling Self; He is spoken of in the Sruti by this term. The Paramatma who is spoken of in the Sruti is Himself the 'Purusha parah' (supreme Person) in this body, who is superior to the Unmanifest, and who will be described later as: 'But different is the supreme Person who is spoken of as the transcendental Self' (Gita 15.17). The Srutis say: 'He is the witness, the knower, unique, and free from attributes', 'He is the lord of all beings, He is the ruler of the worlds, He is the protector of the worlds', 'He is the ruler of the past and the future', 'Having created that, He entered into it', 'Entering with this living Jiva, I shall manifest names and forms', 'The Purusha is superior to the Unmanifest', etc.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
In the verse 'Upadrashta' etc., the Lord states the nature of the Purusha immediately after the birth in good and evil wombs. By this verse, He is the spectator, the one who shows the objects of sight by means of proof, etc. 'Upadrashta' means one who, though not Himself active, observes the active one. Saying that the word 'Upadrashta' is used here due to similarity to that, he says: 'by means of the will in consonance with the functioning of the body.' By the word 'etc.', prompting (prerana) is included. Or, thinking of the distinction that the prompter of the active one is the 'Anumanta', the word 'etc.' is used. Since being the spectator, permitter, etc. depends on a correlative, the body, although present as the location, also deserves to be connected as the correlative; with this intention, he says 'of the body'. To show the division of the characteristics of the Self in relation to the body, such as being the ruler, sustainer, and master, the spectator-status and permitter-status in relation to activity are unified in one sentence, and then the sentence is divided as 'likewise' etc. 'Bharta' means the supporter. He says 'thus' to show that His being 'Mahesvara' is only in relation to the nearby body, etc., due to the stated reason. By saying that He is the experiencer of the fruits born of the body, the master-status (seshitva) of the embodied one is implied, so he says: 'and by being the master of the body'. To show the use of the word 'Isvara' (lord) in a limited sense only for the Jiva who undergoes birth and death due to karma, he says: 'likewise'. To justify the use of the word 'Paramatma' for the Jiva itself by pervading it through knowledge, he shows the connection with the nearby limiting factor (body) by saying: 'in this body'. He thinks that if it referred to the Supreme Self (Paramatman) directly, the phrase 'in this body' would be inappropriate. Here, 'body, senses, and mind'—the separate mention of the mind is to show that it is also a subject for the use of the word 'Self' (Atman). To answer the objection that the word Jiva is inappropriate because there is no 'supreme' (param) word to distinguish it, he says: 'in the body and mind'. This usage is not to be searched for in other texts, but rather within this very context, separated by only one verse. Therefore, to show that the proximity of the distinguished thing regulates its meaning, he says: 'immediately after'. He says 'by the word api (also)' that the word 'Mahesvara', like 'Paramatma', is understood to have a limited scope restricted by the nearby limiting factor, as indicated by the word 'api' which goes beyond the word 'ca' (and). By the word 'uktah' (is called), it is indicated that this relative term 'Paramatma' etc. is applied to the non-paramatman (Jiva). In the phrase 'purushah parah' (supreme Person), the word 'parah' also has a restricted meaning as before, which is supported by the mention of the body and the relative lord-status; showing the division of the subject and predicate in the verse, and the summarized meaning, he says: 'beginningless'. The statement 'He is the beginningless Brahman having Me for the Highest' (13.12) shows the absence of absolute supremacy. The phrase 'limitless power of knowledge' shows only relative supremacy. By the word 'this', the verse 'Upadrashta' falling in the middle is also shown to refer to the Jiva, thereby demonstrating that the context relates to the Jiva. By 'in this body', the previously stated reason is intended, with which intention he says: 'arising from the beginningless conjunction with Prakrti, which results in attachment to the Gunas'.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Thus, having stated the desire for experiencing bliss (rasa-bhoga) as the cause, a doubt arises: when the Jiva, who is of the nature of ignorance and is a tiny part entered into the body etc., is seen experiencing, how does the Purusha experience, and how does that cause the transmigration of the Jiva? Resolving this, the Lord says: 'Upadrashta' etc. The supreme Purusha, Purushottama, in this body is the 'Upadrashta'—the witness who sees from close proximity; and He is the 'Anumanta'—the permitter (approver); the 'Bharta'—the supporter (maintainer); the 'Bhokta'—the enjoyer (protector); and He is 'Mahesvarah'—the great Lord. Similarly, He is the 'Paramatma'. By the word 'ca' (and), the life-force (prana), Jiva, etc., are also spoken of. This is the idea: He is the witness of the one who experiences the enjoyment, which is used for the service of the Lord after surrendering everything like the body to Him, as His prasada—that is, He causes its utility in the primary service of the Lord. Similarly, He approves (rejoices) after the surrender is made. Similarly, He is the 'Bharta'—meaning the supporter and nourisher as a husband. Similarly, as the enjoyer, He is the protector through the knowledge of ownership. Similarly, He is 'Mahesvara'—the lord of the agents of actions like Brahma and others; meaning He is the creator of such things. Similarly, He is 'Paramatma'—meaning He is like a friend to one who possesses such attributes.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
The knower of the field and his power were promised to be explained in 'what it is and what its powers are' (13.4). Of these, the knower of the field has already been described; now his power (influence) is stated in: 'Upadrashta' etc. Previously, it was said that attachment to the Gunas is the cause of birth. That attachment is of four kinds: either by denying the Purusha and giving dominance to the Gunas alone, or by keeping the Purusha secondary and giving dominance to the Gunas, or by giving equal dominance to the Gunas and the Purusha, or by making the Gunas secondary. In the first case, seeing the aggregate of Gunas in the form of body, senses, and mind as the Self, one becomes the enjoyer (bhokta), as do the Charvakas, etc. In the second case, due to the dominance of the Gunas, one assumes real agency in the Self and becomes the accumulator and supporter (bharta) of the fruits of actions, as do the Logicians (Tarkikas), etc. In the third case, due to the equal dominance of the Gunas and the Self, one approves (anumanyate) the experience residing in the Gunas in the unattached Self as well, like a mark of marking-nut on a cloth, as do the Samkhyas. In the fourth case, not seeing any transmission of the attributes of the Gunas to the Self, one remains a spectator (upadrashta) observing the movements of the Gunas in the form of indifferent consciousness, as does our Witness (Sakshi). Among these four types of attachment to the Gunas, the Upadrashta is the best, the Anumanta is middle, the Bharta is low, and the Bhokta is the lowest of all. When he plays by controlling the Gunas, he is called Mahesvara. He who is the creator, sustainer, and destroyer of the universe, the Lord, the indwelling Ruler, is also spoken of as the Paramatma when He exists leaving aside the Gunas. Although the Upadrashta also remains leaving aside the Gunas, yet the one limited by a single aggregate does not observe the activities of other aggregates; whereas this one (Paramatma) observes the activities of all aggregates, and being supreme over all, He is the supreme Self. The Lord will speak of Him as: 'But different is the supreme Person who is spoken of as the transcendental Self, who entering the three worlds, sustains them as the imperishable Lord' (15.17). These two are also related to the Gunas. Thus, the one Self existing in this body, who is supreme and beyond the Gunas, remains by dissolving the Gunas in Himself as the undivided single essence, and becomes six-fold due to association with the Gunas. This indeed is His power. Among these, He is bound by the three forms of Anumanta, Bharta, and Bhokta; whereas in the forms of Upadrashta, Mahesvara, and Paramatma, He is always liberated and one without a second. The commentary's meaning should also be considered here, but is not shown due to fear of length.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Yet in this body there is another, a transcendental enjoyer, who is the Lord, the supreme proprietor, who exists as the overseer and permitter, and who is known as the Supersoul.

 It is stated here that the Supersoul, who is always with the individual soul, is the representation of the Supreme Lord. He is not an ordinary living entity. Because the monist philosophers take the knower of the body to be one, they think that there is no difference between the Supersoul and the individual soul. To clarify this, the Lord says that He is represented as the Paramātmā in every body. He is different from the individual soul; He is para, transcendental. The individual soul enjoys the activities of a particular field, but the Supersoul is present not as finite enjoyer nor as one taking part in bodily activities, but as the witness, overseer, permitter and supreme enjoyer. His name is Paramātmā, not ātmā, and He is transcendental. It is distinctly clear that the ātmā and Paramātmā are different. The Supersoul, the Paramātmā, has legs and hands everywhere, but the individual soul does not. And because the Paramātmā is the Supreme Lord, He is present within to sanction the individual soul’s desiring material enjoyment. Without the sanction of the Supreme Soul, the individual soul cannot do anything. The individual is bhukta, or the sustained, and the Lord is bhoktā, or the maintainer. There are innumerable living entities, and He is staying in them as a friend. The fact is that every individual living entity is eternally part and parcel of the Supreme Lord, and both of them are very intimately related as friends. But the living entity has the tendency to reject the sanction of the Supreme Lord and act independently in an attempt to dominate nature, and because he has this tendency he is called the marginal energy of the Supreme Lord. The living entity can be situated either in the material energy or in the spiritual energy. As long as he is conditioned by the material energy, the Supreme Lord, as his friend, the Supersoul, stays with him just to get him to return to the spiritual energy. The Lord is always eager to take him back to the spiritual energy, but due to his minute independence the individual entity is continually rejecting the association of spiritual light. This misuse of independence is the cause of his material strife in the conditioned nature. The Lord, therefore, is always giving instruction from within and from without. From without He gives instructions as stated in Bhagavad-gītā , and from within He tries to convince the living entity that his activities in the material field are not conducive to real happiness. “Just give it up and turn your faith toward Me. Then you will be happy,” He says. Thus the intelligent person who places his faith in the Paramātmā or the Supreme Personality of Godhead begins to advance toward a blissful eternal life of knowledge.
