Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Completeness is experienced as the Self, not as the perceived object. Hindu seers unanimously agree that turning inward and reflecting upon the Self is the direct path to Self-realisation and its immediate experience. In this chapter, the Self and its adjuncts (upadhis) are demarcated in a beautiful philosophical framework. The understanding born of discrimination between the Self and the non-Self (Atma-anatma viveka) alone will show the seeker how, from the standpoint of Absolute Truth, the inert non-Self is completely non-existent.

Just as a waking man, through a particular state of mind, becomes a dreamer, and as long as the dream lasts, it appears absolute truth to that dreamer; but upon waking, that dream disappears, and the waking man knows that the dream was merely a delusion of his own mind. Similarly, in the real awakening of Self-realisation, this perceived world of plurality disappears. The seeker experiences the Self through his own true nature, wherein this shadow-like world has no existence whatsoever.

Thus, considering according to Vedanta philosophy, it is seen that all living beings are composed of two principles. One principle is inert/insentient (jada-achetan) and the other is the sentient Consciousness (chetan). In the present verse, both of these are defined.

THIS BODY IS CALLED THE FIELD — In this mechanical age, it is easy to understand that energy requires a suitable field to express itself or to do work. Only then can it express itself to serve humanity. Steam power without an engine and electricity without a fan cannot grant us motion and a gentle breeze respectively. Similarly, those adjuncts such as the body through which Consciousness expresses itself are here called the 'Field' (Kshetra).

HE WHO KNOWS THIS IS CALLED THE KNOWER OF THE FIELD — This field is made of inert matter. Nevertheless, due to the expression of Consciousness within it, it functions and perceives objects. In reality, that conscious Principle which, being expressed through these adjuncts, illumines all objects is the 'Knower of the Field' (Kshetrajna). In scriptural terminology, Consciousness conditioned by adjuncts (upadhi-avacchinna chaitanya) is called the Kshetrajna or Jiva.

As long as the Jiva inhabits the body, its presence is clearly known by the tendency to know (perceive). The degree of this inquiring tendency may vary in different individuals, but its expression itself is regarded by us as the mark of life. A creature's capacity to perceive objects and express responses to them constitutes the functioning of life; and when this knower departs after leaving the body, we declare that body to be dead. This knower alone is the Kshetrajna.

TADVIDAH (THE SEERS OF TRUTH) — Here, Lord Krishna assures us that these definitions of Kshetra and Kshetrajna are not His arbitrary declarations nor merely speculative assumptions; rather, they have been certified by the Brahman-established seers themselves. In short, the entire inert universe is Kshetra, and the Consciousness-nature Self is called Kshetrajna.

Is this all that needs to be known on this topic? No, listen further.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
In order to establish that perceived qualities like sorrow and distinction which persist as long as the body exists belong to the non-Self, Sri Anandagiri explains that after describing the Seer (Self) as distinct from the body, in order to avoid the view of the Sankhyas that mere distinction from body brings liberation, the Lord declares the oneness of the knower of the field in all bodies and its identity with the Supreme Imperishable Reality. The words 'idam' etc. indicate the Seer extracted from the perceived body. The particles 'cha' and 'api' imply that the knower of the field in all bodies is non-different from the Supreme Lord Himelf. Through discrimination between Kshetra and Kshetrajna, the delusion of embodiment is dispelled and the non-dual Self is realized.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
The Supreme Lord said — O son of Kunti, Arjuna! This body referred to as 'this' is called the 'field' (Kshetra), and he who knows it is called the 'knower of the field' (Kshetrajna) by those who know them.

Commentary:
1. 'Idam shariram kaunteya kshetram ity abhidhiyate' — The body which is experienced as 'this' (an object of perception) is called Kshetra (the field). Just as a farmer sows seeds in a field and reaps the crop, similarly in this body, the seeds of actions (karmas) are sown and their fruits of pleasure and pain are reaped. The body is an object of knowledge, changing, perishable, and insentient.

2. 'Etad yo vetti tam prahuh kshetrajna iti tadvidah' — He who knows this body as an object, distinct from himself, is called the Kshetrajna (knower of the field). The knower is the conscious Self, unchanging, eternal, and distinct from the body. Those who have realized the true nature of both Kshetra and Kshetrajna are called 'tadvidah' (the seers of truth). The body belongs to the realm of Matter (Prakriti), while the knower belongs to the realm of Spirit (Purusha).

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
The body which is cognised in identity with the experiencing self by co-ordinate predication (Samanadhikaranya) in the propositions, 'I am a god, 'I am a man,' 'I am fat,' 'I am slender' etc., is described by those who know the real nature of the body as only the Field (Ksetra) of experience for the experiencing self, who is distinct from the body. Those who know this, namely, those who know the exact nature of the self, call It the Field-knower (Ksetrajna). That knower who knows the body, as divided into its different members and as their collectivity, can say 'I know it, the body, as an object.' The person with this perception is the one who is called the Ksetrajna or the Field-knower, who must necessarily be different from the Field (Ksetra), which is the object of this knowledge.

It is true that at the time of perceiving an object like a pot which is different from one's body, the seer who thinks 'I am a god who sees it' or 'I am a man who sees it' etc., is putting himself as identical with the body through co-ordinate predication. In the same way he experiences the body as an object of knowledge when he says 'I know this body.' Thus if the body is an object of knowledge, it must be different from the knowing self. Therefore, the Field-knower (Ksetrajna). The knower, is other than the body which is an object of knowledge like a jar, etc.

But this knowledge which arises by way of co-ordinate predication is justified on the ground that the body is inseparable from oneself; for it constitutes an attribute of the self like 'cow-ness' of the cow etc. The knowing self is however unie in being an eternal and subtle form of knowledge. But this is inaccessible to the ordinary man's organs of vision; it is accessible only to a mind refined by Yoga. The ignorant see the knower only in the form of Prakrti because of close proximity to or union with Prakrti. Sri Krsna thus declares later on:  'When in identiciation with the Gunas he departs or stays or experiences, the deluded perceive him not. They, who have the eye of knowledge, see' (15.10).

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
Showing the connection with the previous chapter, Sri Jayatirtha explains the topic of this chapter as 'purvokta...'. That knowledge and means of knowledge described up to the end of the sixth chapter, the object of knowledge (Brahman's nature) explained in the six chapters starting from the seventh, the field (kshetra) declared in verses like 'bhumir apo 'nalo vayuh' (7.4), and the distinction of purushas stated in verses like 'na tv evaham jatu nasam' (2.12) — all these are presented by the Supreme Lord in this chapter. For what purpose? 'Pindikritya', that is, gathering together the topics scattered earlier. Here the suffix ktva is used in a secondary sense meaning for the purpose of synthesizing. Since mere synthesis might cause confusion due to mixing, it is said 'vivichya', meaning by analyzing distinctly for clarity of the subject.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Just as what was previously declared in a sutra-like concise manner and elaborated in the second hexad like a commentary (vritti), now in the third hexad, the Bhashya containing the complete conclusive determination is presented. (1) The two natures (prakritis) previously explained through the discrimination of higher (para) and lower (apara) — where the higher nature along with its accessories is the Kshetra (field), and the lower nature as consciousness is the Kshetrajna (knower of the field) — are knowable as originating from the Aksara (Imperishable), along with knowledge as the means. Expounding this, the Blessed Lord said: 'Idam' (this). Otherwise, an explanation in the middle without a question would not be fitting. Here, some write an additional verse in the original text, 'prakritim purusam chaiva' (13.1), but that is to be disregarded as inappropriate and unprompted, since this third hexad is an elaboration of what was stated earlier. Indeed, following the principle 'as in the microcosm, so in the macrocosm', this body, which is a modification of the non-self prakriti mentioned earlier, is called 'Kshetra' because it is the dwelling place or means of living for the Purusha. When the Purusha does not know this distinctly, he becomes a samsari (embodied soul) merely through identifying as the owner of the field. Whoever knows this distinctly — knowing the field and knowing oneself as the witness-seer — him the experts call 'Kshetrajna'. Although knowledge of the field and field-knower exists even in the worldly state, it is not distinct, but rather identified with the field. Nevertheless, the object of enjoyment is inherently different from the enjoying self, as seen in a dead body. Furthermore, this occurs when there is a relation between the two, not in isolation (kaivalya). The fact that the knower is different from the object known is self-evident, just as a seer is different from a pot. Only the ignorant think 'I am a deity', treating it as an inseparable attribute of the self. But this realization of distinction belongs to the wise person who knows himself distinctly as the Kshetrajna, not to everyone.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
'Let yogis behold through a mind controlled by practice of meditation that attributeless, actionless Supreme Light if they will; but for us, let that sapphire radiance running on the banks of Yamuna (Sri Krishna) be the eternal wonder of our eyes.' Having explained the meanings of the terms 'tat' (That) and 'tvam' (Thou) in the first and middle hexads, the final hexad now commences, focusing on the ultimate sentence-meaning and right knowledge. The Lord previously promised: 'I become their deliverer from the ocean of mortal existence.' Deliverance from mortal existence is impossible without Self-knowledge. Hence, that Self-knowledge by which mortal existence ceases and endowed with which renunciates possess virtues like non-hatred must be expounded. That knowledge reveals the non-difference of the Jiva with the non-dual Supreme Self, since the delusion of duality is the root of all misery. To the objection how Jivas residing in different fields can be non-different from the single Supreme Lord, it is replied that embodiment and diversity are attributes of the non-Self imagined by ignorance (avidya). Therefore, to establish that the Kshetrajna Purusha (individual self) is one and changeless in every field, distinct from body, senses and mind, the discrimination between Kshetra and Kshetrajna is undertaken in this chapter. Elaborating on the lower and higher natures described in Chapter 7, the Lord says: 'Idam'. O son of Kunti, this body, the abode of enjoyment together with senses and mind, is called Kshetra (field) because like a crop field, fruits of actions are harvested here repeatedly. He who knows it with the sense of 'I and mine' is called Kshetrajna (knower of the field) by those who understand the discrimination between field and field-knower.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Now, to state the contextual relation of the third hexad (chapters 13-18), Vedantadeshika summarizes the contents of the first and second hexads. The disciplines of Knowledge (Jnana) and Action (Karma), purified through focus on Yoga for the attainment of Self-realization, were expounded in the first hexad. In the second hexad, worship and devotion to the Supreme Being Vasudeva were declared. Now in the third hexad, the exact natures of Kshetra, Kshetrajna, and Ishvara, the discrimination between Prakriti and Purusha, and the auxiliaries of devotion are systematically determined. The Lord says: 'idam shariram kaunteya kshetram ity abhidhiyate'. This body in all forms (deity, human, etc.) is distinct from the conscious Self who exists as the knower. The body is called 'Kshetra' (field) because it is the abode for reaping the fruits of past actions. He who knows this field as distinct from himself is called 'Kshetrajna'. Both Kshetra and Kshetrajna constitute the body/attributes of the Supreme Lord (Ishvara), and hence are described in coordinate predication with Him.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Objection: In Chapter 2, the nature of the individual self ('tvam' padartha) was declared as unmanifest, unthinkable, eternal and all-pervading. In Chapter 12, the nature of the Supreme Self ('tat' padartha) was similarly declared with identical characteristics. Since their characteristics are identical, there can be no real difference between Jiva and Ishvara. Why then speak of distinction? Answer: Although in the empirical state of ignorance (avidya), a distinction between ruler and ruled is experienced, in the state of ultimate truth, non-difference alone prevails. To prepare the seeker for this identity, the Lord distinguishes the Seer from the body: 'Idam'. O son of Kunti, this body from egoism down to the physical sheath is called 'Kshetra' (field) because it wastes away through ignorance, is protected through knowledge, and is the germinating field of action-seeds. That Conscious Self who illumines/knows this field is called 'Kshetrajna' by the knowers of truth.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: O son of Kunti, this body is called the field, and one who knows this body is called the knower of the field.

 Arjuna was inquisitive about prakṛti (nature), puruṣa (the enjoyer), kṣetra (the field), kṣetra-jña (its knower), and knowledge and the object of knowledge. When he inquired about all these, Kṛṣṇa said that this body is called the field and that one who knows this body is called the knower of the field. This body is the field of activity for the conditioned soul. The conditioned soul is entrapped in material existence, and he attempts to lord it over material nature. And so, according to his capacity to dominate material nature, he gets a field of activity. That field of activity is the body. And what is the body? The body is made of senses. The conditioned soul wants to enjoy sense gratification, and, according to his capacity to enjoy sense gratification, he is offered a body, or field of activity. Therefore the body is called kṣetra, or the field of activity for the conditioned soul. Now, the person, who should not identify himself with the body, is called kṣetra-jña, the knower of the field. It is not very difficult to understand the difference between the field and its knower, the body and the knower of the body. Any person can consider that from childhood to old age he undergoes so many changes of body and yet is still one person, remaining. Thus there is a difference between the knower of the field of activities and the actual field of activities. A living conditioned soul can thus understand that he is different from the body. It is described in the beginning – dehino ’smin – that the living entity is within the body and that the body is changing from childhood to boyhood and from boyhood to youth and from youth to old age, and the person who owns the body knows that the body is changing. The owner is distinctly kṣetra-jña. Sometimes we think, “I am happy,” “I am a man,” “I am a woman,” “I am a dog,” “I am a cat.” These are the bodily designations of the knower. But the knower is different from the body. Although we may use many articles – our clothes, etc. – we know that we are different from the things used. Similarly, we also understand by a little contemplation that we are different from the body. I or you or anyone else who owns the body is called kṣetra-jña, the knower of the field of activities, and the body is called kṣetra, the field of activities itself. In the first six chapters of Bhagavad-gītā the knower of the body (the living entity) and the position by which he can understand the Supreme Lord are described. In the middle six chapters of the Bhagavad-gītā the Supreme Personality of Godhead and the relationship between the individual soul and the Supersoul in regard to devotional service are described. The superior position of the Supreme Personality of Godhead and the subordinate position of the individual soul are definitely defined in these chapters. The living entities are subordinate under all circumstances, but in their forgetfulness they are suffering. When enlightened by pious activities, they approach the Supreme Lord in different capacities – as the distressed, those in want of money, the inquisitive, and those in search of knowledge. That is also described. Now, starting with the Thirteenth Chapter, how the living entity comes into contact with material nature and how he is delivered by the Supreme Lord through the different methods of fruitive activities, cultivation of knowledge, and the discharge of devotional service are explained. Although the living entity is completely different from the material body, he somehow becomes related. This also is explained.
