Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Completely disconnected from the intellect, his bewildered mind runs hither and thither like a madman's, arriving at foolish conclusions. He says: I desire neither victory, nor kingdom, nor pleasure. It is a well-known fact that if a person suffering from hysteria is allowed to speak, he ends up naming the cause of his malady in negative language. For example, when a woman has a hysterical fit, she may rave that she still loves her husband, that she respects him, that there is no disagreement between them, and so on -- and by these very statements she is, without meaning to, revealing the real cause of her condition.

Similarly, from Arjuna's declared indifference to all these things, we can clearly discern the real cause of his mental state: that he did in fact desire victory. He was eager to quickly gain kingdom and happiness for himself and his kinsmen. But on seeing the vast Kaurava army and the well-known valiant warriors within it, his hopes were shattered, his ambition crushed, and he began to lose even his self-confidence. Thus he gradually arrived at the state of despair called Arjuna's disease, the cure of which is the very subject-matter of the Gita.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
By slaying those eager for battle who are now present, victory, kingdom, and pleasures could indeed be gained -- so why should one withdraw from battle at all? Anticipating this objection, he says, in 'Na kankshe...': why should one not desire kingdom and the rest, since all desire it? Might it not be that, through it, the well-being of one's sons, brothers and the rest could be secured? Anticipating this too, he says, in 'Kim...'. He then states the reason for rejecting kingdom and the rest, in 'Yesham...'.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O Krishna! I do not desire victory, nor kingdom, nor pleasures. O Govinda! What benefit is there to us from a kingdom? What benefit from enjoyments? Or what benefit even from living?

Explanation: 'Na kankshe vijayam krishna na cha rajyam sukhani cha' -- suppose we are victorious in battle; through victory we would gain the kingdom of the entire earth, dominion over it. Gaining the kingdom of the earth, we would obtain many kinds of pleasures. But I desire none of these -- that is, my mind holds no craving for victory, kingdom, or pleasures.

'Kim no rajyena govinda kim bhogairjivitena va' -- when there is no craving of any kind (for victory, kingdom, and pleasure) in our minds, then however great a kingdom we might obtain, what benefit is that to us? However many fine pleasures we might obtain, what benefit are they to us? Or, killing our kinsmen, however many years we might live enjoying the pleasures of the kingdom, what benefit is that too? The point is: victory, kingdom, and pleasure can give happiness only when there is craving for them within, when they are dear, when they matter. But within us there is no craving for them at all. So what happiness can they give us? We have no desire to live after killing these kinsmen; for when our kinsmen are dead, of what use will this kingdom and these pleasures be? Kingdom, pleasure and the like exist for the sake of one's family -- but when the family itself is dead, who will enjoy them? Enjoyment aside, we would only have greater anxiety and grief!

Transition: Arjuna states, in the next verse, the reason why he does not desire victory and the rest.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Arjuna said -- Sanjaya said: The high-souled Partha, supremely compassionate, seeing there, stationed in both armies, his fathers, grandfathers, teachers, maternal uncles, brothers, sons, grandsons, and friends -- his own kinsmen arrayed for battle -- was overcome, and spoke thus to Krishna.

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
'Na cha shreyah' etc. up to 'mahikrite': those wrongly conceived as objects to be slain, with the individualizing notion 'these are my teachers and the rest,' would necessarily generate sin thereby. Likewise, the act of slaying, even of those who truly deserve to be slain in battle, if undertaken with the thought 'this battle is being waged for visible ends such as pleasure, happiness and the like' -- that too necessarily generates sin. This notion lies hidden within Arjuna's objection. It is for this very reason that the Lord's reply will be given: 'you must perform actions solely as your own duty, not with the individualizing notion [of who is being slain, or of what is to be gained].'

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
One might object: let there be no unseen benefit; but victory, kingdom, and pleasure are visible benefits, beyond dispute -- to this he answers: earlier, happiness was the object of desire, and the desire for its fruit is what impels one to the means for achieving it. Hence, since there is no craving for that fruit, my engaging in the means -- battle -- makes no more sense than someone without hunger engaging in cooking. One might further ask: why is there no desire in you for what other men do desire? To this he answers, in 'Kim no...': 'bhogaih' means pleasures; 'jivitena' means the means of living, i.e., victory. For us, dwelling in the forest without kingdom, pleasures, or victory over the Kauravas, our life -- praiseworthy in the eyes of the world on that very account -- what use have we for these desired things? By the word 'Govinda,' addressing Him as ever-present as the indwelling ruler of the senses (denoted by 'go'), Arjuna hints: You alone know my detachment from worldly fruits. He then states the reason for rejecting kingdom and the rest -- this refutes the view that even one who is himself detached should strive on behalf of his own people; for a solitary person indeed has no need of a kingdom. But those for whose sake it is needed -- these very kinsmen -- stand arrayed for battle, having abandoned their lives, their hope of life, their wealth, and their hope of wealth; hence this effort is not for my own sake nor for theirs, is the sense. The word 'bhoga,' though earlier subsumed under 'sukha' (happiness), is here mentioned separately, referring specifically to the means of happiness.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
If asked, do you not see any benefit such as victory? To this he answers, in 'Na kankshe...'. He elaborates on this very point, in 'Kim no...' across two-and-a-half verses: for whose sake we need kingdom and the rest, those very ones stand arrayed for battle, having abandoned their lives and wealth, accepting sacrifice. Hence, what use do we have for kingdom and the rest?

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
One might object: since victory over the enemy through battle, and the consequent gain of kingdom and the like, are certain, why do you say you see no good? To this he answers, in 'Na kankshe...': addressing Him as 'O Krishna,' meaning one whose mind is fixed on Vasudeva in japa, homa, worship and the like -- as it is said, 'obstacles to that are fruits such as the position of Maitreya or Indra' -- Arjuna hints that victory, kingdom and the like are themselves obstacles to devotion, and that Your grace toward a devotee consists precisely in drawing him away from such things, as the Lord Himself has said: 'whomever I wish to favor, I take away his wealth.' Hence, having reflected that victory and the rest would obstruct devotion to You, he hints, 'I do not desire it.' One might further object: though renunciants like yourselves are indeed detached regarding your own interest, is it not still proper to desire victory for the sake of your own kinsmen? Anticipating this, and noting that those for whose sake victory would be desired will themselves die here, he asks what use it is to the Pandavas, in 'Kim...' across two-and-a-half verses: what use to us is a kingdom? What use are the pleasures and life arising from it? None whatsoever, is the sense. Compared to seeking kingdom and the rest, it is better for us to dwell in the forest, living on roots and fruits, protecting our own kinsmen -- for protecting one's kinsmen by every means is a duty. By addressing Him as 'Govinda,' the world-teacher who earned that very name through protecting his kinsmen (the cows), Arjuna hints: You alone, by that name, praise such protection of kinsmen. Others say the address 'Govinda' means: You, ever present as indwelling ruler of the senses (denoted by 'go'), alone know my detachment from worldly fruits.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Apart from that, I do not desire victory or kingdom, nor even the pleasures born of them. By the second 'cha' (and), even in devotion I do not desire pleasures, for the very cause of the Lord's satisfaction is austerity, is the sense. He further elaborates, in detail, on this very absence of desire for them, in 'Kim no rajyena...': what use to us is a kingdom, or pleasures, or even life, O Govinda, without You -- these hold no purpose for us at all, is the sense. By the address 'Govinda,' the sense is conveyed: just as You, becoming Indra for the residents of Vraja, arranged their enjoyment of pleasures, so too is it fitting for Your devotees.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
O Govinda, of what avail to us are a kingdom, happiness or even life itself

 Arjuna has addressed Lord Kṛṣṇa as Govinda because Kṛṣṇa is the object of all pleasures for cows and the senses. By using this significant word, Arjuna indicates that Kṛṣṇa should understand what will satisfy Arjuna’s senses. But Govinda is not meant for satisfying our senses. If we try to satisfy the senses of Govinda, however, then automatically our own senses are satisfied. Materially, everyone wants to satisfy his senses, and he wants God to be the order supplier for such satisfaction. The Lord will satisfy the senses of the living entities as much as they deserve, but not to the extent that they may covet. But when one takes the opposite way – namely, when one tries to satisfy the senses of Govinda without desiring to satisfy one’s own senses – then by the grace of Govinda all desires of the living entity are satisfied. Arjuna’s deep affection for community and family members is exhibited here partly due to his natural compassion for them. He is therefore not prepared to fight. Everyone wants to show his opulence to friends and relatives, but Arjuna fears that all his relatives and friends will be killed on the battlefield and he will be unable to share his opulence after victory. This is a typical calculation of material life. The transcendental life, however, is different. Since a devotee wants to satisfy the desires of the Lord, he can, Lord willing, accept all kinds of opulence for the service of the Lord, and if the Lord is not willing, he should not accept a farthing. Arjuna did not want to kill his relatives, and if there were any need to kill them, he desired that Kṛṣṇa kill them personally. At this point he did not know that Kṛṣṇa had already killed them before their coming into the battlefield and that he was only to become an instrument for Kṛṣṇa. This fact is disclosed in following chapters. As a natural devotee of the Lord, Arjuna did not like to retaliate against his miscreant cousins and brothers, but it was the Lord’s plan that they should all be killed. The devotee of the Lord does not retaliate against the wrongdoer, but the Lord does not tolerate any mischief done to the devotee by the miscreants. The Lord can excuse a person on His own account, but He excuses no one who has done harm to His devotees. Therefore the Lord was determined to kill the miscreants, although Arjuna wanted to excuse them.
