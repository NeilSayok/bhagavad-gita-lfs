Life Application:
Admit your confusion honestly when overwhelmed by conflict, and seek guidance from someone with proven wisdom.

Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Finding himself utterly helpless and incapable of any decision, Arjuna now wholly surrenders himself to the Lord's refuge. He admits that his mental state has become deranged, and identifies its chief cause as excessive pity. Out of ignorance he cannot see that this pity is baseless. He admits that, his intellect being clouded on whether to fight or not, he is unable to determine dharma from adharma.

We have already seen the meaning of dharma: that quality of a thing by which its very existence is established is called its dharma. Hindu philosophy emphasizes human dharma, meaning that each person should live in accordance with his own pure divine nature, striving to preserve the dignity of that true nature rather than living an animal-like existence.

Here Arjuna approaches the Lord in the attitude of a disciple, signaling that he has now become fit to receive instruction and will follow the Lord's teaching. It also signals that even if, out of ignorance, Arjuna repeatedly raises doubts and questions, the Lord must resolve them with sympathy and patience. Throughout the Gita we see Arjuna raising doubts amid Krishna's teaching, yet nowhere do we see Krishna lose patience — rather, with each question Arjuna asks, he answers with ever greater enthusiasm on the battlefield.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
He indicates the aspirant's fitness to receive instruction in Self-knowledge, being one who has perceived the faults of worldly existence, become exceedingly disenchanted, seeks liberation, and has properly approached his teacher, in 'Karpanya...': one who cannot bear even a slight loss to himself is called 'kripana' (a miser); by that same token, all who have not known the Self, not having attained the supreme goal, are misers. As scripture says: 'He who departs from this world without knowing the Imperishable, O Gargi, is a miser.' The state of being such is 'karpanya' (miserliness); my nature, my mind, is overpowered, tainted, by that fault. I therefore ask You, my mind confused about dharma — dharma being the Supreme Brahman that sustains all — confused, lacking discrimination, regarding that. What do I ask? That which would be my certain, absolute, unconditional good — not indefinite or temporary like the cessation of disease, nor conditional like heaven — tell me that liberation. Do not think, 'this should not be told, since it is forbidden to tell one who is not a son or a disciple' — for I have become your disciple. Instruct, teach me that liberation. I have taken refuge in You.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
With my nature overcome by the fault of cowardice, and my mind confused about dharma, I ask You: tell me for certain what is truly good for me. I am Your disciple; instruct me who have taken refuge in You.

Explanation: Although Arjuna did not, in his own mind, consider withdrawing entirely from battle to be the very best course, he saw no other means of avoiding sin except to cease fighting. So he wished to withdraw from battle, considering this withdrawal itself a virtue, not the fault of cowardice. But when the Lord called this very withdrawal cowardice and petty weakness of heart, Arjuna, on hearing these unambiguous words, began to think that withdrawing from battle was not proper for him — that it was indeed a kind of cowardice, wholly contrary to his own nature, since meekness and fleeing (turning one's back) have no place in a Kshatriya's nature. Thus, accepting the fault of cowardice named by the Lord, Arjuna says: on one hand my Kshatriya nature has been suppressed by this fault of cowardice, and on the other, my intellect cannot decide anything regarding dharma — such confusion has overtaken my intellect that it is of no use regarding dharma.

In the third verse the Lord had already commanded Arjuna clearly: 'abandon this petty weakness of heart, this cowardice, and rise for battle.' By this Arjuna should have had no doubt left regarding his duty. Yet doubt remains, because on one hand destroying one's family and killing one's elders in battle appears as adharma (sin), while on the other, fighting appears to be the Kshatriya's dharma. Thus, considering his kinsmen, he feels he should not fight, yet from the standpoint of Kshatriya-dharma he feels he should — caught between these two, Arjuna fell into a moral dilemma, his intellect becoming paralyzed in determining dharma. In this state, he asks the Lord to help him determine: 'what exactly is my particular duty at this moment? What is my dharma?'

'Yacchreyah syan nishchitam bruhi tan me' — in the second verse of this very chapter, the Lord had said that Arjuna's withdrawal from battle out of cowardice was conduct 'unbefitting the noble' — that superior persons do not act this way, but act only in whatever leads to their own true good. Hearing this, the thought arose in Arjuna's mind that he too should do what superior persons do. Thus the desire for his true good awakened in Arjuna's mind, and it is with this very desire that he asks the Lord to tell him definitively what would bring his certain good.

'Shishyas te'ham' — having asked about his own good, the thought arose in Arjuna's mind that questions of one's true good are asked of a guru, not a charioteer. With this thought, the sense of being the master of the chariot, by which he had commanded the Lord 'place my chariot between the two armies,' fell away, and Arjuna became the Lord's disciple in order to ask about his own good, saying: 'Maharaj! I am your disciple, fit to receive instruction; tell me of my true good.'

'Shadhi mam tvam prapannam' — a guru will give instruction, will impart knowledge of the path unknown, will give full illumination, will tell the whole matter — but the disciple himself must walk the path. The disciple himself must accomplish his own good. Transition: in the preceding verse Arjuna surrendered to the Lord, yet the thought occurred to him that the Lord's very intention is to make him fight, while he himself does not consider fighting to be dharma for himself. Just as the Lord earlier commanded him to fight by saying 'Arise,' so too He might again command him to fight now. A further thought: perhaps he has not been able to fully lay bare the feelings of his heart before the Lord. With these thoughts, Arjuna, in the following verse, clearly describes the state of his heart in favor of not fighting.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Thus, having begun the battle, one might argue: 'the sons of Dhritarashtra, ceasing their hostile activity, will nonetheless be slain violently by us' -- so be it; but since we, not knowing what is dharma and what is adharma, would gain victory through their slaying, and that victory would itself be unrighteous, it seems to me that being slain by them is actually the better course. Having said this, Arjuna, exceedingly humbled, approached the lotus feet of the Lord, saying: tell me, Your surrendered disciple, what is truly good for me, as You have ascertained it.

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
On 'Karpanya...' and the rest: through the words beginning 'between the two armies' and the rest, it is indicated that Arjuna, overcome with doubt, has not merely ceased fighting through one particular line of reasoning; and it is precisely for this reason that he says 'instruct me, who have taken refuge in You.' Hence he is being taught by the Blessed Lord as one caught between both knowledge and ignorance.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Approaching a teacher is now taught, since one is entitled to receive knowledge only upon properly approaching a teacher, being one who has perceived the faults of worldly existence and become exceedingly disenchanted. He who cannot bear even a slight loss of wealth is known in the world as 'miserly'; in the same sense, all who have not known the Self, not attained the true human goal, are misers — as scripture says: 'He who departs from this world without knowing the Imperishable, O Gargi, is a miser.' Their state is 'karpanya,' an excessive attachment born of identifying with the not-Self — hence, whatever kinsmen were mine in this birth, seeing them slain, what use is life to me? — this fault of possessiveness has overpowered my very Kshatriya nature, disposed toward the effort of battle. Likewise, my mind is confused about dharma, unable to find any decisive proof: is the slaying of these kinsmen dharma, or is their protection dharma? Is the protection of the earth dharma, or is dwelling as one properly should in the forest dharma? — pervaded by such doubts. Being thus, I now ask You: tell me that which would be my certain and absolute good, the supreme human goal. Since I have become Your disciple, not merely Your friend — owing to my lesser knowledge — instruct me, who has taken refuge in You, out of compassion, not neglecting me on the mistaken assumption that I am unfit to be taught.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Hence, my nature overpowered by the fault of miserliness -- how shall I live, having slain these? -- that miserliness, and the fault caused by the destruction of my own family, by these two my nature, characterized by valor and the rest, is overwhelmed; I, being so, ask You. Likewise, my mind is confused about dharma: whether abandoning battle and resorting to begging alms is dharma or adharma for a Kshatriya, being of doubtful mind, is the sense. Hence, tell me that which would be my certain, proper good. Moreover, I am your disciple, fit to be instructed. Hence, instruct me, who have taken refuge in You, teach me.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Knowing the worthlessness of worldly existence, and utterly dispassionate toward enjoyment in this world and the next, one desirous of liberation approaches a teacher — this he indicates, in 'Karpanya...': not knowing the Self, one cannot bear the separation of kinsmen — this is 'karpanya,' miserliness, a state of meanness; my nature, my inner faculty, is overpowered, tainted, by that fault of miserliness. Others say: my nature, characterized by valor and the rest, is overwhelmed by the fault of miserliness; some say it is specifically one's Kshatriya nature, disposed to the effort of battle. My mind, confused about dharma — dharma being the Supreme Self who is the support of all — utterly lacking discrimination regarding that, I ask You. What do I ask? He says: tell me that which would be certain as the true good, established as such by the shruti, smriti, itihasa and purana texts, characterized by eternal, unsurpassed bliss. Do not think this should not be told since it is forbidden to tell one who is not a son or disciple; for I am not without discipleship — I am your disciple, fit to be instructed; instruct me, who have taken refuge in You.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
On 'Na chaitad vidmah' and the rest, he states the intended purport, preceded by the doubt indicated by the word 'cha,' in 'Evam...': it is implied that you, fearing the destruction of your kinsmen, would in fact be causing the destruction of your very closest kinsmen -- Dharmaraja, Bhima, Nakula and the rest -- through your own hesitation. Following the plural form used in 'vidmah' and the rest, he says 'asmakam' (of us): by this word 'asmakam,' it is implied that all are equally disciples, in relation to Bhishma, Drona and the rest who have been designated as fit to be slain. Through the first and second halves of the verse, two doubts reflecting his own preferred positions are indicated. The words 'yadva' and 'yadi va' carry the same sense. The construction of 'yan eva...' and the rest is: those very persons by whose slaying our own life would become undesirable, they themselves, desiring to slay us, stand arrayed there in a manner corresponding to their own slaying [of us]. Through 'na jijivishamah' (we do not wish to live), he expresses the state of mind, culminating in indecision, that is the very cause of his questioning, in 'iti me pratibhati' (thus it appears to me). He then clarifies the qualities of the disciple that render him fit for instruction, as conveyed by the resultant sense of 'yach chhreyah' and the rest, in 'yan mahyam...': the word 'iti' serves the purpose of drawing out a definite conclusion. For a disciple is one who is to be instructed; hence he says, 'I am your disciple; instruct me.' Here, his innate temperament is steadfastness; or, owing to his lack of knowledge regarding the particulars of duty, or owing to his lack of any means to dispel his grief, there is excessive humility. Some say that not abandoning what ought to be abandoned constitutes this humility here; others say it is being absorbed in a pitiable, compassion-evoking state. That he 'approached the lotus feet of the Lord' is itself the fruit of his being established as a disciple, surrendered to the Lord.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having thus stated his own view and declared its faultiness, and resolving now to act on the Lord's command, he says, in 'Karpanya-dosha...': miserliness, the misguided notion that slaying one's kinsmen is improper, is itself a fault; my nature, Kshatriya valor and the rest, is overpowered by that fault; I ask You, being such. One might object: what use is questioning to one whose very nature is overwhelmed, disabled? Anticipating this, he says, 'dharma-sammudha-chetah' (my mind confused about dharma): my mind, confused regarding knowledge of dharma. Are You pleased with the slaying of these, or with not slaying them, or with something between the two, or something else — whatever would truly be Your grace, tell me that with certainty. I am Your disciple, not merely a friend; hence, out of desire to know dharma, instruct me, who have taken refuge in You.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Being one possessed of the doubt already stated, he now asks, in 'Karpanya...': 'karpanya' means meanness of spirit; 'svabhavah' (nature) has the character described further on as 'valor, brilliance, steadfastness, dexterity' and the rest. The remainder is self-evident.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Now I am confused about my duty and have lost all composure because of miserly weakness. In this condition I am asking You to tell me for certain what is best for me. Now I am Your disciple, and a soul surrendered unto You. Please instruct me.

 By nature’s own way the complete system of material activities is a source of perplexity for everyone. In every step there is perplexity, and therefore it behooves one to approach a bona fide spiritual master who can give one proper guidance for executing the purpose of life. All Vedic literatures advise us to approach a bona fide spiritual master to get free from the perplexities of life, which happen without our desire. They are like a forest fire that somehow blazes without being set by anyone. Similarly, the world situation is such that perplexities of life automatically appear, without our wanting such confusion. No one wants fire, and yet it takes place, and we become perplexed. The Vedic wisdom therefore advises that in order to solve the perplexities of life and to understand the science of the solution, one must approach a spiritual master who is in the disciplic succession. A person with a bona fide spiritual master is supposed to know everything. One should not, therefore, remain in material perplexities but should approach a spiritual master. This is the purport of this verse. Who is the man in material perplexities? It is he who does not understand the problems of life. In the Bṛhad-āraṇyaka Upaniṣad (3.8.10) the perplexed man is described as follows: yo vā etad akṣaraṁ gārgy aviditvāsmāḻ lokāt praiti sa kṛpaṇaḥ. “He is a miserly man who does not solve the problems of life as a human and who thus quits this world like the cats and dogs, without understanding the science of self-realization.” This human form of life is a most valuable asset for the living entity, who can utilize it for solving the problems of life; therefore, one who does not utilize this opportunity properly is a miser. On the other hand, there is the brāhmaṇa, or he who is intelligent enough to utilize this body to solve all the problems of life. Ya etad akṣaraṁ gārgi viditvāsmāl lokāt praiti sa brāhmaṇaḥ. The kṛpaṇas, or miserly persons, waste their time in being overly affectionate for family, society, country, etc., in the material conception of life. One is often attached to family life, namely to wife, children and other members, on the basis of “skin disease.” The kṛpaṇa thinks that he is able to protect his family members from death; or the kṛpaṇa thinks that his family or society can save him from the verge of death. Such family attachment can be found even in the lower animals, who take care of children also. Being intelligent, Arjuna could understand that his affection for family members and his wish to protect them from death were the causes of his perplexities. Although he could understand that his duty to fight was awaiting him, still, on account of miserly weakness, he could not discharge the duties. He is therefore asking Lord Kṛṣṇa, the supreme spiritual master, to make a definite solution. He offers himself to Kṛṣṇa as a disciple. He wants to stop friendly talks. Talks between the master and the disciple are serious, and now Arjuna wants to talk very seriously before the recognized spiritual master. Kṛṣṇa is therefore the original spiritual master of the science of Bhagavad-gītā , and Arjuna is the first disciple for understanding the Gītā. How Arjuna understands the Bhagavad-gītā is stated in the Gītā itself. And yet foolish mundane scholars explain that one need not submit to Kṛṣṇa as a person, but to “the unborn within Kṛṣṇa.” There is no difference between Kṛṣṇa’s within and without. And one who has no sense of this understanding is the greatest fool in trying to understand Bhagavad-gītā .
