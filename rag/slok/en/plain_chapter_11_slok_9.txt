sañjaya uvāca .
evamuktvā tato rājanmahāyogeśvaro hariḥ .

Life Application:
Contemplate the supreme Lord revealing His majestic, awe-inspiring universal form to the earnest spiritual seeker.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Maharishi Vyasaji, a man of versatile genius, did not leave any aspect of literature he touched without raising it to the highest peak of perfection. Who can describe the genius of Vyasaji? He possessed an unimaginable capacity for incomparable poetic composition, an unparalleled prose style, pure description, artistic literary creation, and the capability of original new creation in both form and thought. All these qualities were present in full measure. A brilliant philosopher, a man of complete knowledge, and skilled in practical wisdom, Vyasaji was seen sometimes in royal palaces, sometimes on the battlefield, sometimes in Badrinath, and sometimes traversing the path to the silent, solitary snowy peaks—the giant-statured Maharishi Vyasa was the personification of all that is best in Hindu tradition and Aryan culture. A man of such versatile genius and filled with all virtues was perhaps never born at any other time in the history of the world, who had achieved as many accomplishments in his lifetime as Vyasaji did.
Lord Krishna indicated to Arjuna what he could expect to see in the Universal Form (Visvarupa) and where this vision of the Cosmic Form would take place. Now, Vyasaji mentions a very brief interlude in which Sanjaya narrates the events of the battlefield to Dhritarashtra, the blind father of the wicked Kauravas.
From a literary perspective, the purpose of this verse is simply to state that Sri Krishna, in accordance with His promise, did indeed reveal His Universal Form to Arjuna. But along with this, Vyasaji, the skillful author of the Mahabharata, also wants to depict for us Sanjaya's inner feelings and his sympathy towards the Pandavas. We have already said that Sanjaya is our special correspondent. It is clear that his sympathy lies with the Pandavas, the friends of the Lord. This attitude of his is undoubtedly reflected here when he addresses Dhritarashtra merely as 'O King' (rajan), while addressing Sri Krishna as 'Mahayogeshvara' (the great Lord of Yoga) and 'Hari'. One who takes away (harana-karta) is called Hari, meaning the one who destroys the false and establishes the truth.
With these words of Sanjaya, the attention of the audience and the students of the Gita is drawn away from the battlefield and directed towards the royal palace. Perhaps such a change of scene is necessary to remind the readers that the philosophy of the Gita has a close connection with practical life and has utility in life. Sanjaya informs Dhritarashtra that the great Lord of Yoga, Hari, showed Arjuna His divine form. In Sanjaya's mind, there is still some faint hope that upon hearing that the Lord of the Universe, Sri Krishna, is on the side of the Pandavas, the blind king might foresee the future defeat of his sons and, acting with wisdom, stop the destructive war.
In the following verses, Sanjaya attempts to describe the things visible in the Universal Form.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
Hari is so called because He accepts the share of all sacrifices. As stated in the Mokshadharma: 'I accept the share offered to Hari in households and in sacrifices. My complexion is the excellent green (or golden/tawny), therefore I am remembered as Hari.'

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Sanjaya related this account to Dhritarashtra, hence it is said: "Sanjaya said" (sanjaya iti). The form called My Universal Form (Visvarupa) cannot be seen with ordinary (physical) eyes, but only with divine eyes, as previously stated. "Thereafter" (tatah) means after the gift of the divine eyes. "Hari" is He who destroys ignorance (avidya) along with its effects (sakaryam). The supreme, most excellent form of the Lord associated with Maya is what He showed, hence the word "paramam" (supreme) is used.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Sanjaya said — O King! Having spoken thus, the great Lord of Yoga, Lord Sri Krishna, then showed to Arjuna His supreme lordly form.

Explanation — 'evam uktva tato ... paramam rupam aisvaram' — In the previous verse, the Lord said, 'You cannot see Me with your physical eyes, therefore I give you divine vision, so that you may behold My lordly Yoga.' Sanjaya has referred to this by the word 'evam uktva'. In the fourth verse, Arjuna called the Lord 'Yogeshvara' (Lord of Yoga), and here Sanjaya calls the Lord 'Mahayogeshvara' (the great Lord of Yoga). This implies that the Lord showed His universal form far beyond what Arjuna had prayed for. If a devotee has even a little sincere inclination towards the Lord, the Lord fulfills it with His infinite power.

In the third verse, Arjuna referred to that form as 'rupam aisvaram' (lordly form), and here Sanjaya refers to it as 'paramam rupam aisvaram' (supreme lordly form). This means that the Lord's universal form is extremely extraordinary. Lord Sri Krishna, the great Lord of all Yogas, revealed such an extraordinary, supernatural, and wonderful universal form that even Arjuna, who was patient, self-controlled, brave, and endowed with divine vision granted by the Lord, had to call it difficult to look at (11.17) and became terrified (11.45), so much so that the Lord had to reassure Arjuna by saying 'vyapeta-bhih' (free from fear) (11.49).

Connection — Now Sanjaya describes that supreme lordly form of the Lord in the next two verses.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
In the phrase 'mahāyogeśvaro hariḥ', the contextually appropriate meaning of the word 'Hari' is explained as: 'Hari' is derived from the root 'hṛ' (to take/destroy) with the suffix 'i'. 'In houses' means in the places of sacrifice. 'Shrestha' means non-material/spiritual. If He, who possesses a non-material (spiritual) and personal form, is simultaneously present in His own form at infinite places of worship, what need is there to speak of His being the supreme Lord of Yoga (Mahayogeshvaratva)?

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
The Lord showed His divine form to Arjuna, and having seen it, Arjuna was filled with wonder and addressed the Lord; Sanjaya related this account to Dhritarashtra in six verses starting with 'evam'. 'Evam' (thus) means: 'Having spoken thus, i.e., "You cannot see Me with these eyes of yours, therefore I give you divine vision," and thereafter, i.e., after giving the divine eyes. O King Dhritarashtra, be composed to listen. He who is both great (supremely excellent) and the Lord of Yoga is "Mahayogeshvara". Hari, the Lord who destroys all the miseries of His devotees, showed to the one-pointed devotee, the son of Pritha (Arjuna), His supreme, divine, lordly form which is otherwise impossible to be seen.'

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Indicating such majesty of Lord Vasudeva and His affection towards Arjuna, Sanjaya spoke to Dhritarashtra. Having spoken thus in the manner described, thereafter, the great Lord of Yoga, Hari, showed His supreme lordly form to the son of Pritha (Arjuna), who is the object of His supreme love. By the word 'Hari', it is indicated that He is ready to remove the suffering of His devotees, the Pandavas (sons of Pritha). By the word 'Mahayogeshvara', it is indicated that He is extremely capable of removing that suffering by any means, such as by displaying His universal form. By addressing Dhritarashtra as 'O King' (rajan), it is implied: 'You did not make peace, nor are you making peace now, with the Pandavas who are favored by such a Krishna, and therefore you are devoid of political wisdom and are a king in name only.'

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
To explain the meaning of the words 'Mahayogeshvara' (the great Lord of Yoga) and 'Hari', which might seem to contrast with His previously seen form, it is said: 'He who is stationed as the charioteer and is the maternal cousin of Arjuna...' By this, His affection (vatsalya) etc., which are the cause of displaying the divine form, are indicated. The qualification with 'greatness' (mahattva) points to its wonderful nature. Since the word 'maha' qualifies 'yogeshvara', it is appropriate here to understand it as a special kind of yoga; hence it is said 'of supremely wonderful yogas'. By the word 'Ishvara', it is intended that the wonderful yogas of all others are subordinate to Him. The general and specific meaning of the phrase 'Mahayogeshvara Hari' is shown as 'the Supreme Brahman, Narayana'. The closeness of 'Behold My lordly Yoga' (11.8) and 'supreme lordly Form' indicates they have the same meaning. Lest one think that 'showed the lordly form' refers to the form of someone else, it is clarified as 'His own unique form'. The word 'rupa' (form) here should be understood as referring to His essential nature along with its attributes, or the physical form manifesting it, or simply all His unique aspects like form etc. Here, the word 'Partha' implies the relationship of love which is the cause of showing His form; to show this, it is said: 'to the son of His father's sister, Pritha'. The phrase 'supreme lordly form' is elaborated as: 'that which is the support of the diverse and wonderful entire universe...' This support is both in essence and through His form. Or rather, this is the introduction to 'anekavaktra...' (11.10) etc. Therefore, it is connected with 'and it was like this'. This meaning is established by 'here, concentrated in one' (11.7) etc. By the word 'prashasitr' (ruler), His lordship/governance is meant.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Sanjaya said to Dhritarashtra: 'Having spoken thus, the Lord showed His form to Arjuna...' To remove all suffering caused by their pride of being kings, and to remove the demoniac possession of Bhishma and others by giving divine vision, the great Lord of Yoga (Mahayogeshvara), who is capable of doing everything, through the power of His all-pervading Yoga, having spoken thus and having given the supernatural vision, showed His supreme form—the form of Purushottama—to the son of Pritha (Arjuna) whom He had accepted as His own. Then, after that presentation, He showed the lordly form.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Having spoken thus, the Lord showed the divine form to Arjuna, and he, filled with wonder on seeing it, addressed the Lord; Sanjaya related this event to Dhritarashtra in six verses starting with 'evamuktva'. 'Tatah' means after the gift of the divine eye. 'O King' means Dhritarashtra. 'Mahayogeshvara' is analyzed as: He who is both Great (mahan) and the Lord of Yoga (yogeshvara), or the Lord of the great Yoga. He showed the supreme divine form, 'aishvaram', which is related to the wielder of Maya (Mayavin), and not that which transcends Maya.
