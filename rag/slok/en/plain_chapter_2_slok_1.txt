Life Application:
When compassion threatens to slide into paralyzing despair, regain your composure and look toward your true duty.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
The second chapter opens with Sanjaya's narration, in which, through carefully chosen words, he paints a vivid picture of Arjuna's despondent state of mind. Arjuna's mind has become filled with compassion and despondency. This device makes it clear that Arjuna, instead of being master of the circumstances, had himself become their victim. It is precisely such a weak person, victimized by circumstances, who fails at every turn in life's struggle. Arjuna, in his despairing state, had at this moment become the victim of just such external circumstances. Along with describing Arjuna's despondent condition, Sanjaya also indicates to us that his inner personality had shattered, and that a deep fissure had appeared in his character. Though the greatest archer of his age, he was weeping like an ordinary young woman.

Thus, to Arjuna, who was overwhelmed with compassion and grief and weeping without tears, Lord Sri Krishna, Madhusudana (the slayer of the demon named Madhu), spoke the following words. It is worth noting here that modern psychology regards tearless weeping as the extreme state of mental agitation.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Having heard of Arjuna's aversion to battle, arising from the notion that non-violence is the highest dharma and that one should live on alms, and having ascertained that his own sons' kingdom and sovereignty remained undisturbed, Sanjaya, wishing to dispel the false hope of Dhritarashtra -- who sat there with a composed heart -- by relating this account, spoke to him, is the sense, in 'Sanjaya...'. Even though reminded by the Supreme Lord of what was proper and improper to do, Arjuna did not immediately recall it, since sorrow arising from confusion is itself a cause of deepened delusion; yet the Lord did not, for that reason, neglect him -- this is stated, in 'Tam tatha...': to that Partha, thus overcome, gripped, by compassion -- occasioned by seeing the prospect of his own kinsmen's deaths -- his eyes filled and confused with tears, that is, his trembling, tear-drenched eyes, who was despondent, grieving, Madhusudana -- the Lord who had slain the demon named Madhu -- spoke this forthcoming, reasoned utterance; that is, He did not neglect the aforementioned Arjuna, is the sense.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Sanjaya said: To that Arjuna, overcome by such cowardice, who was in despondency and whose power of sight was obstructed by tears, the Lord Madhusudana spoke these words [about to be stated].

Explanation: 'Tam tatha kripayavishtam' -- Arjuna had instructed the Lord, who was seated as his charioteer, saying: O Achyuta, station my chariot between the two armies, so that I may see who these people are that have come to fight against me in this war -- that is, which warriors have found the courage to come and fight against a hero like myself? How did they find the courage to fight against me even while their own death stands visibly before them? Thus, the very Arjuna who had such enthusiasm and valour for battle, upon seeing his own kinsmen in both armies, became so overwhelmed with grief at the apprehension of their death, gripped by delusion, that his body has gone limp, his mouth is drying up, his body is trembling, his hair is standing on end, the bow is slipping from his hand, his skin is burning, he no longer has even the strength to stand, and his mind too has become bewildered. Where, on the one hand, was Arjuna's natural disposition expressed in 'neither cowardice nor flight,' and where, on the other, is this Arjuna now, overcome with grief through the fault of cowardice, sitting down in the middle of the chariot! With great astonishment, Sanjaya expresses this very sentiment through the words quoted above.

Earlier too, in the twenty-eighth verse of the first chapter, Sanjaya had used the words 'kripaya parayavishtah' (overcome by supreme compassion) for Arjuna.

'Ashrupurnakulekshanam' -- even within so great a hero as Arjuna, familial delusion had taken hold, and his eyes filled with tears! So many tears filled his eyes that he could not even see properly.

'Vishidantam idam vakyam uvacha madhusudanah' -- thus, to Arjuna who was in despondency because of his cowardice, the Lord Madhusudana spoke these words [to be stated in the following second and third verses].

Here, simply saying 'vishidantam uvacha' would have sufficed; there was no need to say 'idam vakyam' as well, since the word 'vakyam' (speech) is already implied within the verb 'uvacha' (spoke). Nevertheless, the point of saying 'vakyam' is that this utterance, this speech of the Lord's, is extraordinarily remarkable. Within Arjuna, wearing the guise of dharma, an evil in the form of abandonment of duty had crept in -- and this divine utterance strikes directly at that evil. It is an utterance that will thoroughly shake Arjuna's resolve to withdraw from battle. It is one that will awaken in Arjuna the desire for his own true welfare, by making him aware of his own fault. It is precisely through the influence of this profoundly meaningful utterance that Arjuna comes to accept discipleship under the Lord and takes refuge in Him (2.7).

The purpose behind Sanjaya's use of the word 'Madhusudana' is this: Lord Sri Krishna is the slayer of the demon named Madhu -- that is, He is the destroyer of those of wicked nature. Hence He will not rest until He has brought about the destruction of Duryodhana and the other wicked-natured ones.

Transition: what words the Lord spoke to Arjuna is stated in the following two verses.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
In the first chapter, Partha's grief was declared as renunciation; thus is he established as a fit recipient for the exposition of Sankhya and Yoga. These two, being of the form of the wings of knowledge, resemble the adornments of Hari and Isha, and serve the purpose of firm-mindedness through knowledge of the Self's true nature. From this, through the manifestation of one's status as a portion of the Lord, reverence for taking refuge arises; and the duty of one who has taken such refuge is to observe the dharma enjoined by the Lord's command. Hence, the performance of battle and the like, done in the form of obedience to that command, is held to be proper -- for a devotee of the pushti-marga (path of grace) mixed with devotion would not hold a taste for Sankhya alone. In the midst of this, the statement of one's own dharma made within the context of Sankhya and Yoga will, through it, establish the manner grounded in grace within the heart. In the second chapter, the despondency of the first chapter is called Sankhya; there, one's own dharma, at the conclusion of Yoga, serves the purpose of firm-mindedness. Then, in response to the expectation 'what happened next,' Sanjaya again speaks, in 'Tam tatha...'.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Having heard of Arjuna's aversion to battle, arising from the notion that non-violence is the highest dharma and that one should live on alms, and having ascertained that his sons' kingdom remained undisturbed, Vaishampayana states that Sanjaya, wishing to remove the anxiety in the mind of Dhritarashtra -- who was gladdened at heart, wondering 'what happened next' -- spoke to him thus. 'Kripa' (compassion) here denotes a particular affection born of delusion regarding 'mine.' By that affection, arising naturally, he was 'avishtam,' that is, pervaded. By speaking of Arjuna as the object and compassion as the agent, it is shown that this compassion was not something adventitious but rooted in his very nature. Hence 'vishidantam' (despondent) means: he who was reaching that state called despondency -- that is, the bewilderment of mind caused by the apprehension of separation from his own kinsmen, who were the objects of his affection, otherwise called grief. Here too, by treating despondency as the object and Arjuna as its agent, its rootedness in his nature, not its adventitious character, is indicated. Hence, his eyes were 'akule' -- filled and confused, incapable of clear sight -- on account of compassion and despondency. Thus, to that Arjuna, agitated by compassion and despondency which had, through producing these two effects (the shedding of tears and mental confusion), reached their fullness, He spoke this forthcoming, reasoned utterance -- He did not neglect him. The name 'Madhusudana' conveys the sense: He who is Himself the subduer of the wicked will speak in just such a manner even to Arjuna.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Thus, in response to the expectation of what the Lord said to Arjuna, seated upon the chariot's seat, Sanjaya speaks, in 'Tam...'. As for the alternative reading -- that Sanjaya, having heard of Arjuna's aversion to battle on the notion that non-violence is the highest dharma and one should live by alms, and having ascertained that his sons' kingdom remained undisturbed, spoke so as to gladden the heart of Dhritarashtra who wished to know what happened next -- that reading should be disregarded, since it conflicts with the earlier text. To that Arjuna, thus overcome, pervaded, by compassion born of affection, in the manner described, whose eyes -- incapable of clear sight, filled and confused with tears -- who was reaching that despondency, that grief, occasioned by the apprehension of separation from his kinsmen, He spoke this fitting, forthcoming utterance -- He did not neglect him, is the sense. By the name 'Madhusudana' -- He who subdued the wicked Madhu and others -- it is suggested that He would, through the likes of Bhima, subdue the wicked Duryodhana and the rest as well; this is conveyed.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Now begins the second chapter, whose subject is the removal of grief. Even though there is no break in Sanjaya's speech, the indication 'Sanjaya said' is given to forestall the doubt that this might be someone else's utterance, in the form of marking the start of a new chapter. He explains the three verses beginning 'Tam tatha...', in 'Evam...'. To indicate that everything up to 'vishidantam' is a recapitulation of what was stated in the previous chapter, it is said 'Evam upavishte Parthe' (Partha having thus sat down). 'Tatha' means 'in an improper place.' 'Kripa' is inner despondency; hence, being overcome even by outward grief, his eyes were filled and confused with tears, is the sense. 'Vishidantam' means having attained despondency in the manner described in the previous chapter. By the word 'Madhusudana,' his capacity to destroy the rajas and tamas that are the root of grief is suggested. 'Asthane' (out of place) carries the sense implied by the word 'vishama' (uneven, improper). 'Kashmalam' here is akin to a swoon; this is established from the earlier context, 'shoka-samvigna-manasah' (1.47). The words 'Arjuna,' 'Partha,' 'Parantapa' -- indicating his renowned lineage, valour, learning and the like -- carry, by virtue of his being Kunti's son, an implied reproach directed at you; this is the sense intended by the word 'akshipya' (having reproached). The word 'kutah' (whence) too, imputing causality to what is a mere semblance of a cause, carries an implied rebuke. 'Parantapa' means one who scorches the enemy. 'Klaibyam' here means cowardice; this is elaborated by the word 'hridaya-daurbalya' (weakness of heart). Since the qualifiers used in the previous verse also serve as grounds for the impropriety of cowardice, they are here brought into relevance in meaning, through the phrase 'tam imam vishamastham' and the rest. Those whose discernment has gone far from untruth are the 'aryas,' the wise; others are 'anaryas.' In the word 'asvargyam' (not conducive to heaven), the word 'svarga,' without qualification, denotes merely the next world in general. And since the negative prefix here, functioning to indicate opposition, opposes -- through the fruit intended by the word 'svargya' (conducive to heaven) -- the very thing the word denotes, it is rendered as 'contrary to the next world.' Since the word 'kshudra' (mean, petty) admits of no narrowing here that would restrict it to some relative pettiness, and considering that this quality is attributed to the greater Arjuna in such a state, the word 'atikshudram' (utterly mean) is used to show that a pettiness reaching its extreme limit is intended. Whether by figuratively transferring the cause's name to the effect, or by intending the fruit stated in the surrounding verses, the phrase 'hridaya-daurbalya-kritam' (produced by weakness of heart) is used, meaning: produced by unsteadiness of heart. By the word 'Parantapa,' the contextually intended meaning is conveyed, supplying the elliptical sense: 'rise up for battle.'

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
To King Dhritarashtra -- who hoped that, with Arjuna's withdrawal from battle, his own sons would obtain the kingdom unopposed -- Sanjaya spoke, in 'Tam tatha...': that Arjuna, overcome by 'kripa' -- meaning affection, not compassion in the sense of a wish to remove another's suffering. For that latter kind of compassion, arising subsequent to the conviction of another's weakness, would conflict with Arjuna's own words elsewhere -- fearing his own defeat, he says 'or they may conquer us' (2.6), and, indicative of excessive affection, he says he has no wish to live even after killing them (1.35) -- with such statements this reading would conflict. 'Avishtam' means pervaded. 'Vishidantam' means having attained the despondency described in passages such as 'my limbs give way' (1.28) and the rest. He spoke this forthcoming, worthy utterance. By the name 'Madhusudana' -- he who is the slayer of the wicked -- it is suggested: using Arjuna as the mere occasion, He will indeed slay your sons too; hence you ought not to cherish hope of victory.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
Sanjaya said: Seeing Arjuna full of compassion, his mind depressed, his eyes full of tears, Madhusudana, Krishna, spoke the following words.

 Material compassion, lamentation and tears are all signs of ignorance of the real self. Compassion for the eternal soul is self-realization. The word 'Madhusudana' is significant in this verse. Lord Krishna killed the demon Madhu, and now Arjuna wanted Krishna to kill the demon of misunderstanding that had overtaken him in the discharge of his duty. No one knows where compassion should be applied. Compassion for the dress of a drowning man is senseless. A man fallen in the ocean of nescience cannot be saved simply by rescuing his outward dress -- the gross material body. One who does not know this and laments for the outward dress is called a shudra, or one who laments unnecessarily. Arjuna was a kshatriya, and this conduct was not expected from him. Lord Krishna, however, can dissipate the lamentation of the ignorant man, and for this purpose the Bhagavad-gita was sung by Him. This chapter instructs us in self-realization by an analytical study of the material body and the spirit soul, as explained by the supreme authority, Lord Sri Krishna. This realization is possible when one works without attachment to fruitive results and is situated in the fixed conception of the real self.
