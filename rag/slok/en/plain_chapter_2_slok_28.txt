Speaker: Sri Bhagavan

Slok:
avyaktādīni bhūtāni vyaktamadhyāni bhārata .
avyaktanidhanānyeva tatra kā paridevanā

Word Meanings:
अव्यक्तादीनि - unmanifested in the beginning
भूतानि - beings
व्यक्तमध्यानि - manifested in their middle
भारत - O Bharata
अव्यक्तनिधनानि - unmanifest again in death / dissolution
एव - also
तत्र - there
का - what
परिदेवना - grief.

Life Application:
Appreciate the temporary nature of worldly forms without clinging to them when their time passes.

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
From this verse onward, over the next few verses, the problem is presented before Arjuna very beautifully from the standpoint of the ordinary person of the world. In these ten verses Krishna clarifies the matter according to the outlook and understanding of the common person. In this material world, the law of cause and effect is experienced to operate without fail. The effect arises from the cause. Generally, the effect is seen as manifest, while the cause remains unmanifest. Thus, creation means things coming from an unmanifest state into a manifest state. This same sequence continues on, unfailingly, without pause. In this way, what is manifest today was unmanifest yesterday; in the present it is available in manifest form, but in the future it will again dissolve into the unmanifest state. Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Even though it is improper to grieve targeting the Self, grief might seem proper when targeting the aggregate beings (bodies) -- to this doubt, he says 'karya...' The next verse gives the reason for this, as stated by 'yatah...' The word 'anupalabdhi' (non-perception) rules out the notion of mere visual perception -- since the aforementioned aggregate beings are not perceived before their origination, hence they receive such a designation. What is their middle, called their manifest state, is said with 'utpannani...' -- their practical existence after origination and before death is called their manifest middle. Assuming that dissolution follows the pattern of birth, the intended meaning is given with 'maranat...' Puranic testimony for this meaning is cited with 'tatha ca...' The meaning of 'tatra' is explained with 'adrsta...' -- previously unseen, then seen, then destroyed again. Thus, being objects of delusion, cycling like a water-clock, there is no room for lamentation caused by grief regarding these beings -- this is the purport.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O Bharata! All beings were unmanifest before birth and will become unmanifest after death; only in between do they appear manifest. So what reason is there to grieve over this?

Explanation -- 'avyaktadini bhutani' -- all the beings (bodies and so on) that come within seeing, hearing, and understanding were all unmanifest before their birth, that is, they were not seen.

'avyaktanidhanani eva' -- all these beings will become unmanifest after death, that is, upon their destruction, they will all pass into 'non-being' and will not be seen.

'vyaktamadhyani' -- all these beings appear manifest only in between, that is, after birth and before death. Just as there was no dream before sleep and none remains after waking, so too these beings' bodies were absent before and will be absent after. But even while appearing to exist in between, in reality they are ceasing to exist every single moment.

'tatra ka paridevana' -- that which does not exist at the beginning and the end does not exist in the middle either -- this is the principle. All beings' bodies did not exist before and will not exist after; therefore, in truth, they do not exist in the middle either. But this embodied Self existed before and will exist after; therefore it will surely exist in the middle too. The conclusion is that bodies are always non-existent, and the embodied Self is never non-existent. Therefore there can be no grief for either of these.

Connection -- Now the Lord describes the extraordinary nature of the embodied Self.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Human beings etc., (i.e., bodies) exist as entities; their previous stages are unknown, their middle stages in the form of man etc., are known, and their (final) and future stages are unknown. As they thus exist in their own natural stages, there is no cause for grief.

After thus saying that there is no cause for grief even according to the view which identifies the body with the self, Sri Krsna proceeds to say that it is hard to find one who can be said to have truly perceived the Atman or spoken about It or heard about It or gained a true conception of It by hearing. For the Atman, which is actually different from the body, is of a wonderful nature.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
It is not reasonable to grieve even for beings which are constituted by bodies and organs, since 'all beings remain unmanifest' etc. (Bharata, O descendant of Bharata;) bhutani, all beings, avyaktaduni, remain unmainfest in the beginning. Those beings, viz sons, friends, and others, constituted by bodies and organs, [Another reading is karya-karana-sanghata, aggregates formed by material elements acting as causes and effects.-Tr.] who before their origination have unmanifestedness (avyakta), invisibility, nonperception, as their beginning (adi) are avyaktaadini. Ca, and; after origination, before death, they become vyakta-madhyani, manifest in the middle. Again, they eva, certainly; become avyakta-nidhanani, unmanifest after death. Those which have unmanifestness (avyakta), invisibility, as their death (nidhana) are avyakta-nidhanani. The idea is that even after death they verily attain unmanifestedness. Accordingly has it been said: 'They emerged from invisibility, and have gone back to invisibility. They are not yours, nor are you theirs. What is this fruitless lamentation!' (Mbh. St. 2.13). Ka, what; paridevana, lamentation, or what prattle, can there be; tatra, with regard to them, i.e. with regard to beings which are objects of delusion, which are invisible, (become) visible, (and then) get destroyed!

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
The clarification is not merely by way of describing the nature of birth and death (that is, it is not merely a description of essential nature, but also of the underlying reason).

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Again, he teaches through Sankhya alone -- with 'avyaktadini...' Here the word 'bhutani' should be understood thus -- if 'bhuta' refers to bodies as effects, then the unmanifest Pradhana (primordial nature) itself is their origin, and it is in that same Pradhana that their dissolution also occurs. This states their nature as caused. Their manifestness in the middle is their status as effect; seeing this, one should not grieve, since they are unmanifest at both the beginning and the end. Otherwise, one would have to grieve even for a broken jar. Or, if 'bhuta' refers to the individual selves, then they have become manifest (differentiated) from the unmanifest, imperishable, great Being (the Supreme Self) alone, and their dissolution occurs in that same Being -- their identity with It should be understood in this way. Even seeing the manifestness in the middle, in the form of identification with the body, one should not grieve, since being unmanifest at the beginning and end, unmanifestness alone should be assumed in the middle too -- the manifestness that appears is brought about by Prakriti through My (the Lord's) will; this is the settled conclusion.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Thus, from every standpoint, the freedom of the Self from grief has been established. Now the Lord removes Arjuna's doubt: even though the Self is not to be grieved over, I still grieve for the bodies that are aggregates of the elements. These bodies, made of earth and the other elements, were unmanifest, that is, unperceived, at the very beginning before birth; in the middle, after birth and before death, they are manifest, that is, perceived; and at death they again become unmanifest -- just like lives that merely appear in a dream or an illusion, like mother-of-pearl mistaken for silver, not truly existing either before or after that cognition. Thus, by the maxim 'what does not exist at the beginning and end does not truly exist in the present either,' these too do not truly exist even in the middle. This being so, what lamentation, what sorrowful prattle, can there be over these false and utterly trivial beings -- none whatsoever is proper, this is the meaning. Just as an ordinary person, having seen various kinsmen in a dream, does not grieve upon waking at their separation. This same idea is stated in the Purana: 'It arose out of the unseen, and has gone back into the unseen; it is not yours, nor are you its -- why this vain lamentation?' Hence grieving over bodies is not proper, this is the intent. Or, this verse may also be applied with reference to the great elements such as space and the rest.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Moreover, having considered the nature of the body and the rest, he states that even the birth and death of the Self conditioned by these limiting adjuncts is not a matter for grief -- with 'avyaktadini...' 'Avyakta' means the unmanifest Pradhana (primordial nature), which is the origin, that is, the prior form, of these; such beings, that is, bodies, whose origin is unmanifest, arise while always existing in causal form. And their middle is the manifest state, that is, the interval between birth and death. Their dissolution, that is, their merging, occurs in the unmanifest alone; such is the nature of these beings. So what lamentation, what grief-caused prattle, can there be regarding them? Just as one who has awoken does not grieve over things seen in a dream, so too grief is not proper here -- this is the meaning.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Objection: Even though the Self is eternal, I grieve for the beings that are aggregates of cause and effect -- to this, he says 'avyaktadini...' Those beings that are aggregates of cause and effect, such as sons and friends, whose origin, before coming into being, is 'avyakta,' that is, non-perception, and whose end is likewise avyakta, that is, death -- what lamentation, what grief can there be regarding them? No one grieves over the illusion of silver mistaken in mother-of-pearl -- this is the intent. It is likewise said: 'It arose out of the unseen, and has gone back into the unseen; it is not yours, nor are you its -- why this vain lamentation?' By addressing him as 'Bharata,' it is hinted that just as Bharata and others arose from the unmanifest and merged back into the unmanifest, so it is here too.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Thus, freedom from grief was stated on the ground of inevitability. Now freedom from grief is stated on a further ground: since each thing's nature is fixed, and since one cannot determine which of the prior and subsequent states is pleasurable or painful, and since both alike pass into a state beyond perception -- this is said with 'avyaktadini...' Here the word 'bhuta' is used in the sense of 'body,' as shown by 'manushyadi' (human beings, etc.). To dispel the confusion of taking words like 'avyakta' and 'vyakta' as referring to some particular state of Prakriti, and to show that the meaning of 'sat,' 'Brahman,' and the like as proposed by Yadavaprakasha has no application here, 'anupalabdhi' and the rest are stated. 'It came here from the unseen, and has gone back to the unseen; it is not yours, nor are you its -- why do you grieve in vain?' -- this too is said elsewhere in the Mahabharata. 'They abide in their own natures' means: the manifest state of humanity and so on is not the inherent nature of these substances, since it is brought about by a particular combination; nor is the unmanifest prior-and-subsequent state their nature, since that too is brought about by separation. Hence, in general, being subject to change alone is their nature. Therefore, just as the unmanifest prior state and manifest middle state of a changeable substance are no cause for grief, neither is the subsequent state. Here the word 'eva' conveys either inevitability or the attainment of one's own nature. If you think the unmanifest state alone is the (true) nature, then the state of humanity, being an abandonment of that nature, ought to be the one grieved over; and if you think the state of humanity is the (true) nature and the unmanifest state is due to some obstruction, then, since the obstruction cannot be averted, on its removal humanity may be attained once more -- hence, from no standpoint whatsoever is grief proper.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Objection: It is improper to destroy oneself the bodies created by the Lord -- to this, even considering the origination, continuance, and dissolution of bodies, he states the absence of grief with 'avyaktadini...' 'Avyakta' means the imperishable (Akshara, the indestructible Prakriti), which is the origin of these beings, that is, bodies. 'Vyakta' means the visible world, which is their middle, that is, the state between origination and dissolution. In the unmanifest, that is, in the Akshara alone, occurs their dissolution, that is, their merging; such are these beings. So what lamentation, what concern can there be regarding them? The sense here is: it is improper to grieve even for oneself, since destruction occurs right where origination occurs. Nor is there any possibility of hell and the like for oneself after being slain, since one's own destruction too will occur at the very place of one's origination.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Even granting the Self is not to be grieved over, still grief arises from the destruction of a beloved body -- to this objection, he establishes the falsity of the body and other caused things -- with 'avyaktadini...' 'Bhuta' means space and the rest, as well as their modifications such as living beings born from a womb. Their origin is the unmanifest ignorance itself, not manifest. Their middle -- the manifest, clear state -- extends from origination up to just before death. Their dissolution occurs in the unmanifest alone. The sense is: the ignorance that is the cause of mistaking a rope for a snake is not manifest either as the rope or as the snake; even on close examination it does not come within the range of sight. Hence it is unmanifest. The snake that arises from it dissolves back into that same ignorance, not into the rope. Likewise, the beings imagined upon the Self have an unmanifest beginning and end alike. Therefore, by the maxim 'what does not exist at the beginning and end does not truly exist in the present either,' even though they appear in the middle, they are as unreal as the rope-snake. In such a case, what lamentation can there be over that matter? Just as no knower of truth laments over the disappearance of a mirage's lake.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
All created beings are unmanifest in their beginning, manifest in their interim state, and unmanifest again when annihilated. So what need is there for lamentation?

 Accepting that there are two classes of philosophers, one believing in the existence of the soul and the other not believing in the existence of the soul, there is no cause for lamentation in either case. Nonbelievers in the existence of the soul are called atheists by followers of Vedic wisdom. Yet even if, for argument’s sake, we accept this atheistic theory, there is still no cause for lamentation. Apart from the separate existence of the soul, the material elements remain unmanifested before creation. From this subtle state of nonmanifestation comes manifestation, just as from ether, air is generated; from air, fire is generated; from fire, water is generated; and from water, earth becomes manifested. From the earth, many varieties of manifestations take place. Take, for example, a big skyscraper manifested from the earth. When it is dismantled, the manifestation becomes again unmanifested and remains as atoms in the ultimate stage. The law of conservation of energy remains, but in course of time things are manifested and unmanifested – that is the difference. Then what cause is there for lamentation either in the stage of manifestation or in unmanifestation? Somehow or other, even in the unmanifested stage, things are not lost. Both at the beginning and at the end, all elements remain unmanifested, and only in the middle are they manifested, and this does not make any real material difference. And if we accept the Vedic conclusion as stated in the Bhagavad-gītā that these material bodies are perishable in due course of time ( antavanta ime dehāḥ ) but that the soul is eternal ( nityasyoktāḥ śarīriṇaḥ ), then we must remember always that the body is like a dress; therefore why lament the changing of a dress? The material body has no factual existence in relation to the eternal soul. It is something like a dream. In a dream we may think of flying in the sky, or sitting on a chariot as a king, but when we wake up we can see that we are neither in the sky nor seated on the chariot. The Vedic wisdom encourages self-realization on the basis of the nonexistence of the material body. Therefore, in either case, whether one believes in the existence of the soul or one does not believe in the existence of the soul, there is no cause for lamentation for loss of the body.
