Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
In three verses, Lord Krishna exhaustively describes the relationship that a man of Wisdom maintains with the objects and individuals of the world. A man's culture can be a false mask. As long as sufficiently tempting circumstances do not present themselves before us, many of us can behave like gods. As long as power does not come into a man's hand, he may not be cruel; as long as he is poor, he may lead a quiet life; and in the absence of temptations, he may be above corruption. Thus, many virtues with which we consider many people to be endowed are merely artificial cosmetics. Their real base nature remains hidden behind that mask. Potential evil men move about in the world wearing borrowed robes of artificial virtue. Therefore, the true test or identification of a wise man is not in forests or mountain caves, but in the marketplace, where he is tested by the wickedness of the world. Jesus Christ was never as great as he was at the moment of his crucifixion; it is when crushed by the world that our true nature is revealed. Sandalwood yields its fragrance only when rubbed. The fingers that crush the tulsi leaf are left scented with its fragrance.

The wise man, seated like one unconcerned, is not moved by the gunas. In all the good, bad, and indifferent experiences of the world, he remains like an indifferent spectator, because he knows that all this is merely a play of the mind. We are not agitated by the happy or tragic endings of a film shown in a cinema theatre, because we know that this play of shadows is presented for our entertainment. This does not mean that the wise man has no connection whatsoever with world events. Vyasa chooses his words very carefully. He says that the wise man appears as if he were indifferent — 'udasinavad asinah'. This implies that he does not become perturbed or agitated by events occurring in his life and in the outer world. He knows full well that these continuous changes taking place in his inner equipment belong only to the gunas, and furthermore, the experience of the outer world also changes according to the state of mind. The man of right vision, knowing the process of changes taking place in his internal and external worlds, remains unshaken by them. To observe this play of gunas, one has to remain as a witness. Abiding in his own Self, he enjoys watching the inner and outer play of the gunas. A person watching a street brawl from a balcony above is not affected by that fight. Similarly, the wise man cannot be disturbed from his state of equanimity by the gunas. Clarifying the previous verse further, He says:

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
Having answered the first question starting with 'By what marks...', Anandagiri now addresses the second question regarding the conduct of a transcendent person ('How does he act?'). He explains the illustration given in the commentary ('as an indifferent man...'), noting that an indifferent observer cannot take sides. He then presents the application: the Knower of the Self, established in the knowledge of the immutable nature of the Self, becomes free from the sense of agency and makes no egoistic effort. Knowledge alone is the path to transcending the gunas. To dispel the doubt that sense-objects like sound might cause a deviation from this immutable Self-knowledge, Shankara uses the word 'gunaih' (by the gunas). Anandagiri elaborates on how objects of the senses act as motivators through attraction and aversion. The clause 'he who remains firm' connects with the subsequent description of the transcendent person. Addressing the grammatical query of why the Parasmaipada 'avatisthati' is used instead of the Atmanepada 'avatisthate', he attributes it to preserving the poetic metre. In an alternative reading, action is described as merely the continuance of sublated traits. Realizing that the gunas transformed into senses interact only with gunas transformed into objects and not with the Self, the wise man firmly retains his immutable vision of the Self and does not move.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
He who is seated like one unconcerned, who cannot be distracted by the gunas, and who, with the conviction that 'the gunas alone operate among the gunas', remains established in his own essential Self and makes no personal effort whatsoever.

Commentary:
Udasinavad asinah — If two individuals are in a dispute, one who takes a side is called biased, and one who adjudicates between them is a mediator. But one who merely observes both of them without taking sides or saying anything to either is called neutral (udasa). Similarly, when looking at both the world and the Supreme Lord, a person who has transcended the modes appears like a neutral observer. In reality, the world has no independent existence; it appears to exist only due to the existence of the Supreme Reality. Therefore, when in the vision of a transcendent person the world has no real existence and only the Supreme Lord exists, from whom should he be indifferent? Yet, in the eyes of those who perceive the independent existence of both the world and God, such a person appears neutral.

Gunair yo na vicalyate — Modifications of the modes of Sattva, Rajas, and Tamas do arise in his mind, but he is not disturbed by them. Just as the mental modifications occurring in others' minds make no difference to oneself, similarly, modifications occurring in his mind make no difference to him; he cannot be shaken by them. This is because in his mind, the non-existence of the entire world (including the mind itself) and the presence of the Supreme Truth are perpetually and naturally realized.

Guna vartanta ity eva yo 'vatisthati — Realizing that 'modes alone act upon modes' (Gita 3.28), i.e., all actions take place strictly within the domain of the modes, he abides immutable in his own essential Self.

Na ingate — Earlier, the phrase 'guna vartanta ity eva' negated his connection with the modes; now, the phrase 'na ingate' indicates the absence of action in him. The implied meaning is that the transcendent person makes no personal effort, for in the imperishable, pure Self, no activity ever takes place.

[In verses 22 and 23, the Lord describes the detachment and neutrality of a transcendent soul.]
Context — In verse 21, Arjuna asked about the conduct of a person who has transcended the modes. The Lord answers this second question in the following two verses.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
'Seated like one unconcerned...': Because he is satisfied with the vision of the Self as distinct from the gunas, he sits like one indifferent towards all non-Self objects. He is not moved by the gunas through attraction or aversion; rather, realizing that the gunas operate in their respective effects such as illumination, he remains steady and does not act in alignment with the gunas.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Having thus stated the characteristics, he now answers the second question, 'How does the transcendent person act?', in three verses. Just as an indifferent person observing two disputants does not side with either, feeling neither attachment nor aversion, similarly this Knower of the Self, being free from attachment and aversion, rests firmly in his own essential nature. He is not moved or deviated from his abiding state in the Self by the gunas transformed into pleasure, pain, etc. Instead, he firmly holds: 'These gunas alone, transformed into body, senses, and objects, interact mutually among themselves; I am the illuminator of all this, unattached to any attribute of the illuminated, free from possessiveness. This illuminated world is inert and illusion-like, comparable to a dream, whereas I am self-luminous, absolute Truth, immutable, and non-dual.' Deciding thus, he abides in his true Self. (If the reading is 'yo nu tisthati', 'nu' should be understood separately). 'Na ingate' means he does not engage in any activity anywhere. This connects with the third verse: 'he is said to have transcended the gunas'.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
Having thus stated the internal characteristics known to oneself, Sri Sridhara Swami now answers the second question, 'How does he act?', to describe the external characteristics perceived by others, in three verses beginning with 'udasinavat'. Seated like a neutral witness, he is not distracted or deviated from his essential Self by the gunas or their effects such as pleasure and pain. On the contrary, with discriminative knowledge realizing that 'the gunas alone operate in their own effects and I have no connection whatsoever with them', he abides silently. (The Parasmaipada 'avatisthati' is an archaic usage). 'Na ingate' means he does not move.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
Having resolved the first question ('By what marks...'), Dhanapati now answers the question 'How does he act?' in three verses. Just as an indifferent person takes no one's side, similarly the Knower of the Self, freed from egoism of agency through knowledge of the immutable Self, remains seated in Truth-knowledge which is the path to transcending the gunas. He is not moved or distracted from the state of discriminative Self-knowledge by the gunas. Clarifying this, he states: 'The gunas, transformed into body, organs, and objects, interact among themselves and not in Me' — having firmly determined this, he who remains in immutable Knowledge does not move ('na ingate'), meaning he remains established solely in his own Self. The use of Parasmaipada 'avatisthati' instead of Atmanepada 'avatisthate' is to avoid a break in the Anustubh metre (where the fifth syllable must be short). An alternative reading is 'anutisthati'.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Vedantadeshikacharya answers the question, 'How then does a embodied person remain unagitated when encountering favorable or unfavorable circumstances?': He says 'udasinavat...' (like one unconcerned). Indifference towards everything other than the Self is the cause for the cessation of hatred and craving towards the effects of the gunas. 'Na vicalyate' (is not moved) means he does not engage in external objects through actions. To clarify unshakeability, the mode of movement is stated as 'through hatred and longing'. Showing that 'guna vartante' has the same meaning as 'gunah gunesu vartante' (Gita 3.28), he states the cause of unshakeability: 'the gunas act in their own effects'. The word 'iti' denotes the mode of reflection ('reflecting that...'). The word 'eva' implies 'remaining silent'. Parasmaipada is an archaic usage to avoid metric flaw, meaning 'remains silent'. The underlying feeling is: 'These gunas are engaged in their own actions, what have I to do with them?' This explains indifference. 'Na ingate' clarifies 'na vicalyate', meaning 'he does not act in accordance with the effects of the gunas', i.e., he does not act under the influence of hatred or craving.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
Having thus answered the query about marks, Purushottamji answers the query about conduct: 'udasinavat...'. Seated like an unconcerned observer, free from the absence of experiencing pleasure and pain, seeing My work as a witness, he is not moved from his essential Self by worldly gunas while observing My divine acts. Furthermore, realizing that 'the gunas, which are divine in nature, operate in gunas (their own effects) naturally by the Lord's will alone', he remains thus established, and 'na ingate' — does not deviate from his former nature.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
Now, Neelakantha describes the supreme Knower of Brahman (Brahmavid-variyan) who has reached the sixth stage of enlightenment called 'padartha-bhavani': 'udasinavat...'. He remains like an unconcerned person in samadhi, seeing no purpose in worldly waking state (vyutthana), being free from latent desires like 'this is my duty'. He simply remains seated, never moved by the gunas unless awakened by others' effort. Even when awakened by others, observing the gunas and knowing that 'gunas alone operate', he remains motionless; he is not shaken ('na ingate') by pleasant or unpleasant touches caused by the gunas. The illustration is: just as a person eating, due to numbness of the tongue, does not perceive the taste of vegetables, etc., and even when informed by others and briefly perceiving a taste, remains indifferent to it because that perception quickly vanishes, so he does not experience pleasure or pain caused by it. Such should this wise sage be known.

 Arjuna submitted three different questions, and the Lord answers them one after another. In these verses, Kṛṣṇa first indicates that a person transcendentally situated has no envy and does not hanker for anything. When a living entity stays in this material world embodied by the material body, it is to be understood that he is under the control of one of the three modes of material nature. When he is actually out of the body, then he is out of the clutches of the material modes of nature. But as long as he is not out of the material body, he should be neutral. He should engage himself in the devotional service of the Lord so that his identity with the material body will automatically be forgotten. When one is conscious of the material body, he acts only for sense gratification, but when one transfers the consciousness to Kṛṣṇa, sense gratification automatically stops. One does not need this material body, and he does not need to accept the dictations of the material body. The qualities of the material modes in the body will act, but as spirit soul the self is aloof from such activities. How does he become aloof? He does not desire to enjoy the body, nor does he desire to get out of it. Thus transcendentally situated, the devotee becomes automatically free. He need not try to become free from the influence of the modes of material nature. The next question concerns the dealings of a transcendentally situated person. The materially situated person is affected by so-called honor and dishonor offered to the body, but the transcendentally situated person is not affected by such false honor and dishonor. He performs his duty in Kṛṣṇa consciousness and does not mind whether a man honors or dishonors him. He accepts things that are favorable for his duty in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, otherwise he has no necessity of anything material, either a stone or gold. He takes everyone as his dear friend who helps him in his execution of Kṛṣṇa consciousness, and he does not hate his so-called enemy. He is equally disposed and sees everything on an equal level because he knows perfectly well that he has nothing to do with material existence. Social and political issues do not affect him, because he knows the situation of temporary upheavals and disturbances. He does not attempt anything for his own sake. He can attempt anything for Kṛṣṇa, but for his personal self he does not attempt anything. By such behavior one becomes actually transcendentally situated.
