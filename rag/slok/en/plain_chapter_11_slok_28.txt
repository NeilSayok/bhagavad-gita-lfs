Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
In this verse, the simile of rivers rushing towards the ocean, eager to merge with it, is given. From the very source where a river originates, it acquires its distinct personality. The river neither stops at any single point nor hesitates to move forward. A person of shallow intellect might say that every drop of the river is moving towards some specific nearby place. But a realist knows that all rivers flow only towards the ocean, and until they merge with the ocean, they neither can nor will stop anywhere along the path. Upon merging with the ocean, all differences among the various rivers come to an end. Every drop of river water has come from the ocean itself. First, in the form of clouds, it reached the mountain peaks and appeared there as rain. Providing water to the riverbank regions, giving life and nourishment to the fields, those drops, with a swift flow, merge back into their place of origin, from where they had taken this flight of compassion. Similarly, all individual souls (vyashti jiva) have emerged from the collective whole (samashti) to serve their society, nourish culture, and contribute to the beauty of the world. However, none of them can stop in the middle of this pilgrimage. Everyone must quickly move towards their original source. The river loses nothing by reaching the ocean. Although on the way it acquires some special qualities due to which it gets a specific name and form, this form of its is temporary. This is a convenient form assumed by the ocean water to enrich the dry land in abundance. The more we contemplate this verse, the more joy inherent in it we will receive. Commentary: 'yathā nadīnāṁ bahavo'mbuvegāḥ samudramevābhimukhā dravanti' -- Originally, all water belongs to the ocean. The same water, falling on the earth as rain through clouds, takes the form of rivers through streams, channels, etc. Whatever speeds and flows those rivers have, they all naturally rush towards the ocean. This is because the origin of water is the ocean itself. All those water currents, on reaching the ocean, abandon their names and forms (that is, names like Ganga, Yamuna, Saraswati, etc., and the form of flow) and become the ocean itself. Then, those water flows have no separate, independent existence other than the ocean. In reality, they did not have a separate existence earlier either; they only appeared separate due to being in the form of river flows. 'tathā tavāmī naralokavīrā viśanti vaktrāṇyabhivijvalanti' -- Like rivers, individual souls (jivas) rush towards the Supreme Lord with the desire for eternal bliss. However, by mistake, by identifying themselves with the unreal, transient body, they get attached to worldly accumulation and pleasures born of contact, and begin to believe in their separate existence. Among those souls, they alone are the real heroes who, instead of engaging in worldly accumulation and sensory pleasures, are diligently engaged in the path of God-realization, for which this body was attained. These heroes of the human world, such as Bhishma, Drona, etc., who have come to the battlefield, are entering Your luminous (knowledge-incarnate) mouths. Among the people visible in front, those who seek God-realization are extraordinary and very few. Therefore, the remote pronoun 'amī' (those) has been used for them.

Connection: Arjun describes in the next verse the entry of those who have come to the battlefield out of greed for kingdom and praise, and who are engaged in acquiring worldly accumulations and enjoyments, into the Cosmic Form using the illustration of moths.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
To show that the destruction is rooted in their own actions as indicated by the word 'tvaramāṇāḥ' (rushing) in 11.27, that everyone is withdrawn into the same single entity, and that the single entity remains in a common form suitable for the destruction of all, two illustrations are given in these two verses (28 and 29). Since the destruction of the Pāṇḍavas and others is not mentioned, and only 'scorching the world' is mentioned later, the phrase 'naraloka-vīrāḥ' (heroes of the world of men) has been summarized as 'these hosts of kings'. In 'ambuvegāḥ', the word 'flow' is used to imply velocity. To rule out other meanings of the word 'patanga' (like birds), the word 'moths' is used. The word 'abhivijvalanti' (blazing), which also belongs to the previous verse, is read after the fire illustration to show its compatibility with it. Imagining 'samṛddha-vegāḥ' to mean the same as 'tvaramāṇa', it is said 'rushing of their own accord'. Since moths could potentially extinguish a flame with the force of their wings, to exclude that, 'blazing fire' is specified, indicating 'for their own destruction'. The destruction of a river's flow means abandoning its separate identity as a flow, whereby the designation of 'river flow' ceases in that very substance; whereas the moths turn to ash, acquiring a different designation. Two illustrations are given to show this difference in the manner of destruction. Alternatively, the river flow illustration implies that it is impossible to turn back by one's own will, while the moth illustration highlights that such destruction is brought about by an activity initiated by one's own will. The ocean is compared to show that the Lord does not become full or change even when all enter Him, while the fire is compared to show sudden destruction.
