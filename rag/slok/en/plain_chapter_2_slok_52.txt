Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Here Arjuna is given the assurance that dispassion follows the cessation of delusion. The word 'shrotavya' (what is to be heard) refers to all sense-enjoyments not yet directly experienced, while 'shruta' (what has been heard) refers to all enjoyments already experienced. It is natural that once the intellect becomes pure, no attachment to sense-enjoyment remains.

Although the Self is by nature divine and conscious, it appears entangled in a covering of delusion (moha), caused by an inexplicable power called Maya. Like unseen electricity, Maya is not directly perceptible, but its existence is proved by its manifestations. Studying Maya's working in every being, the teachers of Vedanta found it operates in two ways at two levels of personality: as the 'covering power' (avarana shakti), which veils the intellect with ignorance of one's true nature, and as the 'projecting power' (vikshepa shakti), which works on the mind, making it regard the not-Self world as real and identify itself with the body.

This verse states that by continuing to act in the spirit of karma yoga, the intellect is purified, making it possible to remove this veil and directly realize one's true nature. The result of the cessation of delusion is dispassion toward sense-enjoyment; but in the state of self-ignorance, a person toils day and night hoping to find happiness only in sense objects.

Just as in winter a person sits by a fire while the sun is hidden by clouds, but leaves the fire for sunshine once the clouds clear and its warmth is felt, so too does a person's restless pursuit of sense objects — born of ignorance of his own blissful nature — cease spontaneously once he recognizes his true Self.

Here the entire world is denoted by the two words 'shruta' and 'shrotavya', covering all objects known through the senses. The karma yogi's intellect neither dwells on previously experienced pleasures nor hopes for future ones.

Shankaracharya connects this with the next verse: if you ask when, having pierced the veil of delusion and gained discrimination-born Self-knowledge, you will obtain the fruit of karma yoga — union with the Supreme — the answer follows.

Commentary by Sri Madhavacharya:
This addresses the question: for how long must a seeker of liberation necessarily continue performing prescribed actions? The answer is given here. The word 'nirveda' in this verse means full and abundant attainment, not dispassion or disgust — as shown by its use in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad ('tasmad brahmanah pandityam nirvidya...'), where dispassion toward scholarship as such would not be fitting. Indeed, knowers of the Lord do not become disinterested in hearing about His glory; rather, as the Bhagavata states, even self-content sages free from all bonds render causeless devotion to Hari of such glorious qualities — as Shuka and other great devotees did. Nor is the fruit of such devotion mere happiness, since that happiness is itself the greatest bliss — the bliss that embodied beings attain through meditating on the Lord's lotus feet or hearing accounts of His deeds, a bliss so complete that even attainment of oneness with Brahman is not desired in its place. Scriptural statements also establish a gradation among the liberated: some devotees do not even value the Lord's grace so long as it is not the culmination of devotion, some do not even desire oneness with Him, and some, though offered liberation, decline it — proving that liberation itself has degrees, and that devotees surpass others in bliss even after liberation, through devotion and knowledge focused on the Lord, though all describe their state as 'equal' only in the sense of freedom from suffering, not in the exact measure of bliss. Therefore, dispassion regarding scripture (shruta) and its hearing is not what is meant here; rather, one should attain the very fruit of what has been heard and is to be heard — i.e. full attainment — since there is no compelling reason to restrict the term's meaning elsewhere, while great authorities do restrict it only to exclude unworthy or non-authoritative scriptures.

Commentary by Sri Anandgiri:
This continues the explanation of when the intellect aimed at the vision of ultimate truth is attained through performance of duty. When a person practises actions with such vision as the goal — rather than for some limited, separate result — a kind of miserliness would arise from acting merely for limited fruits; but one who undertakes action with the vision of ultimate reality as its object attains, through the purification of the instruments of knowledge that such action brings, the direct realization of ultimate truth even while still embodied, thereby transcending all agency and its residues and reaching liberation. Hence the mind should be fixed on that state called yoga, meaning skill in performing actions with the vision of ultimate truth as their goal; and if action is undertaken with that vision as its aim, then through the purity of intellect produced by such action, one becomes a knower, renounces the fruit born of action, and — free from bondage — shares in liberation. The verse now reveals, in response to an implied question, the time at which such intellect is attained. Because attachment to results might hinder its rise, the verse specifies 'when' — indicating the maturing of discriminative insight through the word 'time'. It clarifies that this impurity (kalila) is essentially a fault belonging to you (the seeker) that must be shed. It shows that the fruit of purified intellect is discrimination, and the fruit of discrimination is dispassion. The words 'shrotavya' and 'shruta' here denote all scripture other than that concerned with Self-knowledge; once the aforesaid discrimination is attained, everything relating to the not-Self is seen to be fruitless.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
The moment your intellect crosses over the quagmire of delusion, that very moment you will become dispassionate toward the pleasures you have already experienced and those yet to come.

'Moha' is the sense of 'I' and 'mine' attached to the body and to relations, people and possessions connected with the body — a false identification, since neither the body nor these relations truly belong to the Self. Becoming elated when favourable things, people or events occur, and becoming agitated when unfavourable ones occur — along with the resulting partiality, envy and discord in the world and in families — this entire tangle is the 'kalila', the quagmire. When the intellect gets stuck in this quagmire of delusion, a person becomes utterly bewildered about what to do.

Though itself pure consciousness, the self falsely identifies with the inert body and related things, assuming a permanent bond with them — even though none of these things can remain with it forever, nor it with them. Blinded by delusion, a person fails to see this and instead keeps forging new attachments, sinking ever deeper into worldly entanglement — like a traveller who, before reaching his destination, pitches camp along the way and wastes his time in play and amusement.

There are two means of crossing this quagmire of delusion: discrimination (viveka) and service (seva) to others. Sharp discrimination creates distaste for unreal, worldly objects. Alternatively, if the mind becomes absorbed in the intention of serving and bringing happiness to others, the desire for one's own comfort and pleasure automatically diminishes — the stronger the wish to give happiness to others, the weaker becomes the craving for one's own happiness. This is why the Lord has called karma yoga superior to, easier than, and quicker in bestowing perfection than the path of mere philosophical discrimination (Sankhya yoga) (verses 5.2, 5.3, 5.6).

All the pleasures one has already heard of, enjoyed and thoroughly experienced are covered by the word 'shruta' (what has been heard); all the pleasures of higher worlds such as heaven or the world of Brahma, which can only be heard of but not yet experienced, are covered by the word 'shrotavya' (what is yet to be heard). When your intellect crosses this quagmire of delusion, you will become dispassionate toward both these worldly and other-worldly pleasures. This is because, once delusion is crossed, sharp discrimination awakens in the intellect — the realization that the world is changing every moment while 'I' remain the same, so how can this ever-changing world give me peace, or remove my sense of lack? Then dispassion toward everything covered by 'shruta' and 'shrotavya' arises spontaneously.

One might expect the Lord to have said 'enjoyed' instead of 'heard' and 'to be enjoyed' instead of 'to be heard' — but He deliberately used 'hearing', because both the direct and indirect attraction toward worldly objects arises chiefly through hearing about them; hence hearing is emphasized. Even in the paths of knowledge and devotion, which lead one out of worldly bondage, 'shravana' (hearing) is given primary importance — because hearing is the principal factor both in becoming attached to the world and in turning toward God.

The words 'when' and 'then' indicate that there is no fixed rule that dispassion toward these pleasures will occur only after so many years, months or days — rather, the very instant the intellect crosses the quagmire of delusion, that very instant dispassion toward all such pleasures, heard and yet to be heard, arises. There is no delay involved in this at all.

Commentary by Sri Jayatritha:
This verse, though appearing to say nothing directly about yoga, is connected to the preceding discussion by explaining how long actions performed without desire for results, as forms of worship of the Lord, must necessarily continue for a seeker of liberation. Though yoga is taught as a means to knowledge, and a means must ordinarily be practised until the result is achieved, in fact yoga itself is not the direct cause of knowledge — rather it is hearing of scripture (shravana) and the like that directly produces valid cognition, since a means and its result share sequential causal relation, while yoga only assists by first producing purity of mind through unseen merit (adrshta), which purity in turn assists the process of hearing.

The word 'nirveda', ordinarily understood as 'dispassion', is here shown to more properly carry the sense of 'complete attainment/gain', on the strength of its usage elsewhere (as in the Upanishadic passage about a Brahmana attaining nirveda through scholarship) — since dispassion toward scholarship itself would make no sense there. Similarly here: those who truly know the Lord's glory do not become disinterested in hearing more of it — self-content sages free from bonds still render causeless devotion to Hari, as Shuka and others did, and their devotion's fruit is not merely finite happiness, since that itself is described as very great bliss, greater even than the bliss of dissolving into Brahman. This is corroborated by devotees who, even offered various forms of liberation, decline to seek the mere cessation of embodied existence, or do not even desire oneness with the Lord, or refuse liberation itself when offered — showing that among the liberated there exists a real gradation of bliss depending on devotion and knowledge, even though all such states are described as 'equal' only insofar as they equally lack suffering, not insofar as bliss is identical in degree. Various objections regarding scripture's authority, the purpose of continued practice after knowledge, and the consistency of such gradation with texts describing liberation as uniform and supreme are raised and answered, concluding that scripture does not intend disgust toward hearing sacred teaching in general, but only toward inferior or unauthoritative teachings, and that in ordinary usage 'nirveda' more naturally denotes thorough attainment than aversion.

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
In response to the question 'When shall I attain that state (of yoga)?', it is said: when your intellect becomes steady, free from sorrow, endowed with firmness, and thus wholly resolute, you will attain dispassion toward the fruits of action born of the three gunas, whether already heard of or yet to be heard — that is, you will no longer seek to inquire into them, regarding them as unworthy of pursuit. When such a steady intellect becomes fixed in deep absorption (samadhi), you will attain yoga itself, and thereby the fulfilment of your purpose.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
This answers the question: since I am performing actions in this way, when will purity of mind (sattva-shuddhi) come to me? There is no fixed span of time governing this; rather, when your intellect crosses beyond the dense confusion of delusion — the deeply rooted ignorance manifesting as notions like 'I' and 'mine' — that is, when it sheds the impurities of rajas and tamas and attains purity, then you will attain indifference (vitrishnya) toward the fruit of action, both what is to be heard and what has already been heard. This accords with the scriptural statement: 'Having examined the worlds gained through action, the Brahmana attains dispassion.' The implication is that you will recognize your inner purification precisely through this fruit of dispassion.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:
In response to the question 'When shall I attain that state?', it is explained: 'moha' is the false identification of the Self with the body and such, and this very delusion is called 'kalila', meaning a dense thicket, as confirmed by lexicons. Thus the meaning is: when, through worship of the Supreme Lord performed as described, your intellect — by His grace — thoroughly crosses this dense thicket of delusion born of identification with the body, then you will attain dispassion toward what is to be heard and what has been heard — meaning you will no longer feel the need to inquire into them, regarding them as unworthy of pursuit.

Commentary by Sri Dhanpati:
This answers the question of how the practice of prescribed action, taught by the Lord, leads one to the discriminative (Sankhya) intellect. First explanation: when your intellect crosses beyond the impurity of delusion, born of confusion caused by hearing of many different means and ends, you will attain dispassion toward what is yet to be heard and what has already been heard. Having thus become free from distraction regarding hearing, when the mind becomes steady and unwavering, fixed in that state of concentration (samadhi), you attain yoga — here meaning the Sankhya path of discrimination. Second explanation: the intellect endowed with discrimination between the eternal and the non-eternal, born of the purity produced by the practice of yoga along with dispassion and other aids and qualifying one for knowledge — this arises when your intellect crosses the impurity born of confusion; then you attain dispassion toward the fruit of action, both to be heard and already heard. Having thereby gained, through purity of mind, the discrimination-born intellect, the question of when the fruit of karma yoga — union with the Supreme — will be attained is then addressed in what follows; both explanations are supported by the commentarial tradition.

Commentary by Sri Purushottamji:
In answer to the question of when that attainment will occur, it is said: when your intellect thoroughly crosses beyond the dense delusion regarding worldly things such as the body, you will attain nirveda, that is, liberation. At that very moment you will attain dispassion regarding both what is yet to be heard — whether taught later or found in scripture — and what has already been heard. Alternatively, 'shruta' may refer to the previously taught path of Sankhya; when your intellect crosses that delusion, you attain dispassion regarding 'shrotavya', the path of devotion yet to be heard. The implication is that as long as delusion regarding paths such as karma persists, the fruit of the path of devotion does not manifest, since devotion can be attained only exclusively, through itself alone; hence it will be taught later in that manner. It is not taught now because the necessary qualification is still lacking, but it is hinted at here so that the seeker may attain that qualification.

Commentary by Sri Neelkanth:
This answers the question of when people become truly wise. Your 'moha' — the mental agitation caused by the pain of union with the unwanted and separation from the wanted — is itself the 'kalila', the impurity affecting the intellect. When your intellect crosses beyond this impurity, that is, becomes clear and untroubled, then you will attain dispassion regarding the portion of scripture yet to be heard and already heard. The point is this: when the intellect is impure, even scriptural meaning grasped again and again fails to become clear, so both what is to be heard and what has been heard remain fruitless; and likewise, when the intellect is pure, scriptural meaning becomes instantly clear, making both superfluous, since the goal is already attained — in either case, dispassion toward them is fitting here. A pure intellect alone is fit to be mastered in meditation; hence, having set aside hearing and the like, one should remain established in meditation alone.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
When your intelligence has passed out of the dense forest of delusion, you shall become indifferent to all that has been heard and all that is to be heard.

 There are many good examples in the lives of the great devotees of the Lord of those who became indifferent to the rituals of the Vedas simply by devotional service to the Lord. When a person factually understands Kṛṣṇa and his relationship with Kṛṣṇa, he naturally becomes completely indifferent to the rituals of fruitive activities, even though an experienced brāhmaṇa. Śrī Mādhavendra Purī, a great devotee and ācārya in the line of the devotees, says: sandhyā-vandana bhadram astu bhavato bhoḥ snāna tubhyaṁ namo bho devāḥ pitaraś ca tarpaṇa-vidhau nāhaṁ kṣamaḥ kṣamyatām yatra kvāpi niṣadya yādava-kulottaṁsasya kaṁsa-dviṣaḥ smāraṁ smāram aghaṁ harāmi tad alaṁ manye kim anyena me “O my prayers three times a day, all glory to you. O bathing, I offer my obeisances unto you. O demigods! O forefathers! Please excuse me for my inability to offer you my respects. Now wherever I sit, I can remember the great descendant of the Yadu dynasty [Kṛṣṇa], the enemy of Kaṁsa, and thereby I can free myself from all sinful bondage. I think this is sufficient for me.” The Vedic rites and rituals are imperative for neophytes: comprehending all kinds of prayer three times a day, taking a bath early in the morning, offering respects to the forefathers, etc. But when one is fully in Kṛṣṇa consciousness and is engaged in His transcendental loving service, one becomes indifferent to all these regulative principles because he has already attained perfection. If one can reach the platform of understanding by service to the Supreme Lord Kṛṣṇa, he has no longer to execute different types of penances and sacrifices as recommended in revealed scriptures. And, similarly, if one has not understood that the purpose of the Vedas is to reach Kṛṣṇa and simply engages in the rituals, etc., then he is uselessly wasting time in such engagements. Persons in Kṛṣṇa consciousness transcend the limit of śabda-brahma, or the range of the Vedas and Upaniṣads.
