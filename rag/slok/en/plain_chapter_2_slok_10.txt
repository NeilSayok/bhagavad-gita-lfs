Commentary by Shri Purohit Swami:

Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Thus, in the midst of the arraying of these two armies of dharma and adharma, of good and evil, Arjuna (the individual soul) fully surrenders himself to the refuge of his charioteer, Lord Sri Krishna (the subtle discriminative intellect), who holds the chariot (the body), driven by five horses (the five sense organs), in complete control.

To such a grieving and bewildered individual soul, Arjuna, Sri Krishna, smiling, along with assuring him of ultimate victory, now imparts the spiritual teaching of the Gita on Self-liberation, the knowledge of which forever removes a person's grief and delusion.

Keeping the Upanishadic allegory in mind, if we describe the scene painted by Sanjaya in the manner above, we shall behold in it a vision of eternal, transcendent truth. When the individual soul (Arjuna), overcome with despondency, sinks down in the body (the chariot), and, abandoning all the instruments of self-interested action (the Gandiva bow), restrains the senses (the white horses) with the reins of the mind, then the charioteer (the pure intellect) grants the soul, with the help of the power of dharma, that divine strength and guidance by which even the powerful army of adharma is vanquished, and the soul attains complete victory.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
O Bharatavamshi Dhritarashtra! To that Arjuna, despondent in the midst of both armies, the Lord Hrishikesha, smiling as it were, spoke these words (about to follow).

Explanation: 'Tam uvacha hrishikeshah... vishidantam idam vachah' — Arjuna, with great valor and enthusiasm, had asked the Lord to place his chariot between the two armies so that he could see the warriors. But now, at that very spot, between the two armies, Arjuna sank into despondency! In truth, he should have stood ready for battle according to the very purpose for which he had come. But abandoning that purpose, Arjuna fell into anxiety and grief. Hence now, right there between the two armies, the Lord begins to instruct the grief-stricken Arjuna.

'Prahasan iva' (smiling, as it were) signifies that, seeing the change in Arjuna's disposition — that the earlier resolve to fight had now turned into despondency — this is...

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Sanjaya said: Seeing Partha, who, out of misplaced affection and compassion arising at an improper occasion, had come to regard the Kshatriya's supreme dharma of battle as adharma, and who, desiring to know true dharma, had surrendered to Him, and understanding that Arjuna's delusion would not be pacified without knowledge of the true nature of the Self — knowledge that battle undertaken without desire for its fruit is itself the means to attaining the true nature of the Self — the Supreme Person, the Blessed Lord, undertook the revelation of the science of the Self. As it is said: 'Seeing Partha, bewildered by affection and compassion misplaced at this occasion, and by confused notions of dharma and adharma, surrendered to Him, the Lord undertook the revelation of scripture' (Gitartha-sangraha 5). Seeing him thus overcome with grief born of true knowledge of the relation between body and Self, yet also speaking of dharma arising from knowledge of the Self as distinct from the body — mingling contradictory qualities — and seeing him, in the midst of both armies arrayed for battle, suddenly inactive, the Supreme Person, as though smiling, spoke thus — as though in jest — beginning with 'Never was there a time when I did not exist' (2.12) and continuing through to 'I shall liberate you from all sins; grieve not' (18.66), covering the subject matter of karma-yoga, jnana-yoga, and bhakti-yoga, the means to attaining the true nature of the Self and of the Supreme Self and union with Him.

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
On 'Karpanya...' and the rest: through the words beginning 'between the two armies' and the rest, it is indicated that Arjuna, overcome with doubt, has not merely ceased fighting through one particular line of reasoning; and it is precisely for this reason that he says 'instruct me, who have taken refuge in You.' Hence he is being taught by the Blessed Lord as one caught between both knowledge and ignorance.

Commentary by Sri Shankaracharya:
Here the entire text from 'Then, seeing the Pandava army arrayed' (1.2) through 'Saying, "I will not fight," to Govinda, he fell silent' (2.9) is to be explained as demonstrating the origin of the faults that are the seed of the cycle of grief, delusion, and worldly bondage for all beings. Thus, Arjuna, through his notions of 'I' and 'mine' regarding his kingdom, teachers, sons, friends, kinsmen, and relations, displayed grief and delusion caused by the severance of such attachment, as shown in 'How shall I fight Bhishma in battle...' (2.4) and the rest. Overcome by grief and delusion, his discriminative wisdom obscured, though naturally engaged in the Kshatriya's dharma of battle, he withdrew from that battle and proceeded to adopt another's dharma, namely a life of begging alms. In just the same way, all beings whose minds are possessed by such faults of grief and delusion will, by their very nature, abandon their own proper duty and take up what is forbidden to them. And even those who remain engaged in their own duty do so with activity of speech, mind, and body that is driven by desire for its fruit and marked by egoism. Given this, through the accumulation of merit and demerit, the cycle of birth, desired and undesired pleasure and pain, and so forth — that is, worldly existence — remains unceasing. Hence grief and delusion are the very seed of worldly bondage. And from these two, freedom comes from nothing other than knowledge of the Self preceded by the renunciation of all action — desiring to teach this, and for the benefit of the whole world, the Blessed Lord Vasudeva, taking Arjuna as the occasion, speaks beginning with 'those not to be grieved for' (2.11) and so on.

Here some say that liberation is never attained merely from steadfastness in Self-knowledge preceded by the renunciation of all action alone; rather, it is the settled conclusion throughout the Gita that liberation comes from knowledge combined with the Vedic rites such as the agnihotra. They cite as evidence such passages as 'if you will not wage this righteous war' (2.33), 'you have a right to action alone' (2.47), and 'therefore you must perform action' (4.15). And lest it be objected that Vedic action, being bound up with violence, could not therefore be dharma, they point to the statement that battle, though involving the extreme violence of slaying one's teachers, brothers, and sons, is nonetheless one's own dharma as a Kshatriya, and hence not adharma, and that its omission would result in the forfeiture of one's own dharma and fame, leading to sin (2.33) — implying, they say, that actions enjoined by scripture, even those involving violence such as animal sacrifice, are established beforehand as not adharma.

This view is untenable, because the Lord Himself distinguishes two separate paths of steadfastness — one grounded in knowledge (the intellect of Sankhya) and one grounded in action (the intellect of Yoga) — belonging to two different classes of aspirants, as He says: 'This understanding has been declared to you in Sankhya; now hear it with regard to Yoga' (2.39). He further declares that He taught the steadfastness of knowledge, belonging to the Sankhyas, 'in ancient times' (3.3), and separately taught the steadfastness of action, belonging to the Yogis, through 'karma-yoga' (3.3). Thus the Lord Himself distinguishes these two paths precisely because He recognizes that the two mutually exclusive attitudes — of being an agent and of being a non-agent — cannot simultaneously belong to a single person. This same distinction is shown in the Shatapatha Brahmana and the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, where, having enjoined the renunciation of all action for those who desire the world of the renunciate, it further declares indifference even toward begetting offspring, since this very Self is one's true world; and it shows that all Vedic rites are prescribed only for one who still desires and is under the sway of ignorance, while withdrawal from these and renunciation is enjoined only for the desireless one who seeks the Self alone as his world. Hence this distinction would be untenable if the Lord truly intended a combination of Vedic ritual action with knowledge.

Nor would Arjuna's later question, 'if you deem knowledge superior to action...' (3.1), make sense on the theory of combination, since it presupposes that the two cannot be jointly practiced by a single person — for Arjuna would never falsely attribute to the Lord a superiority of knowledge over action that the Lord had never Himself stated, unless the Lord had indeed earlier taught them as distinct and mutually exclusive paths. Moreover, if a combination of both were truly intended for everyone, including Arjuna, then Arjuna's later question, 'tell me decisively which one of these two is better for me' (5.1), would make no sense — for one does not ask a physician who has already prescribed both sweet and cooling foods for the pacification of bile, 'tell me instead just one cause for pacifying the bile.'

Even if Arjuna's later question be attributed merely to his failure to grasp the Lord's earlier teaching, the Lord's answer ought properly to address that very confusion directly — 'I taught a combination of knowledge and action; why then are you so bewildered?' — rather than offering an answer unrelated to the question asked, namely, 'two distinct paths were taught by Me of old' (3.3). Nor can it be maintained that the combination intended was of knowledge together with the smriti-based obligatory rites alone, since Arjuna, already fully aware that battle is his own smriti-ordained duty as a Kshatriya, would then find no sense in his objection 'why do you engage me in this dreadful action?' (3.1).

Hence, in the entire scripture of the Gita, no one can rightly show even the slightest combination of Self-knowledge with either Vedic or smriti-based action. Rather, for one who, whether from ignorance or from the taint of passion and the rest, has become engaged in action, and whose being has become purified through sacrifice, charity, or austerity, and in whom knowledge of the ultimate truth — that all this is but one, non-dual Brahman, the non-agent — has thereby arisen, though such a person has, in truth, ceased from action and from any purpose in action, yet, for the sake of guiding the world, continues to act with effort exactly as before; and this outward semblance of activity is not, in reality, action of the sort by which it could be combined with knowledge — just as the Blessed Vasudeva's own Kshatriya conduct is not combined with His knowledge for the attainment of any personal end, so too for the knower, since the absence of desire for the fruit of action and of egoism is equally present in both. The knower of truth thinks, 'I do nothing,' and does not fix his intent upon its fruit. Just as one who has kindled the sacred fires for the sake of obtaining heaven and the like, and who continues the same optional rites even after the desire that motivated them has been partly or wholly extinguished, does not thereby perform a desire-motivated rite at all — so the Lord Himself shows in many places: 'though acting, he is not bound' (5.1[recte 3.28 area]), 'though he acts, he is not bound' (13.31), and so forth.

As for statements such as 'what was practiced of old by our forefathers' (4.15) and 'Janaka and others attained perfection through action alone' (3.20), these must be understood with proper discrimination. If Janaka and others, though already knowers of truth, continued to act, it must be understood either that, out of a desire to guide the world — 'the wise act among the ignorant just as the ignorant do, for the sake of guiding the world' (3.29 area) — they had already attained perfection through knowledge alone, and, even though renunciation of action was now available to them, they nonetheless remained engaged in action alongside that perfection rather than formally renouncing it; or else it must be understood that these forefathers, not yet knowers of truth, attained perfection — whether purity of being or the arising of knowledge — as the fruit of action offered to the Lord as a means. The Lord conveys this same sense elsewhere: 'they perform action for the purification of being' (5.11 area), and, having said 'having thus worshipped Him through his own proper action, a man attains perfection' (18.46), He goes on to describe, for one who has attained such perfection, the subsequent steadfastness in knowledge, beginning 'one who has attained perfection, being thus fit for Brahman...' (18.50).

Hence it is the settled conclusion of the Gita scripture that liberation is attained through knowledge of truth alone, not through knowledge combined with action; and this same conclusion will be shown, section by section, at each relevant point hereafter.

It is precisely for the sake of this teaching that the Blessed Lord Vasudeva, seeing no other means of rescue than Self-knowledge for Arjuna — whose mind is utterly confused about dharma, possessed of false knowledge, and sunk deep in the ocean of grief — and wishing, out of compassion, to lift him up, now, in order to lead him toward Self-knowledge, speaks: The Blessed Lord said—

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
Thus, though Arjuna had turned away even from battle itself, the Blessed Lord did not turn away from him — this is stated to dispel Dhritarashtra's misplaced hope, in 'Senayor ubhayor madhye...': to that Arjuna, who, having come with the resolve for battle, had instead fallen into despondency, into delusion, opposed to that very resolve, in the midst of both armies — Hrishikesha, the indwelling controller of all, smiling, as though plunging him into an ocean of shame by exposing his improper conduct, spoke this deeply profound teaching about to follow, beginning 'those not to be grieved for...,' which itself exposes his improper conduct — but did not turn away from him, is the sense. The exposing of improper conduct so as to produce shame is here called 'smiling'; and shame, being of the nature of suffering, would properly belong to an object of hatred. But since Arjuna is an object of the Lord's grace, and since the exposing of improper conduct is here the cause of the arising of true discrimination rather than of humiliation, this 'smiling' is used only in a secondary sense — hence the word 'iva' (as though). The Lord exposes Arjuna's improper conduct not to produce shame, but as if to produce discrimination; whether shame incidentally results or not is not the primary intent, is the sense.

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Referring back to the point about fitness for humor, he says, in 'Evam...': the word 'ubhayoh' (of both) refers to what was earlier stated, 'both arrayed for battle'; by this, the occasion for imparting instruction is indicated. The word 'sidamanam' (sinking down) conveys his utter inactivity. Through the word 'madhye' (in the midst), indicating a state unfit for withdrawal from battle, he conveys the intended sense in 'akasmat' (suddenly): there is here no proper cause, such as unrighteousness or fear of defeat, for withdrawing from battle; hence, this abandonment, undertaken without any real cause, is itself fit for gentle mockery, is the sense. Here, the epithet 'Hrishikesha' conveys the complete uniqueness of the speaker — the Supreme Person — relevant to the scriptural authority of what is about to be taught. Although, being the ruler of the senses, He could, by mere will, govern all of Partha's senses and impel him toward the removal of the earth's burden, nevertheless, being one who benefits the world through the ordinary means available to mortals, He instead sets him in motion through the teaching of scripture concerning the means to the highest human goal, common to Partha and all others, is the sense. Or, alternatively: having Himself, as controller of the senses, stirred the steadfast Arjuna, He, smiling as it were, spoke this scripture for the world's benefit — hence, by this particular connection and by consideration of the immediately following verse, the object of this smile is properly Partha's own words, is the sense. Or again: since laughter at one who has surrendered, upon observing his fault, would be improper in a teaching addressed to a disciple concerning the Self, and since a mere jest without any illustrative purpose would likewise be inappropriate here, the word 'Partha' is not used, is intended. Hence, 'prahasan iva' (smiling, as it were) conveys the ease, the delightful accessibility, and effortless utterance of that vast body of meaning hidden deep within the entire Vedanta. The word 'idam' (this) refers to the whole of the Lord's teaching about to follow, and its intent is indicated even by suggestion, in 'parihasa...' and so on. Since the verse 'those not to be grieved for' (2.11) too carries a shade of gentle humor for the sake of securing the disciple's attention to the teaching, and is therefore, strictly speaking, merely the introduction to the revelation of scripture rather than the scripture itself, the actual scripture is said to begin from 'never was there a time...' (2.12) onward. Or, alternatively, the verse 'those not to be grieved for' (2.11) is itself the object of 'prahasan iva,' while 'never was there a time...' (2.12) and what follows constitute the referent of the word 'idam.' Here, the teaching extending to 'grieve not for me' (16.5) is designated as concerning bhakti-yoga, since the surrender described in the final verse of the text, applicable to every seeker, will be shown, in its natural connection with the present context, to remove all obstacles to devotion.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
O descendant of Bharata, at that time Krishna, smiling, in the midst of both the armies, spoke the following words to the grief-stricken Arjuna.

 The talk was going on between intimate friends, namely the Hrishikesha and the Gudakesha. As friends, both of them were on the same level, but one of them voluntarily became a student of the other. Krishna was smiling because a friend had chosen to become a disciple. As Lord of all, He is always in the superior position as the master of everyone, and yet the Lord agrees to be a friend, a son or a lover for a devotee who wants Him in such a role. But when He was accepted as the master, He at once assumed the role and talked with the disciple like the master — with gravity, as it is required. It appears that the talk between the master and the disciple was openly exchanged in the presence of both armies so that all were benefited.
