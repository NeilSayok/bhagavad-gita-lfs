Commentary by Swami Chinmayananda:
Here Arjuna describes a few more symptoms of his condition. Earlier he described the symptoms manifesting in his gross body; now he also describes the unbalanced state of his mind. His mind, besides being unsteady, agitated and bewildered, has also lost all courage. He has sunk to the point that, losing all discrimination, he begins to see the many superstition-born ill omens that are believed to signal defeat and destruction in war. The following verses not only describe his mental confusion but also make clear the extent to which his discrimination and moral courage had already been destroyed.

Commentary by Swami Ramsukhdas:
Arjuna said -- O Krishna! Seeing this assembly of kinsmen, eager for battle, present before me, my limbs are giving way and my mouth is drying up; my body is trembling and my hair is standing on end. The Gandiva bow is slipping from my hand, and my skin too is burning. My mind seems to be reeling, and I am unable even to stand.

Explanation: 'Drishtvemam svajanam krishna yuyutsum samupasthitam' -- the name 'Krishna' was very dear to Arjuna. This form of address occurs nine times in the Gita -- no other address for Lord Sri Krishna occurs so often. Likewise, the Lord too held Arjuna's name 'Partha' very dear. So the Lord and Arjuna would use these names with each other in conversation, and this fact was well known among people. It is with this in view that Sanjaya, at the end of the Gita, mentions the names 'Krishna' and 'Partha': 'Where Yogeshvara Krishna is, where Partha the bow-wielder is' (18.78).

Dhritarashtra had earlier said 'samaveta yuyutsavah' (assembled, eager to fight), and here Arjuna too says 'yuyutsum samupasthitam' (eager for battle, present) -- but there is a great difference in their two perspectives. In Dhritarashtra's view, Duryodhana and the rest are 'my sons,' and Yudhishthira and the rest are 'Pandu's sons' -- such a distinction exists; hence Dhritarashtra there said 'mamakah' and 'pandavah.' But in Arjuna's view this distinction does not exist; hence Arjuna here says 'svajanam' (my own people), which includes both sides. The point is: Dhritarashtra fears and grieves only over the possible death of his own sons in the war; but Arjuna grieves over the possible death of kinsmen on both sides -- whoever dies, on whichever side, is still our own kinsman.

The word 'drishtva' (having seen) has occurred three times so far: 'drishtva tu pandavanikam' (1.2), 'vyavasthitan drishtva dhartarashtran' (1.20), and here, 'drishtvemam svajanam' (1.28). The point of these three occurrences is: Duryodhana's seeing remained of one kind throughout -- his was purely a martial sentiment; but Arjuna's seeing was of two kinds. At first, seeing Dhritarashtra's sons, Arjuna, filled with valor, stood up, bow raised, ready for battle; but now, seeing his own kinsmen, he is overcome with faint-heartedness, withdraws from battle, and the bow slips from his hand.

'Sidanti mama gatrani...bhramativa cha me manah' -- anxiety over the war's coming outcome fills Arjuna's mind, causing him great sorrow, and the effect of that anxiety and sorrow spreads through his entire body. Arjuna describes this effect in plain words: each limb of my body -- hands, feet, mouth and the rest -- is giving way! My mouth is drying up, making it hard even to speak! My whole body is trembling! All the hair on my body is standing on end -- my whole body has horripilation! The Gandiva bow, whose very twang strikes fear into enemies, is today slipping from my hand! My skin -- my whole body -- is burning! My mind is reeling -- I cannot even think what I ought to do! I am unable even to stand here on the battlefield, in the chariot! It feels as though I shall faint and collapse! Even standing in such a calamitous war feels like a sin.

Transition: having described these eight symptoms of grief in his body in the previous verse, Arjuna now, in terms of the ominous portents foretelling the outcome, explains why it is improper to fight.

Commentary by Sri Ramanuja:
Arjuna said -- Sanjaya said: The high-souled Partha, supremely compassionate, seeing there, stationed in both armies, his fathers, grandfathers, teachers, maternal uncles, brothers, sons, grandsons, and friends -- his own kinsmen arrayed for battle -- was overcome, and spoke thus to Krishna.

Commentary by Sri Abhinav Gupta:
'Na cha shreyah' etc. up to 'mahikrite': those wrongly conceived as objects to be slain, with the individualizing notion 'these are my teachers and the rest,' would necessarily generate sin thereby. Likewise, the act of slaying, even of those who truly deserve to be slain in battle, if undertaken with the thought 'this battle is being waged for visible ends such as pleasure, happiness and the like' -- that too necessarily generates sin. This notion lies hidden within Arjuna's objection. It is for this very reason that the Lord's reply will be given: 'you must perform actions solely as your own duty, not with the individualizing notion [of who is being slain, or of what is to be gained].'

Commentary by Sri Vallabhacharya:
Beginning with 'sidanti' (my limbs give way) through 'bhramativa cha me manah' (my mind seems to reel): through the perception of external objects, brought about by his sense of identification with the body, Arjuna reports his own state, beginning with 'nimittani' (omens) and the rest.

Commentary by Sri Madhusudan Saraswati:
'I am not able to stand' -- meaning unable to hold up my body -- indicates fainting. The cause is given: 'my mind seems to reel' -- the resemblance to whirling here indicates a particular disturbance of mind that is a precursor state to fainting; both are causes here. Since this is so, I am unable to stand, is the meaning. Further, he states the cause of his inability to stand: 'I see omens' -- as portents signaling imminent misfortune, adverse ones, such as the twitching of the left eye and the like, I perceive, I experience. This too is why I cannot stand. Being unaware of the true Self, and hence suffering, I experience the affliction born of grief -- whereas You, being ever of the nature of bliss, are untouched by grief -- this is conveyed by the word 'Krishna.' Hence, even though the sight of one's own kinsmen is the same for both, by this distinguishing mark of being untouched by grief, the sense is: may You render me too free from grief. The word 'Keshava' conveys the capacity to do so -- its etymology being 'he to whom even Brahma the creator and Rudra the destroyer go for mercy.' Or, the word 'Krishna' may convey His being drawn by the suffering of His devotee.

Commentary by Sri Sridhara Swami:

Commentary by Vedantadeshikacharya Venkatanatha:
Venkatanatha now states the condensed sense of the remainder of the chapter, in 'sa tu...': the word 'tu' (but) signals the contrast between Duryodhana as described earlier and the character of Partha about to be described. His epithet 'high-souled' (mahamana) conveys that, out of unwillingness to slay even enemies who bear the mere designation of kinsmen, he counts victory -- even sovereignty over all three worlds -- as no more than a blade of grass (as he says later, 'I do not desire victory,' 1.31). That he cannot bear even his enemies' suffering is conveyed by 'supremely compassionate' ('overcome by the deepest compassion'). The phrase 'fathers, then grandfathers' -- expanded by the list 'teachers, fathers, sons' (1.34) and the like -- conveys the sheer breadth of those toward whom his affection extends, expressed through this long compound; or, the sense is that even when a kinsman does one grave wrong, one's bond with him does not loosen. (Compare 'all these kinsmen, indeed our own people,' 1.37.) Since even those on the side of the aggressors -- teachers and the rest -- are, on reflection, not to be slain, and since Arjuna foresees the chain of unrighteousness born of the destruction of a family, he is called 'supremely righteous.' His intent is that any sanction to slay an aggressor applies only to persons other than teachers and the like. 'Sabhratrika' (along with his brothers) conveys that this is not Arjuna alone, but all the Pandavas together, feeling this way -- this gathers up all the plural forms used later ('us,' 'we,' 'by us,' etc.). Or, the sense is: it is not merely out of disregard for personal injury that he overlooks their killing him, but even in the face of wrongs done to Dharmaraja, Draupadi and others -- for whom he has the deepest reverence, affection and compassion, as though they stood in the place of his own teachers. Arjuna's intent is that no fault of killing a teacher should fall even upon his brothers. To indicate that they are indeed to be slain, the word 'ghnatah api' ('even while being slain,' 1.35) is used. This is elaborated in 'bhavadbhih...' and the like; the term 'aggressor' (atatayin) is also explained there, with reference to acts such as the burning of the house of lac. As the law-books say: 'The poisoner, the arsonist, the one who attacks with weapon in hand, the plunderer of wealth, the seizer of land, and the abductor of another's wife -- these six are aggressors' (Manu 8.350); and 'one may slay an approaching aggressor without hesitation; no fault whatever attaches to the slayer of an aggressor' (Manu 8.351). The repeated use of 'et cetera' shows that such grounds for being deemed an aggressor had been committed by them many times over, and had not ceased even now -- as indicated by the present-tense phrase 'even while being slain' (Mahabharata 1.1.14). By the word 'bhavadbhih' ('by you all'), Arjuna implicates even Dhritarashtra, who out of fatherly affection consents to and is thus complicit with the wrathful Duryodhana ('I follow the deluded, wrathful Duryodhana,' Mahabharata 1.1.145). Thus it is shown that Duryodhana and the rest, their minds overpowered by excessive greed, stand in stark contrast to what is meant by 'high-souled.' The phrase 'Supreme Person' is used to produce grief at the certainty that Dhritarashtra's people, including allies like Shakuni and Karna, are doomed to be slain regardless. The compound may mean 'one whose ally is the Supreme Person,' or 'one who is merely the instrument, the Supreme Person being the true agent' -- as the Lord will later say, 'these have already been slain by Me; be merely the instrument, O ambidextrous one' (11.33). And Arjuna, even before this, had determined that the mere presence of the unarmed Supreme Person was the true cause of victory, surpassing even the presence of a thousand mighty armies, and had chosen Him for that very reason. To indicate that this is not the proper occasion for such affection, the phrase 'seeing your own people' is used. The passage beginning 'out of affection for kinsmen' and the rest shows that Arjuna's dejection was unlike Duryodhana's -- his was not born of hatred toward kin, cruelty, or fear of the enemy, nor from any weakness in one who has the Supreme Person as his ally. The sense of the passage from 'the limbs sink' (1.30) through 'the mind' (1.30) is condensed with the word 'excessively,' etc. 'Sakhin' means age-mates; 'suhridah' means well-wishers irrespective of age. 'Senayorubhayorapi' conveys that nearly all of these relations were found, for the most part, within a single army -- i.e., looking at them thoughtfully, as befits scriptural and worldly propriety. 'Sarvan bandhun' conveys that not a single kinsman remained absent from that gathering.

Commentary by A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada:
I am now unable to stand here any longer. I am forgetting myself, and my mind is reeling. I see only causes of misfortune, O Kṛṣṇa, killer of the Keśī demon.

 Due to his impatience, Arjuna was unable to stay on the battlefield, and he was forgetting himself on account of this weakness of his mind. Excessive attachment for material things puts a man in such a bewildering condition of existence. Bhayaṁ dvitīyābhiniveśataḥ syāt ( Bhāg. 11.2.37): such fearfulness and loss of mental equilibrium take place in persons who are too affected by material conditions. Arjuna envisioned only painful reverses in the battlefield – he would not be happy even by gaining victory over the foe. The words nimittāni viparītāni are significant. When a man sees only frustration in his expectations, he thinks, “Why am I here?” Everyone is interested in himself and his own welfare. No one is interested in the Supreme Self. Arjuna is showing ignorance of his real self-interest by Kṛṣṇa’s will. One’s real self-interest lies in Viṣṇu, or Kṛṣṇa. The conditioned soul forgets this, and therefore suffers material pains. Arjuna thought that his victory in the battle would only be a cause of lamentation for him.
